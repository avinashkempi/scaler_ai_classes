Recommender Systems · Part Two

From Baskets to Brains: DailyMart Builds a Recommendation Engine

Matrix Factorization survives its toughest audience yet — a room full of skeptical leadership, six hard questions, and one whiteboard.

DailyMart, Nisha, and everyone in this story are fictional — a teaching device. The machine learning techniques are real, and are the same ones used by real recommendation engines at real companies.

Quick recap of where we left off

Nisha joined DailyMart and was asked, vaguely, to "use data to fix" a sales plateau. She turned that into two concrete wins:

  1. Market Basket Analysis — using the Apriori algorithm and metrics like Support, Confidence, and Lift, she found which products get bought together, and turned that into shelf-placement and bundling ideas.
  2. Recommendation Systems — she reframed "make the app personal" as filling in a giant, sparse user-item matrix, and sketched out two building blocks: Collaborative Filtering and Content-Based Filtering (to handle the "cold start" problem for brand-new users and products).

She closed her first pitch with a promise: a technique called Matrix Factorization could tie both ideas together and scale to millions of users — the same way it connects to PCA/SVD, which the team already knew.

Leadership didn't say yes right away. They said: "Convince us it's worth building." This is where we pick up.

The follow-up meeting

Two weeks after Nisha's first pitch, she's back in the same glass-walled conference room. Her manager, Arvind, has read her proposal, and so has Meera, the VP of Product. They're not rejecting it — they're stress-testing it, the way good leadership should.

Arvind starts listing questions on the whiteboard. Meera adds a sixth, almost as an afterthought. Nisha writes all six down — it's actually a good roadmap. She'll answer them one at a time, over the next hour. We'll follow along.

Question 01

Is this genuinely new? — MF and clustering

Nisha answers with something the marketing team already does: customer segmentation using K-Means clustering. Her claim — K-Means is secretly a special case of Matrix Factorization.

The analogy — a seating chart

Imagine every customer is a guest at a wedding, with k tables (clusters). A seating chart is a grid: one row per guest, one column per table, with a tick mark showing which table that guest sits at. That's matrix Z. Separately, a small table describes each table's characteristics — its centroid — matrix C. K-Means finds a Z and C such that reconstructing each guest from their table's description lands as close as possible to reality: X ≈ Z⋅Cᵀ. That's the exact shape of Matrix Factorization — with one extra rule: Z can only contain 0s and 1s, one per row.

The seating chart, hard vs. soft

Each guest sits at exactly one table — one 1 per row, everything else 0.

In real life, a customer might be 70% "bargain hunter" and 30% "premium loyalist." That's not K-Means anymore — it's a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), also a special case of MF. The only change: rows of Z now contain fractions that add to 1, like a guest splitting their evening between two tables.

MF is the common ancestor

Nisha draws a small diagram connecting Matrix Factorization to four things the team already trusts.

Takeaway

All four are really the same question — "can I describe this big matrix as the product of two smaller, more meaningful matrices?" — wearing different constraints as costumes. Arvind nods; question one is answered.

Check your understanding — 01

In the K-Means-as-Matrix-Factorization view, what does the seating-chart matrix Z actually store, and why is it restricted to 0s and 1s?

Explanation Z is the "seating chart" — one row per customer, one column per cluster. A 1 means "this customer sits at this table." Because K-Means gives every point to exactly one cluster, each row has a single 1. Let those values become fractions instead, and you've moved from K-Means to a Gaussian Mixture Model.
Question 02

How detailed should a profile be? — choosing d

Meera's second question is about a number Nisha glossed over: when the giant user-item matrix A is broken into a customer-profile matrix B and a product-profile matrix C, each profile is a list of d numbers — A ≈ B⋅Cᵀ.

The analogy — describing a person with crayons

Imagine you're only allowed to describe a customer using a fixed number of "traits," like crayons in a box. d is that crayon count. Too few, and the model can't tell people apart. Too many, and it stops generalizing.

1
crayon

"This customer leans toward X" — barely useful, too vague to tell customers apart.

3
crayons

"Price-sensitive," "brand-loyal," "snack-lover" — starting to feel real.

300
crayons

Basically memorizing each customer's exact history. That's overfitting.

Two ways to pick d

Approach 1 — the elbow point (quick, but risky). Try many values of d, plot the reconstruction loss against d. Loss keeps shrinking as d grows, but the curve bends at some point — the "elbow." The catch: this loss is measured on data the model already saw, so a huge d can just be memorizing.

Approach 2 — train/test split (slower, but honest). Hide 20% of known purchases, train B and C on the remaining 80%, then check how well those profiles predict the hidden 20%. Pick the d that does best on data it never saw.

Check your understanding — 02

Why is "train on 80%, test on 20% of known purchases" generally more trustworthy than picking the elbow point on a loss-vs-d curve computed on all the data?

Explanation The elbow method only looks at how well the model reconstructs data it already trained on — a model can look great there simply by memorizing. Holding out a genuine test set tells you whether the learned profiles will actually predict future, unseen customer behaviour — the real goal.
Question 03

Will the numbers make sense on a dashboard? — Non-Negative MF

Meera's concern is specific: merchandising wants a dashboard that says "this customer scores high on 'festive shopper' affinity." If that score can go negative, what does "−0.4 festive shopper" even mean?

Ordinary Matrix Factorization doesn't promise this — the numbers inside B and C can be positive or negative, because the optimizer only tries to make B⋅Cᵀ close to A. It never promised the individual numbers would be human-friendly. Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) adds one rule: force every number in B and/or C to be zero or positive.

The analogy — mixing paint, not signing a bank statement

A bank balance can go negative — you can be in debt. But describing "how much red, how much blue, how much yellow" went into mixing a paint colour, negative amounts make no sense. NMF forces customer and product traits to behave like paint quantities: present to some degree, never anti-present.

This is useful in two places DailyMart cares about: the customer-segmentation dashboard itself — remember the seating chart Z from Question 1? Already secretly non-negative. NMF is the general-purpose cousin of clustering. And product images, where pixel brightness values are naturally non-negative too.

The trade-off

NMF is a stricter version of MF, so it fits the data slightly less precisely. For anything customer-facing or dashboard-facing, that's a fair price for interpretability. For DailyMart's core "what will this person buy next" engine, plain MF is fine — nobody needs to read the individual numbers, the system only needs B⋅Cᵀ to be accurate.

Question 04

We have more than one signal — combining multiple matrices

DailyMart's app logs more than completed purchases. It also tracks P (purchased), W (added to cart), and D (removed from cart before checkout — a soft negative signal). Three matrices, all shaped customers × products. How should Nisha combine them?

A = P + W + D

Purchases and cart-removals are opposite signals. A customer who removed a product is telling you close to the reverse of what a purchase tells you. Blindly summing adds a "yes" and a "no" and hopes the result still makes sense. It doesn't.

A = α₁P + α₂W + α₃D

More sensible — give purchases a strong positive weight (say 1), cart-adds a smaller positive weight (say 0.1, weaker intent), and cart-removals a negative weight (say −1). The catch: three new hyperparameters to tune, and getting them wrong quietly biases the whole system.

Factorize P, W, and D independently. Each customer ends up with three separate profile vectors — purchase behaviour, browsing interest, rejection — combined with sensible business logic at the end: recommend items the purchase- and cart-profile both point toward, downweight anything close to the rejection-profile.

The analogy

Idea 1 is like averaging someone's opinions on three unrelated topics into a single number and hoping it means something. Idea 3 is like keeping their three separate opinions distinct, and only combining them at the very last step, when a decision is actually needed — preserving far more nuance.

Nisha's recommendation

Idea 3. It's more work upfront, but it avoids both the "meaningless sum" problem of Idea 1 and the "fragile hand-tuned weights" problem of Idea 2.

Check your understanding — 03

DailyMart has a purchases matrix P and a cart-removals matrix D. Why is directly computing A = P + D (Idea 1) usually a bad starting point?

Explanation Purchases and cart-removals point in roughly opposite directions — one says "I want this," the other says "I decided against this." Naively summing treats a yes and a no as if they simply add up, losing the distinction that made each signal useful. Weighting them, or factorizing independently before combining, preserves that distinction.
Question 05

Who else has solved this at scale? — the Netflix Prize

Around 2008–09, Netflix released an anonymized movie-rating dataset and offered a $1 million prize to improve its recommendation accuracy by 10%, measured using RMSE. The winning solution combined dozens of models — but Matrix Factorization became especially important.

1. Stochastic Gradient Descent

MF needs a way to find the best customer and product profiles. SGD repeatedly looks at a known rating, measures the prediction error, and slightly adjusts the profiles to reduce it — think of it like adjusting a recipe one taste-test at a time. A small regularization penalty stops the profiles from becoming unnecessarily extreme and overfitting.

2. Adding biases

Not every customer or product behaves the same way. Some customers naturally rate higher; some products tend to receive higher ratings. So the prediction breaks into: overall average + product bias + customer bias + taste match. This stops the model from confusing a customer's general rating style with their actual preference.

3. Preferences change over time

Preferences drift. For DailyMart, this matters around festivals and seasonal events — a customer's preferences during Diwali may look very different from an ordinary month.

The lesson

Matrix Factorization works well, but practical systems need SGD, regularization, biases, and time-aware preferences to make the predictions truly useful.

Check your understanding — 04

In the rating formula r ≈ μ + b_u + b_i + (profile match), what does the customer bias term b_u capture?

Explanation b_u isolates a customer's personal rating habit — a tendency to rate generously or to be a tough critic — separately from how much they genuinely like a specific product. Without it, the model risks confusing "this customer is naturally stingy with stars" for "this customer disliked the product."
Question 06

Can we reuse any of this? — MF as feature engineering

Once Nisha's model learns a d-dimensional profile for every customer and product, those profiles can be reused as features in other ML models — like a shared filing cabinet: DailyMart builds the profiles once, and other teams reuse them instead of starting from scratch.

TeamHow they can use the profiles
SearchFind products with similar profiles for "customers who viewed this also liked…"
AdsUse customer + product profiles to predict whether someone will click an ad
Other ML modelsUse the learned vectors directly as input features
Important

We don't need to know what each of the d numbers means. What matters is: similar customers → similar vectors, similar products → similar vectors.

Text: product descriptions & reviews

Build a matrix — rows are documents/products, columns are words, values are how often each word appears — then factorize it. Each word gets a compact d-dimensional vector, and words used in similar contexts end up close together in vector space. Another option is a co-occurrence matrix, where (i, j) is how often word i appears near word j — words like "principal" and "component" often appear together in data-science text, so their vectors become similar.

Truncated SVD

Text matrices can become huge. Instead of keeping every possible direction, Truncated SVD keeps only the most useful k directions — think: keep the 5 most important points from a long meeting instead of the entire transcript. Matrix Factorization + SVD → compact, useful representations.

Check your understanding — 05

DailyMart's search team wants to reuse the product profile vectors the recommendation team already built via Matrix Factorization. What are they relying on to make this work?

Explanation MF profile vectors don't come with interpretable labels, and don't need to be non-negative to be reusable (that's only needed for NMF-style dashboards). What makes them useful is simpler: products that behave similarly end up placed near each other in vector space, so nearest-neighbour lookups reliably surface genuinely similar items.

Wrap-up: what we covered

Matrix Factorization isn't brand new — it's the same "decompose into two smaller, meaningful matrices" pattern behind K-Means and GMM clustering.

The number of hidden traits (d) should be chosen with an honest train/test split, not just the elbow point on a training-only loss curve.

Non-Negative Matrix Factorization trades a little accuracy for numbers that stay human-interpretable — useful for dashboards and clustering-style outputs.

Multiple behavioural signals are best factorized independently, then combined thoughtfully, rather than summed naively.

The Netflix Prize showed that SGD, regularization, bias terms, and time-aware retraining turn a conceptual MF idea into a genuinely accurate, production-worthy engine.

The customer/product profile vectors learned along the way are reusable features — powering search and ads, and generalizing to text and even images.
