Setting the Scene

🏭 The Production Requirement

The retail client is blunt: "We can't keep re-running a spreadsheet every quarter. We need this segmentation living in BigQuery, refreshing automatically, feeding straight into our marketing tools."

Aditi faces the same three questions as before — who bought recently, who buys often, who spends the most — but now she must answer them in SQL, joining tables, computing quantiles across the full customer base, and encoding segment logic as a query anyone on the team can re-run.

Why Three Dimensions, and Why 125 Permutations

Before the SQL, the reason RFM uses three signals rather than one. Valuing customers on a single parameter is flawed — and at 5 bands per metric the scoring space gets large fast.

⚠️ One Parameter Is Never Enough

The biggest-value customer may have only purchased once or twice in a year. The most frequent purchaser may have a value so low it is almost not profitable to service them. One parameter will never give you an accurate view of your customer base — and you'll ignore customer lifetime value entirely. RFM scores all three together: a customer earns more points if they bought in the recent past, bought many times, or spent more.

With 5 bands per metric, there are 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 possible RFM score combinations. Managing 125 individual marketing responses is impractical — so similar permutations are grouped into the UK Data & Marketing Association's (DMA) 11 named segments, each with a prescribed marketing strategy.

The DMA's 11 segments and the marketing action recommended for each

5 R bands × 5 F bands × 5 M bands = 125 permutations. More resolution than last week's 3×3×3 = 27.

F + M Combined

Frequency and Monetary are averaged into a single FM score, reducing from 125 to 25 (5 R × 5 FM) combinations to work with.

11 DMA Segments

The DMA model groups the 25 R×FM combinations into 11 named, actionable segments with prescribed marketing strategies.

Q1 · With 5 bands each for R, F and M, there are 125 possible permutations. Why does the DMA model group these into just 11 named segments instead of 125 individual responses?
A Because BigQuery cannot handle more than 11 categories
B Because many permutations call for very similar marketing responses, so grouping into 11 segments keeps the strategy actionable without losing meaningful distinctions
C Because only 11 permutations are mathematically possible
D Because customers cannot be scored higher than 3 in any metric
✓ Correct! 125 individual permutations is too granular to manage — a customer scoring (5,5,4) and one scoring (5,5,5) don't need fundamentally different campaigns. Grouping into 11 segments keeps the model both statistically grounded and operationally usable.

The 5-Step SQL Pipeline

The BigQuery RFM pipeline runs in 5 sequential steps, each saving its output as a new table for the next step:

1

Data Processing

Calculate total cost per line item (Quantity × UnitPrice), then aggregate to total bill per invoice using a CTE + GROUP BY. Save as bill table.

2

Compute R / F / M per Customer

Join sales + bill tables. Get last_purchase_date, first_purchase_date, num_purchases and monetary per customer. Then compute recency (days from reference date) and frequency (purchases ÷ months active). Save as RFM table.

3

Determine Quintiles

Use APPROX_QUANTILES(col, 100) to get the 20th, 40th, 60th, 80th and 100th percentile cut-offs for M, F and R. Save as quintiles table.

4

Assign Scores 1–5

CASE statements map each customer's raw value to a score. Recency is reversed (low days → score 5). F and M go low-to-high. Average F and M scores into fm_score. Save as score table.

5

Define Segments (R × FM Grid)

A final CASE statement maps every (r_score, fm_score) pair to one of the 11 DMA segments — from Champions down to Lost.

Step 1–2: Data Processing & R/F/M Values

Total Bill per Invoice

-- CTE: compute amount per line item WITH bills AS ( SELECT InvoiceNo, (Quantity * UnitPrice) AS amount FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.sales` ) SELECT InvoiceNo, SUM(amount) AS total FROM bills GROUP BY InvoiceNo

Compute R / F / M per Customer

-- Step A: first/last purchase, purchase count, monetary total per customer SELECT CustomerID, DATE(MAX(InvoiceDate)) AS last_purchase_date, DATE(MIN(InvoiceDate)) AS first_purchase_date, COUNT(DISTINCT InvoiceNo) AS num_purchases, SUM(total) AS monetary FROM ( -- one row per invoice, so SUM(total) doesn't double-count the bill SELECT s.CustomerID, s.InvoiceDate, s.InvoiceNo, b.total, ROW_NUMBER() OVER(PARTITION BY s.InvoiceNo ORDER BY s.InvoiceNo) AS RN FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.sales` s LEFT JOIN `customer-segmentation.retail.bill` b ON s.InvoiceNo = b.InvoiceNo ) A WHERE A.RN = 1 GROUP BY CustomerID -- Step B: recency & frequency SELECT *, DATE_DIFF(reference_date, last_purchase_date, DAY) AS recency, num_purchases / (months_cust) AS frequency FROM ( SELECT *, MAX(last_purchase_date) OVER () + 1 AS reference_date, DATE_DIFF(last_purchase_date, first_purchase_date, month) + 1 AS months_cust FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.monetary` ) ORDER BY CustomerID
Q2 · Frequency here is calculated as num_purchases / months_cust, not just the raw count. Why divide by the number of months the customer has been active?
A Division is only used to make the numbers smaller
B It normalises for how long the customer has been around — 10 purchases in 2 months is a much more frequent buyer than 10 purchases in 24 months, and raw counts alone can't tell them apart
C BigQuery requires all metrics to be fractions
D months_cust is always equal to 1, so it has no real effect
✓ Correct! A raw purchase count rewards long-tenured customers even if they buy rarely. Dividing by months_cust converts the count into a genuine rate — purchases per month — so a newer, highly active customer and an older, sporadic one are compared fairly on how often they actually buy.

Step 3: Quintile Boundaries with APPROX_QUANTILES()

Instead of hardcoding cut-off values (which would change each quarter), BigQuery computes them dynamically from the actual data distribution — so the model works for any dataset automatically.

🔗 Interactive: Quintile Histogram
-- Get the 20th, 40th, 60th, 80th and 100th percentile cut-offs for M, F and R SELECT a.*, -- Monetary percentiles b.percentiles[offset(20)] AS m20, b.percentiles[offset(40)] AS m40, b.percentiles[offset(60)] AS m60, b.percentiles[offset(80)] AS m80, b.percentiles[offset(100)] AS m100, -- Frequency percentiles c.percentiles[offset(20)] AS f20, c.percentiles[offset(40)] AS f40, c.percentiles[offset(60)] AS f60, c.percentiles[offset(80)] AS f80, c.percentiles[offset(100)] AS f100, -- Recency percentiles d.percentiles[offset(20)] AS r20, d.percentiles[offset(40)] AS r40, d.percentiles[offset(60)] AS r60, d.percentiles[offset(80)] AS r80, d.percentiles[offset(100)] AS r100 FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.RFM` a, (SELECT APPROX_QUANTILES(monetary, 100) percentiles FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.RFM`) b, (SELECT APPROX_QUANTILES(frequency, 100) percentiles FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.RFM`) c, (SELECT APPROX_QUANTILES(recency, 100) percentiles FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.RFM`) d ORDER BY CustomerID

💡 Why APPROX_QUANTILES?

APPROX_QUANTILES(col, 100) returns an array of 101 elements — the first is the approximate minimum, the last the approximate maximum — and OFFSET() picks an element out by position. The trade-off is real: approximate aggregate functions are scalable in terms of memory usage and time, but they produce approximate results instead of exact ones. For quintile cut-offs that is an acceptable trade — a customer sitting right on a boundary shifting one band does not change the marketing strategy.

-- Assign 1–5 per metric; combine F+M into fm_score SELECT CustomerID, m_score, f_score, r_score, CAST(ROUND((f_score + m_score) / 2, 0) AS INT64) AS fm_score FROM ( SELECT *, CASE WHEN monetary <= m20 THEN 1 WHEN monetary <= m40 AND monetary > m20 THEN 2 WHEN monetary <= m60 AND monetary > m40 THEN 3 WHEN monetary <= m80 AND monetary > m60 THEN 4 WHEN monetary <= m100 AND monetary > m80 THEN 5 END AS m_score, -- f_score: same direction as m_score CASE WHEN frequency <= f20 THEN 1 WHEN frequency <= f40 AND frequency > f20 THEN 2 WHEN frequency <= f60 AND frequency > f40 THEN 3 WHEN frequency <= f80 AND frequency > f60 THEN 4 WHEN frequency <= f100 AND frequency > f80 THEN 5 END AS f_score, -- r_score: REVERSED (low recency days = high score) CASE WHEN recency <= r20 THEN 5 WHEN recency <= r40 AND recency > r20 THEN 4 WHEN recency <= r60 AND recency > r40 THEN 3 WHEN recency <= r80 AND recency > r60 THEN 2 WHEN recency <= r100 AND recency > r80 THEN 1 END AS r_score FROM `customer-segmentation.retail.quintiles` )

Q3 · A customer has r_score = 1 and fm_score = 5. Which segment does the SQL CASE statement assign them to, and why does that make business sense?
A Champions — because their FM score is maximum
B Can't Lose Them — they historically bought a lot and often (high FM), but haven't purchased in a very long time (lowest R), so they're a high-value customer at real risk of being lost
C Lost — because their recency score is the lowest possible
D Recent Customers — because they are still active
✓ Correct! r_score=1 means it's been a very long time since their last purchase. fm_score=5 means, historically, they bought often and spent a lot. The CASE statement's "r_score=1 AND fm_score=5 → Can't Lose Them" branch captures exactly this: don't let a high-value customer quietly churn.

⚖️ What RFM Does Not See

This type of segmentation focuses on actual buying behaviour and ignores the differences in motivations, intentions and lifestyles of consumers. It will tell you that a customer went quiet; it will not tell you why. RFM is nonetheless a useful starting point, and because of its simplicity it can be executed fast and in an automated way — giving companies the power to act and decide on business strategies swiftly.

Other Segmentation Models

RFM is one of four main customer segmentation approaches — it's the most automatable, which is why it runs so well as a SQL pipeline:

💻

Technographic

Segment by technology usage — device type, software, online services subscribed to.

🛒

Behavioral (RFM)

Divide by buying habits, likes, and wants. Price-focused vs quality-focused vs brand-focused. RFM lives here.

❤️

Needs-Based

Segment by financial, emotional, and physical needs — budget gift-seeker vs comfort-seeker, etc.

🔄

Customer Status / Lifecycle

Leads, new customers, loyal, at-risk, churned. RFM is the data-driven method to achieve this automatically.

Today's Journey

01

Scale Problem

5 bands × 5 × 5 = 125 permutations → grouped into 11 DMA segments. F+M averaged into fm_score to reduce to 25 cells.

02

Data Processing

CTE collapses line items → bill-per-invoice. Same instinct as a Sheets Pivot Table, just in SQL.

03

Frequency as Rate

num_purchases ÷ months_cust = purchases per month. Prevents long-tenured-but-inactive customers being over-scored.

04

APPROX_QUANTILES

Computes quintile cut-offs from the data itself — no hardcoded thresholds. Scales to any dataset automatically.

05

Recency Inversion

Recency CASE runs backwards: low days (recent) → score 5; high days (old) → score 1. Same rule as Sheets, different SQL direction.

06

11 DMA Segments

Champions, Loyal Customers, Potential Loyalists, Promising, Customers Needing Attention, Recent Customers, About to Sleep, At Risk, Can't Lose Them, Hibernating, Lost.
