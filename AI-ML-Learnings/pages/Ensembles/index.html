Why Do We Need Ensemble Learning?

So far, we have studied several supervised learning algorithms, including Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, KNN, and Decision Trees. Each of these models learns patterns from the training data and makes predictions independently.

However, no machine learning model is perfect. Every model has its own strengths and limitations. For example, a Decision Tree is highly interpretable but is also sensitive to small changes in the training data. Even a slight variation in the dataset can produce a completely different tree, leading to different predictions.

This naturally raises an interesting question:

Can we improve our predictions by combining multiple models instead of relying on just one?

04

What are Ensembles?

Instead of relying on a single model, what if we train multiple base learners (models) and combine their predictions intelligently?

The key idea is:

Train many base learners (models) that are as different as possible and combine them smartly.

For example, instead of training one Decision Tree, we could train 100 Decision Trees and combine their predictions to make a final decision.

This is the core principle of Ensemble Learning.

The word ensemble generally refers to a collection or combination of multiple entities working together. Similarly, in machine learning, we combine multiple models to build a stronger predictor.

An ensemble can consist of:

  • Multiple instances of the same model (e.g., many Decision Trees).
  • Different types of models, such as Decision Trees, Logistic Regression, and KNN, whose predictions are combined intelligently.
05

What are the Types of Ensembles?

Ensemble Learning can be broadly categorized into three major approaches:

  1. Bagging
    • Trains multiple learners independently.
    • Example: Random Forest
  2. Boosting
    • Trains learners sequentially, with each learner improving upon the previous one.
    • Example: Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT)
  3. Stacking
    • Trains multiple base learners and combines their predictions using a meta-learner.
    • Example: Decision Tree + SVM + Logistic Regression → Meta-Learner

In practice, hybrid approaches that combine ideas from these techniques are also possible.

We have now seen the three major types of ensemble learning at a high level. To understand how ensemble methods work in practice, let's begin with Bagging, the simplest and one of the most widely used ensemble techniques. Before we study the algorithm itself, let's build an intuition through a real-life analogy that captures the core idea behind Bagging. This intuition will make the formal concepts much easier to understand.

Bagging refers to training different models for the same task, independently of each other and then cleverly combining their predictions.

Boosting

Boosting Analogy


So far, we have explored one major family of ensemble methods—Bagging. In Bagging, multiple base learners are trained independently on different subsets of the training data, and their predictions are combined using voting (for classification) or averaging (for regression). This makes the overall model more stable and helps reduce variance.

However, Bagging is not the only way to build an ensemble. There is another powerful approach called Boosting, where the base learners are not trained independently. Instead, they are trained sequentially, with each new learner trying to correct the mistakes made by the previous one. Rather than combining many independent opinions, Boosting builds a strong model by gradually improving weak learners, one step at a time.

In the upcoming lecture, we will study Boosting in detail—understanding the intuition behind sequential learning, how weak learners are combined, and why this approach is often able to achieve excellent predictive performance.

09

Stacking

Unlike Bagging and Boosting, which combine learners through voting, averaging, or weighted combinations, Stacking introduces an additional model to make the final prediction.

Instead of directly combining the outputs of multiple learners, Stacking first trains several different models and then uses another model, called a meta-learner, to learn how to combine their predictions effectively.

How Does Stacking Work?

The process can be understood in three simple steps:

  1. Train multiple base learners on the same dataset.
  2. Each learner makes its own prediction.
  3. A meta-learner takes these predictions as input and produces the final prediction.

Rather than relying on a fixed rule such as majority voting, the meta-learner learns the best way to combine the predictions of the base models.

Example

Suppose we train three different models:

  • A Decision Tree
  • A Support Vector Machine (SVM)
  • A Logistic Regression model

Each model makes a prediction for the same input.

Instead of selecting the majority prediction, a meta-learner uses the predictions from all three models to determine the final output. In this way, the ensemble learns how much to trust each base learner for making better predictions.

10

How Does Combining Multiple Models Help?

So far, we have seen that an ensemble combines the predictions of multiple models instead of relying on a single one.

But an important question still remains:

Why does combining multiple models often produce better predictions?

The key idea is that different models do not always make the same mistakes. While one model may misclassify a particular data point, another model may classify it correctly.

When we combine the predictions of several models using majority voting (for classification) or averaging (for regression), the correct predictions tend to reinforce one another, making the final prediction more reliable.

Intuition

Suppose we have three decision trees making predictions for the same data point.

Model Prediction
Tree 1 Cat
Tree 2 Dog
Tree 3 Cat

If we rely only on Tree 2, the prediction would be Dog, which is incorrect.

However, if we combine all three predictions using majority voting, the final prediction becomes Cat, since two out of the three models agree on that class.

In this case, the ensemble is able to correct the mistake made by an individual model.

Notice that the ensemble does not require every model to be perfect.

Instead, the idea is that different models contribute different perspectives, and by combining these predictions, the ensemble often produces a result that is more stable and reliable than relying on any single model alone.

This is the fundamental reason why ensemble methods are widely used in Machine Learning.

Bagging Vs Boosting

So far, we have introduced the two major families of ensemble learning:

  • Bagging
  • Boosting

Although both combine multiple learners to build a stronger model, they differ significantly in the way those learners are trained.

The key difference lies in how the individual learners are created and combined.

Comparison

Property Bagging Boosting
Training Strategy Parallel Sequential
Learners Independent Dependent
Main Idea Combine many independent learners Improve learners one after another
Prediction Voting / Averaging Weighted combination of learners

Understanding the Difference

Imagine you have to solve a difficult problem.

Bagging is like asking many people to solve the problem independently and then combining their answers.

On the other hand, Boosting is like having one person solve the problem repeatedly, learning from their previous mistakes after every attempt until the final answer improves.

Although both approaches produce an ensemble, the learning strategy is fundamentally different.

Bias-Variance Tradeoff

When building Machine Learning models, we often face a tradeoff between bias and variance.

  • A model with high bias is usually too simple and may fail to capture important patterns in the data, leading to underfitting.
  • A model with high variance is often too complex and may fit the training data very closely, resulting in overfitting.

The goal is to build a model that strikes a balance between these two extremes, achieving good performance on both the training data and unseen data.

Although both Bagging and Boosting combine multiple learners, they improve model performance in different ways.

  • Bagging primarily helps reduce variance by combining multiple independently trained learners.
  • Boosting primarily helps reduce bias by sequentially improving weak learners.

As a result, ensemble methods often produce models that generalize better than a single learner.

Method Primarily Reduces Main Idea
Bagging Variance Combine many independent learners
Boosting Bias Improve learners sequentially

This does not mean that Bagging only affects variance or that Boosting only affects bias. In practice, both techniques can influence both aspects of model performance.

However, as a general rule of thumb:

  • Bagging → Lower Variance
  • Boosting → Lower Bias

This simple intuition is often sufficient when deciding which ensemble approach is appropriate for a problem.

Random Forest: The Most Popular Bagging Algorithm

So far, we have learned that Bagging is a general ensemble learning technique that combines multiple independently trained learners.

A natural question now is:

Which machine learning algorithm makes the best use of Bagging?

One of the most successful answers is the Random Forest algorithm.

Random Forest is one of the most widely used ensemble methods in Machine Learning because it combines the simplicity of Decision Trees with the power of Bagging.

The Decision tree base learners in Random Forest are Deep Decision Trees (non-shallow).

As they are deep,

  • the models overfit slightly on a sub sample of data, as the base learners are made of m rows and d′ columns only.

Now, we perform aggregation/averaging on these slightly overfit models (high variance and low bias)

  • This aggregation helps in reducing the variance of the model.

We just said that aggregation reduces variance. But that is a big claim. Why should averaging a bunch of overfit models give us something better? Let us build the intuition step by step so this does not feel like a magic trick.

Why Does Bagging Reduce Variance?

(Recall) Question: What does overfit mean?

Let's try to understand the intuition behind how bagging reduces variance

  • Say, we had n points in our training data
  • we sampled m points and made k samples for k models.

Remember, High variance model means model changes if data points change slightly.

and

Base learners are high variance, low bias models

Let's assume

we are representing varying datapoint using yellow point

Question: When we sample, how many sample will contain yellow point?

Answer: Only a handful of samples will have yellow point

when there's a change in yellow datapoint,

  • it'll only impact handful of base learners

On top of that, we are aggregating the results of all base learners

  • so it'll negate the impact of noise/ variance in datapoints.

Hence, reducing the variance.

At first glance, Random Forest may seem straightforward—just train many Decision Trees and combine their predictions.

However, an important question still remains.

If every Decision Tree is trained on exactly the same data, won't they all become identical?

If all the trees make similar predictions, combining them will provide very little benefit.

To make Bagging effective, the individual trees must be different from one another.
