INTERACTIVE POST READ

Streamlit and Optuna

From static notebooks to interactive, optimized apps

A model that works is not finished. Two things usually happen next, and both are usually done badly: choosing hyperparameters honestly, and letting somebody else touch the model. This read covers what Optuna and Streamlit actually do about each — and spends most of its time on the part that isn't easy: reading the numbers they produce without fooling yourself.

Preface

Two gaps, one afternoon of API

Last time ended with a repository: code with a history, commits with messages, and a clear answer to which version produced which script. That solved the code half of reproducibility. This session is about the two things that happen immediately after a model starts working.

The first is choosing hyperparameters. You can already search a space — GridSearchCV enumerates one, RandomizedSearchCV samples one — and for a handful of candidate values, neither needs replacing. What neither gives you is a search that uses its own results to decide where to look next, a space that can branch, or a record of the search that outlives the process that ran it. Optuna is those three things, and this session is as much about when they're worth a new dependency as about how to call them.

The second is letting somebody else touch the model. A notebook is a development environment, not a delivery format. A domain expert who wants to know why the model flagged a particular case cannot read a predict call. Streamlit turns a Python script into a web application with no front-end work, which makes the model something a non-engineer can interrogate.

Both tools are easy to use. The part that isn't easy — the part this session spends most of its time on — is reading the numbers they produce without fooling yourself. An optimizer will always hand you a best value. Whether that number means anything depends on what you optimized against and what you optimized for, and this post measures the cost of getting either one wrong.

BY THE END, YOU WILL BE ABLE TO

1) Say what a TPE sampler does that GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV cannot — and when that difference doesn't justify a new dependency.
2) Choose an objective metric that matches the problem, and show why accuracy is the wrong one when the positive class is rare.
3) Write an Optuna objective function, run a study, and inspect its trials.

01 — Motivation

The two gaps after a model works

Consider a situation that happens at every company that does ML. You've spent two weeks on a classifier. It reaches 85% accuracy on your held-out set. You send the notebook to your manager.

"I cannot open this. Can you make it so I can type in some numbers and see whether the prediction makes sense? And can we get that accuracy up?" — your manager, reading a .ipynb file

Two separate requests, two separate gaps.

GAP 1

The interaction gap

A notebook is a development artifact. It assumes a Python kernel, your package versions, your file paths, and a reader who can tell a cell from its output. Everyone who needs to judge whether the model behaves sensibly — the domain expert, the product manager, the person accountable when it's wrong — is locked out.

GAP 2

The optimization gap

n_estimators=100 was not a decision. It was a default that survived because nobody challenged it. There's no record of what else was tried, no evidence the shipped configuration beats one that wasn't tried, and no way to repeat the search.

Streamlit closes the interaction gap. Optuna closes the optimization gap. Neither is hard to use — the rest of this post is mostly about using them without drawing conclusions they don't support.
02 — Concepts

What Optuna adds to the search you already know

You've already searched a hyperparameter space. GridSearchCV enumerates a grid, RandomizedSearchCV samples from distributions for a fixed budget, and both are two lines of code. The question is narrow: what can a search do that those two cannot?

Vocabulary
Trial

One configuration, evaluated once, producing one number. In the vocabulary you already have, a trial is one row of cv_results_.

Vocabulary
Study

A collection of trials pursuing one objective — the whole table, except an Optuna study is an object in its own right rather than an attribute hanging off a fitted estimator.

There are three answers, and only the first is about the algorithm.

A search that uses its own results

GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV share a property that's easy to miss because it sounds like a virtue: neither looks at its own results. A grid is fully determined before the first fit. A random search draws its ninetieth sample from exactly the distribution it drew the first from — eighty-nine evaluations of which regions are good change nothing about where the ninetieth lands.

Sequential model-based search drops that. Keep a record of every trial and the score it achieved. Split the record into configurations that did well and the ones that didn't, and fit a simple probability density over each group — call them l(x) for the good ones and g(x) for the rest. To choose the next configuration, sample candidates and keep the one that maximizes the ratio l(x) / g(x): the point that looks most like what worked and least like what didn't.

THE TREE-STRUCTURED PARZEN ESTIMATOR (TPE)

That's TPE, Optuna's default sampler. It spends its first n_startup_trials sampling at random, because a density fitted to three points is worthless, and concentrates after that. It is not magic — on a space where every configuration scores about the same, it buys you nothing over random sampling.

3.2  A baseline to beat, and the trap in the metric

Tuning is only meaningful against a reference. Before the reference, though, "better" has to be defined — and on a rare-class problem the obvious choice is a trap.

TWO THINGS THAT DECIDE THE REST OF THE SESSION

A model that never predicts an eight scores about 90% accuracy. It's completely useless — it finds nothing — and on the metric most people reach for first, it looks respectable. Accuracy on a 10% positive rate mostly measures the majority class.

The default random forest has perfect precision and poor recall. Everything it calls an eight really is an eight, and it misses roughly a third of the eights that exist. In the real versions of this problem — the fraud not flagged, the defect that shipped, the disease not caught — the misses are the expensive half.

So the search optimizes F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, only high when both are. That single decision is worth more than every hyperparameter about to be searched over — and it's made in the objective function, not anywhere in Optuna's API.

3.3  The objective function

An objective function takes a trial, asks it for hyperparameter values, does the work, and returns one number. That's the entire interface. Two decisions are encoded below, and both matter more than anything Optuna does with them.

# One configuration -> one cross-validated F1 on the TRAINING set.
def objective(trial):
    params = {
        "n_estimators": trial.suggest_int("n_estimators", 50, 300),
        "max_depth": trial.suggest_int("max_depth", 2, 20),
        "min_samples_leaf": trial.suggest_int("min_samples_leaf", 1, 20),
        "class_weight": trial.suggest_categorical(
            "class_weight", [None, "balanced", "balanced_subsample"]
        ),
        "random_state": RANDOM_STATE,
        "n_jobs": -1,
    }
    model = RandomForestClassifier(**params)
    cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=4, shuffle=True, random_state=RANDOM_STATE)
    return cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="f1").mean()
Decision 1 — class_weight is in the space at all
The default forest's problem was recall, and class_weight is the recall knob — so the search is pointed at the failure the baseline actually has. What that single entry turns out to be worth gets measured in section 4.
Decision 2 — what the function returns
It returns a cross-validated F1 computed inside the training set, and never touches X_test. That's not a stylistic preference — the cost of getting it wrong is measured in section 4.2.

Optuna doesn't care what the number means. It cares only whether to push it up or down, via direction. Return an RMSE and minimize. Return an F1 and maximize. Return a weighted blend of recall and inference latency and maximize that. The objective is where the actual definition of "better" lives.

A NOTE ON STORAGE

By default a study lives in memory and disappears when the kernel restarts. Passing storage="sqlite:///rf_eight.db" with load_if_exists=True persists it, letting a study resume after a crash or several workers contribute to the same study. That's the right choice for a long search on a real problem — and the wrong choice here, since re-running the cell would silently append another thirty trials. Studies that need to survive belong in a tracking system, the next thing this course builds.

Three things, in order of how much they should change your behavior:

Optuna scored highest on a third of the fits the grid needed

The grid has to try every combination — 3×3×3×3 = 81 configurations, each fitted once per fold. Optuna and random search were both given 30 configurations. That ratio gets worse for the grid as the space grows: a fourth value per parameter takes the grid to 256 configurations while leaving the other two exactly where they are, because their cost is a budget you choose rather than a product you're stuck with.

At an equal budget, TPE beat random sampling

The same 120 fits, a better result. Don't over-read one comparison on one dataset — on a small space it often doesn't hold when you vary the budget.

None of the three picked class_weight = None

They disagree about n_estimators and max_depth, and agree about the thing that matters. The grid also couldn't express the winning n_estimators=167 — it had to be picked from [50, 150, 300] in advance.

3.7  The importance paradox

Optuna can attribute the variance in trial scores to individual parameters. The result is useful and very easily misread, so this covers both.

Hyperparameter importance
optuna.importance.get_param_importances(study)
READ THIS AGAINST THE TRIAL TABLE ABOVE IT

class_weight is last, with an importance of essentially zero. Yet every one of the top five trials set it to balanced, and the worst trials in the study are the ones that didn't.

Both results are correct — they answer different questions. Importance is computed over the trials that actually ran, attributing variance in the objective to the parameters that varied. TPE settled the class_weight question within its first few trials and then stopped exploring it. A parameter that stops varying stops explaining variance, so the parameter that mattered most sinks to the bottom precisely because the search resolved it immediately and spent the rest of its budget elsewhere.

When a parameter looks unimportant, group the trials by it and look at the scores directly before believing it:

Only 3 of 30 trials used None and 8 used balanced_subsample — TPE spent most of its budget on balanced once that pattern emerged, which is also why the group sizes are so uneven.

The three trials with no class weighting scored far below the twenty-one that used balanced. That's a large effect, and the importance ranking put it last. So read the importance list as what was still in contention when the search stopped, not what matters — genuinely useful for that, since parameters at the bottom can often be fixed at the value the search chose to buy more trials elsewhere. The causal question is answered properly by removing a parameter from the space and re-running, which section 4.1 does.

CHECKPOINT 3.1

Optuna's default sampler is TPE. Whether TPE beats random sampling on your problem is an empirical question, and it's cheap to answer.

  1. Run a second study with sampler=optuna.samplers.RandomSampler(seed=42), same objective, same 30 trials. Compare the two best values.
  2. Run both again with n_trials=8. TPE spends its first n_startup_trials (ten, by default) sampling randomly, so at eight trials you're comparing random search against random search.
  3. Print study.best_trial.number for each. A best trial found at trial 3 of 30 tells you something different from one found at trial 28.
04 — Judgment

Reading the result honestly

There's a tuned model and a number that went up. This section is about whether to believe it — and about the much worse situation where the number goes up because you made it. This is the part of the session that transfers. Optuna's API takes twenty minutes to learn and you won't think about it again. Deciding whether a reported improvement is real is a judgment you'll make for the rest of your career, usually under pressure from somebody who liked the first number.

4.1  Is the improvement real?

One train/test split gives one number, and that number has a standard error nobody printed. The way to find out whether an improvement survives is to repeat the whole procedure — split, baseline, tune, evaluate — across several different splits, then look at the distribution rather than the point. The cell below also runs a control: it repeats the identical search with class_weight removed from the space and everything else unchanged. If the gain is really about the search being clever, removing one entry shouldn't matter much.

with class_weight in the search space, vs. the same search with it removed
with class_weight in space
class_weight removed (control)
With class_weight: mean gain +0.0611, won 8 of 8 splits. Without it: mean gain −0.0039, won 1, tied 5, lost 2 of 8.
THE MOST USEFUL SENTENCE IN THIS SESSION

A search can only find what is in the space you gave it. Remove one entry and the gain collapses — same sampler, same trial budget, same objective, same everything else, and the search no longer reliably beats the default it started from. It's also the answer to the earlier importance paradox: importance describes the trials that ran; removing the parameter and re-running describes what it was worth. Optuna found class_weight="balanced" quickly because it was there to be found. Thirty trials over a space that doesn't contain the fix return the best of thirty near-identical wrong answers — with a plot that looks exactly like a successful search.

Two habits follow: before adding trials, ask whether the space contains anything that could plausibly fix the failure you actually have, and look at how the baseline fails before choosing what to search over. The default model's problem was recall, and class_weight is the recall knob — a connection available for the cost of reading a classification report.

4.2  The number you optimize is not an estimate

Now the failure that matters, because it's silent and it's everywhere. Suppose the objective function is pointed at the held-out set — returning F1 on X_test instead of a cross-validated score on the training data. Nothing errors. The code is shorter. The study runs faster, because it fits one model per trial instead of four.

Read the columns against each other. The leaky run reports the best number of the three by a wide margin — a perfect score. Every one of the hundred images classified correctly, exactly what you'd expect from a procedure that tried many configurations against those hundred images and kept whichever one happened to get them all. In production, that model does considerably worse: the gap is not a property of the model, it's the price of having selected on those rows.

The honest run reports a lower number, and that number survives contact with the production holdout — it comes in slightly under what the model actually delivers, the direction you want to be wrong in. Notice which model is better in production: the honest one. The leak didn't merely inflate a number, it also selected a worse configuration, because it optimized for the idiosyncrasies of a hundred images rather than for anything that generalizes.

A PERFECT SCORE IS A SYMPTOM, NOT AN ACHIEVEMENT

When a metric comes back at 1.0, the first hypothesis is not that the model is perfect. It's that the evaluation is broken — usually by leakage, a duplicated row, or a feature that encodes the label.

The last row is the one that decays. A test set consulted ten times during development is a validation set, and the number it produces is no longer an estimate of anything. If you've looked at it more than once, say so when you report the number.

05 — Serving

How Streamlit actually works

Streamlit turns a Python script into a web page. The one sentence the rest of it follows from, before any widget syntax:

A Streamlit app is a script that is re-executed from the first line to the last, every time anything changes.

Not a callback. Not a diff. The entire script, top to bottom. A user drags a slider, and Streamlit reruns the file from line one. st.slider returns the new position this time, the code below it recomputes, and the page is rebuilt. Module-level variables are gone and recreated on every rerun.

three consequences, and how Streamlit answers each
consequencethe mechanism
The page is laid out in execution orderlayout containers
Expensive work repeats on every interactioncaching
Ordinary variables can't hold statesession state

Six ideas, six scripts

Each idea below is one short script, run the same way and stopped with Ctrl-C: streamlit run snippets/01_hello.py.

st.write("This is a simple web app built with Python.") renders below the title, in the order the calls ran.

import streamlit as st

st.title("My First MLOps App")
st.write("This is a simple web app built with Python.")

There's no template and no layout engine. Each st. call appends its element to the page at the moment it executes — the title renders in the middle, because that's where the call ran.

import streamlit as st

st.write("I appear first")
st.title("Big Title")
st.write("I appear last")

This is trivial until you try to render a summary above a table whose contents you only compute below it — at which point you need st.empty() or st.container() to reserve a slot and fill it later.

st.sidebar writes to a second column that doesn't scroll with the main page. The convention is inputs on the left, results on the right — worth following, because users have learned it.

import streamlit as st

st.title("Main Page")
st.write("This is the main area")

st.sidebar.title("Sidebar")
st.sidebar.write("Controls usually go here")

A widget call returns the widget's current value. It does not register a callback — it hands back the value as it stands on this rerun.

import streamlit as st

st.header("User Inputs")
username = st.text_input("What is your name?", "Student")
age = st.slider("Select your age", 18, 100, 25)

st.write(f"On this rerun, username={username!r} and age={age}")

st.button is the exception that catches everyone. It returns True only on the rerun immediately following the click, and False on every rerun after that — a result rendered inside if st.button(...) disappears as soon as the user touches any other widget. Session state is the fix.

if st.button("Say Hello"):
    st.success(f"Hello {username}! You are {age} years old.")

st.header("Random Data Plot")
chart_data = pd.DataFrame(np.random.randn(20, 3), columns=["a", "b", "c"])
st.line_chart(chart_data)

That chart is also a bug you'll write by accident: np.random.randn runs on every rerun, so it redraws with different data every time the user moves the age slider — a slider that has nothing to do with the chart.

@st.cache_data remembers a function's return value, keyed by its arguments. Same arguments on the next rerun, no recomputation.

# cache HIT: the draw sits inside the function body
@st.cache_data
def load_data():
    time.sleep(3)
    n = np.random.randint(10)
    return f"This is {n}"

# cache MISS: the draw happens at the call site — it's an argument
@st.cache_data
def load_data(number):
    time.sleep(3)
    return f"This is {number}"

The 06 version waits three seconds on the first click and answers instantly after that, and the number never changes — the function ran exactly once. The 07 version waits three seconds every click and shows a different number each time, because the random draw happens at the call site and becomes part of the cache key. A cache whose key changes on every call isn't a cache — it's a dictionary that only grows, and it's the most common reason an app that "has caching" is still slow.

06 — The interface

The model playground

Now both halves together. app.py loads the artifact trained earlier and lets someone page through the test set, look at each image, and see what the model said about it — including, and especially, the cases it got wrong.

It works immediately, because rf_eight_best.joblib is committed alongside it. The app has three modes; Review cases is the one that matters, and the reason the app is worth building at all. A precision and recall pair tells a stakeholder that the model misses some eights. It doesn't tell them which ones — "show me the ones it missed" is the first thing any domain expert asks, and a table of 64 pixel columns can't answer that. A picture can.

a badly-written eight

The model gave this image a 33% chance of being an eight. A precision and recall pair could never have told you that. Six images in the test set are like it, and a domain expert can now page through all six and judge whether the model is being unreasonable, or the handwriting genuinely is ambiguous — a question only they can answer, and one they couldn't even be asked before the app existed.

Switching to auto-tuning and running a search shows the progress bar filling, then the result — deliberately two numbers rather than one:

st.success(f"Best cross-validated F1 (what was optimized): {study.best_value:.4f}")

st.info(f"F1 on the held-out test set (not optimized): {test_f1:.4f}")

The green number is what the search optimized. The blue number is the held-out score it never touched. They're close here — what honest tuning looks like. When they're far apart, something has leaked, and an app that showed only the green number would give no way to tell the two situations apart. Below the fold, the page also renders the trial table and the Optuna plots.

Four decisions inside app.py

Named columns on the way in

Every prediction goes through to_row(values), returning a DataFrame with columns in fitted order. Handing model.predict a bare array works, produces no error, and is wrong the moment pixels arrive in a different order — measured in section 8.1, where the cost is total.

The right cache decorator for each job

load_artifact uses @st.cache_resource; load_split uses @st.cache_data. Without either, every interaction reloads the model from disk and re-splits the dataset.

An in-memory study

A Streamlit script reruns constantly. A study created with storage="sqlite:///..." and a timestamped name would write a new study into that file on every rerun of the page, growing without limit.

Two numbers, labelled differently

The auto-tuning mode reports the cross-validated score it optimized and the test score it didn't. An app that shows only the first is how a tuned-on-the-test-set number reaches a slide deck.

A WARNING

Two of the app's three modes retrain from the browser. Training inside a user-facing app is not how production works. In production you train offline, save the artifact, and the serving app only ever loads it. Training in the request path means every user pays the training cost, two users training at once compete for the same CPU, and there's no record of which model answered which request. It's there because watching a search run in a browser makes the mechanics concrete — and it's the first thing you'd remove.

07 — Deployment

Sharing it

Running it locally is enough for you. The point of building it was somebody else, so it needs a URL.

A temporary link from Colab

Colab runs on a Google machine, so localhost:8501 is a port on a container you can't reach. A tunnel service gives that port a temporary public URL — fine for a demo, and not deployment. The link dies with the runtime, there's no authentication in front of it, and anyone holding it reaches your app.

Streamlit Community Cloud

This one is real deployment. It builds from a GitHub repository, gives a persistent public URL, and rebuilds on push — the first continuous deployment loop any of these models get.

Two things in the repository are doing work that's easy to miss:

The artifact is committed

rf_eight_best.joblib is in the repository, not in a .gitignore. The deployed app loads it and never trains. Git stores a fresh copy on every change, acceptable for a forest this size and not acceptable for long.

The versions are pinned

Unpinned, requirements.txt resolves to whatever is newest on build day, and an app that worked in March fails in June because a library changed a default. A model pickled by one scikit-learn version and loaded by another may warn, or fail outright.

08 — Failure modes

What goes wrong

Four failure modes. The first is the one to spend time on, because it produces confident wrong answers rather than an error message.

1Feature order skew▸

The model was fitted on a DataFrame whose columns had names and an order. Serve it a plain array, and scikit-learn can't check the names — it assumes the values arrive in fitted order and predicts on whatever it's given. For image data this isn't hypothetical: an 8×8 image is stored as 64 numbers, and whether you flatten it row by row or column by column is a choice somebody's code made once and nobody wrote down. NumPy will do either, the arrays are the same shape, and the model accepts both.

correct orientation → "an eight"
flattened the other way → "not an eight"

Same pixels, same dtype, same shape — transposed. No exception, no warning in the UI. The model finds not a single eight, and nothing anywhere reports a problem.

This is the failure mode worth carrying out of this session, because of how it presents. It doesn't look like a bug — it looks like the model being worse than you thought, and teams have spent weeks investigating it as a data-quality problem before someone checked the serving code.

The fix costs one line: pass a DataFrame with named columns, and scikit-learn checks them for you.

>>> final_model.predict(pd.DataFrame([transposed_row], columns=mislabelled))
ValueError: The feature names should match those that were passed during fit.

That's why app.py has to_row, why the feature order is written into model_meta.json, and why a served model should be handed named columns rather than a positional array. The check costs nothing and converts a silent, weeks-long failure into an exception on the first request.

2Recomputing everything on every interaction▸

The default state of a Streamlit app is that every user interaction reloads the data and reloads the model, because the script reruns from line one. Survivable on a small forest, fatal on anything real — a 200 MB model reloaded from disk on every click.

The symptom is an app that gets slower the more the user interacts with it. The fix is @st.cache_resource on anything expensive that's read-only, and @st.cache_data on the frames.

3The artifact that is not in the repository▸

The single most common Streamlit Cloud failure. The app works locally, the deploy goes green, and the app crashes on first load with FileNotFoundError: rf_eight_best.joblib. Almost always the .joblib was produced locally and never committed, or was caught by a .gitignore rule meant for something else. Check with git ls-files rather than ls — the question is what the repository contains, not what your laptop contains.

4A search that stopped early▸

Thirty trials over a four-parameter space is not a thorough search. It's a sample. If plot_optimization_history still slopes upward at the right edge, the study hadn't converged and the "best parameters" are simply the best of a short look. Report the trial budget alongside the result — a best value without a trial count isn't interpretable.

CHECKPOINT 8.1

Reproduce the feature-order bug from the serving side, which is where you'll actually meet it.

  1. Run app.py and review a case in Review cases mode. Record what the model says and its confidence.
  2. Edit to_row to transpose the pixel array before building the DataFrame, simulating the same bug from the app's entry point.
09 — Context

Where this sits in the lifecycle

Both tools attach to the experimentation stage of the lifecycle, and both have a clear boundary where something else takes over.

Training produces an artifact, and serving consumes it. That separation is the structural idea the rest of the course builds on.

Optuna searches. It does not remember.

A study holds every trial while it exists, and when the kernel restarts you have hyperparameters and no context — no record of which dataset version they were found on, which commit produced the training script, or what the resulting model scored in production. "These parameters gave 0.96" is only useful alongside the data and code that produced it. Storing that association is experiment tracking, the next thing this course builds.

Streamlit serves a human. It is not a serving layer for software.

A Streamlit app talks to a browser and holds per-session state. Another service that needs predictions wants an HTTP endpoint with a schema, a latency budget, and no UI. The two coexist — the API serves the product, and the Streamlit app gives humans a window onto the same model. A Streamlit app is still not the thing you put behind a load balancer.

What today changed about the workflow is narrower than it looks, and worth stating plainly: the model is no longer the last artifact you produce. There's now a .joblib file that something else loads, a model_meta.json that records what it expects, and a repository whose contents determine what is deployed.

10 — Class summary

A model that works is not finished

You could already search a hyperparameter space. What Optuna adds is a sampler that uses its own history, a space that can branch, and a study that outlives the process. On this problem it reached a better configuration than an exhaustive grid while fitting a third as many models. The API is small, and you'll have it in an afternoon. The honest caveat travels with it: for a dozen combinations searched once, GridSearchCV is still the right answer.

The judgment is the part that lasts, and this session measured three pieces of it.

What you optimize for decides everything

On a problem where one image in ten is positive, a model that finds nothing scores about 90% accuracy. The default forest had perfect precision and missed a third of the eights. Neither fact is visible if accuracy is the number on the dashboard, and no amount of searching fixes a metric pointed at the wrong thing.

A search can only find what is in the space you gave it

Tuning beat the default on every split, and with class_weight removed from the search space the same sampler with the same budget stopped winning. The gain was never TPE being clever — the space contained the one setting that addressed the failure the baseline actually had, known because the classification report was read before choosing what to search over.

The number you optimize is not an estimate of anything

A search pointed at the holdout reported a perfect score and delivered considerably less, while also selecting a worse configuration than the honest run. A metric that comes back at 1.0 is a symptom to investigate, not a result to report.

Streamlit closes the other gap, and the whole of it follows from one sentence: the script reruns top to bottom on every interaction. Layout is execution order because of it, caching exists because of it, and session state exists because of it.
