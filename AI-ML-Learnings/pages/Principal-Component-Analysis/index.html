Before we start

Why this comes right after clustering

GMM and DBSCAN both asked "which points belong together?" PCA asks a different question first: can we even see the data well enough to tell?

High-dimensional data reduces down to a few informative dimensions, which is what makes structure and clusters easier to visualize and interpret in the first place. It's the step that comes before — or alongside — clustering, not instead of it.

Rohit wants one chart, for a wall screen, showing whether biopsy samples cluster into benign and malignant. That's the brief we'll follow through the rest of this piece.

01 · The problem

The wall screen that can't be drawn

Meera's dataset has 569 patients and 30 tumour measurements per patient. Her first instinct is to plot pairs of columns and see what turns up.

Column 1 against column 2. Fine. Column 1 against column 3. Also fine. Then the arithmetic catches up with her: with 30 columns, there are 435 possible two-column scatter plots. Nowhere near a wall screen's worth.

Her first instinct is that this is just tedious. It's worse than tedious — the extra columns are actively working against her. And here is the frame for everything that follows: the goal isn't to model anything. It's to see.

The curse of dimensionality

Here's the idea everything else rests on: as the number of dimensions grows, everything becomes equally far from everything else. Distance stops discriminating between points.

Picture a friend who is exactly one kilometre away. On a road, that's trivial to search — walk either direction and you'll find them. Put that same friend, same one kilometre away, in a one-kilometre square field, and now you have an area to search instead of a line. Put them in a one-kilometre cube — a multi-storey car park — and it's nearly hopeless to search by feel. Nothing about the friend changed. Nothing about the distance changed. Only the space around them got emptier, level by level.

That's what happens inside NovaCare's 30-dimensional dataset. The consequences are concrete: data gets sparse, compute cost inflates, and models start memorising noise instead of pattern — which shows up later as poor generalisation to new patients.

Ratio of farthest / nearest pairwise distance, 200 random points per dimension
Hover a point for the exact ratio at that dimension count.

At 2 dimensions the ratio is 217 — some points are dramatically closer than others, so distance is a meaningful signal. By 100 dimensions it has collapsed to 1.6. By 300, it's 1.32: the nearest and farthest points are almost equally distant. This is why distance-based methods like k-NN and clustering lose their footing as columns pile up.

So more columns cost something real, measurably. The obvious fix is to delete some of them. But which ones does Meera drop? She can't tell a pathologist she dropped concavity on a hunch. What's needed is a principled way to shrink the data — and deleting columns turns out not to be the only option.

02 · What PCA actually promises

Selecting vs. combining

There are two fundamentally different moves available here, and separating them cleanly is the conceptual hinge for everything that follows.

Not this Selecting

Keep 2 of the 30 original columns. Throw the other 28 away entirely, along with anything useful they carried.

PCA does this Combining

Build 2 brand-new columns, each one a blend that carries a little bit of information from all 30 original columns.

PCA doesn't pick favourites among existing columns — it builds new ones.

Think of the 30-column dataset as a 1,000-page book. Selecting is tearing out three pages at random and calling that a summary. Combining — what PCA does — is writing an actual 3-page summary. You lose the subplots. You keep the plot. And the detail that matters: you cannot write that summary without reading all 1,000 pages first. PCA reads every column before it compresses anything. Nothing is ignored on the way in — only on the way out.

Be honest about the trade being made here, because a manager like Rohit will ask: this is trading some accuracy for a large gain in simplicity, deliberately. That's not a flaw to hide — it's the whole point, and the next few sections work out exactly how to quantify what gets given up.

Check yourself

Meera drops 28 of the 30 columns and keeps only the two with the widest numeric range. What has she done that PCA would not have done?

Why: This is selection, not combination — it discards the 28 dropped columns entirely, even if they held useful shared variance. PCA reads all 30 columns and builds new axes from combinations of them. Numeric range alone also has nothing to do with how much true variance a column carries, which is the trap this question sets.

03 · Variance is information

If it never changes, it can't tell you anything

Consider a diagnostic question: if a column has the exact same value for every single patient, is it useful?

display — 02 flat feature vs. spread feature
A column that never changes carries zero information.

The feature on the left never changes — every patient lands at the same value. It cannot possibly help tell one patient apart from another: zero variance, zero information. The feature on the right spreads out, and that spread is exactly where the signal lives.

Push that one step further and the entire objective of PCA falls out, before any maths: if flat is useless and spread is useful, then spread — variance — is the thing worth preserving. That's the whole goal.

The cars example

Take two features: engine power and max speed. Plot the cars, then project every point onto just the power axis — collapse it to 1D. Do the same onto just the max-speed axis. Max speed keeps more spread — it "wins" — but look at how much gets lost either way.

display — 03 projecting onto a single existing axis
Both single-axis views throw information away — one just throws away less.

Both of those axes are bad choices. But notice — the choice was only ever between the axes already on hand. What if, instead of picking one of the axes we were handed, we could draw a brand-new one, angled exactly to catch the most spread? That question — can we draw our own axis — is the entire idea of a principal component.

04 · Building PC1 by hand

Rotate a line through the cloud

PC1 is the direction in which the data varies the most. If this 2D cloud had to be represented with just one line, PC1 is the line that preserves the maximum possible spread — and therefore loses the least information.

Take that same cloud of points and rotate a candidate line through it, like a clock hand sweeping across the data. For every angle, drop a perpendicular from each point onto the line — that's the projection of each point onto that candidate direction.

Pick any single point. Its distance from the origin, call it A, is fixed — it doesn't change as the line rotates. But A splits into two pieces depending on the line's angle: the part that lands on the line (the projection) and the part left over, perpendicular to the line (the error). By Pythagoras:

A2 = projection2 + error2

Since A is constant, the two pieces trade off exactly. Maximise the projected part, and the leftover error part is automatically minimised. So finding the direction that captures the most spread is exactly the same as finding the direction that loses the least information — the candidate line with the largest total squared projected distance is PC1.

Rotate to roughly 0° and the line is nearly horizontal — a low score. Rotate to roughly 90° and it's nearly vertical — also low. Somewhere around a third of the way through the sweep, angled right through the long axis of the cloud, the score peaks by a wide margin. That angle is PC1.

Name what was just built properly, because it matters for everything that follows: PC1 is not a column you already had. It's a linear combination — a recipe, something like 0.81 × power + 0.59 × max_speed. A new column, built out of old ones. This is the book analogy's 3-page summary, and it only exists because both original pages — both axes — were read to build it.

Check yourself

Two candidate lines are drawn through the same cloud of points. Line A gives a larger sum of squared projected distances than Line B. What is simultaneously true of Line A?

Why: Because each point's total distance from the origin is fixed, projected distance and perpendicular error trade off exactly by Pythagoras. A line that maximises projected spread simultaneously minimises leftover error — they're the same optimum, not two separate ones to balance. Perpendicularity describes the relationship between PC1 and PC2, not between two arbitrary candidate lines.

05 · PC2 and orthogonality

What's left over, at a right angle

PC1 gives the single direction that captures the most variation. But the original data was 2D, and reducing it to just PC1 throws some information away. What happens to the variation PC1 couldn't capture?

That's the job of PC2: the direction that captures the maximum remaining variation after PC1 has taken as much as it can. But PC2 can't point in any direction it likes — it must be perpendicular (orthogonal) to PC1.

If PC2 pointed even partly along PC1, it would capture some of the same variation PC1 already captured, and the two components would overlap in what they represent. Forcing PC2 to be perpendicular guarantees it captures a genuinely new, non-redundant direction — exactly the variation PC1 missed. In two dimensions, once PC1 is fixed, there is only one perpendicular direction left (up to sign). That direction becomes PC2.

PC2

Captures the maximum remaining variation, while staying perpendicular to PC1.

Together, PC1 and PC2 form a new coordinate system for the data, with axes ordered by how much variation they explain. In two dimensions there's only one possible choice for PC2 once PC1 is fixed. In NovaCare's real 30-dimensional dataset there are 30 possible components — each one forced to be perpendicular to every component before it, and each one capturing strictly less variance than the one before.

06 · Variance explained

Turning PCA into a number Rohit can use

Each principal component captures a certain amount of the data's variation. Here's how that turns into a simple percentage a manager can act on.

Variance captured by each component

Divide the sum of squared projections by (n − 1). Say there are 31 patients, so n − 1 = 30.

PC1: 300 / 30 = 10     PC2: 60 / 30 = 2

Total variance

Add the components together.

10 + 2 = 12

Explained variance ratio

Each component's variance, divided by the total.

PC1: 10 / 12 ≈ 83%     PC2: 2 / 12 ≈ 17%

Instead of saying "we reduced the data to one dimension," Meera can now tell Rohit something specific and defensible:

"Our one-dimensional representation using PC1 preserves 83% of the original variation in the data."

That's the payoff of explained variance: it tells you how much information survives when you keep a given number of principal components.

07 · Reading a scree plot

How many components are worth keeping

With more than two features, PCA hands back several principal components — usually more than you need. A scree plot helps decide how many are worth keeping.

The bars

Variance explained by each individual component. PC1 captures the most, PC2 the next most, and PC3 onward generally contribute less and less.

The line

Cumulative variance explained. It answers: "if I keep the first k components, how much of the original variation have I preserved?"

For example, if the cumulative line reaches 90% at PC3, keeping PC1, PC2, and PC3 preserves about 90% of the variation in the original data.

The elbow

Read the bars left to right. Initially there's a large drop in variance explained; eventually the bars shrink and flatten out. That bend is the elbow. Before it, each additional component adds meaningful new information. After it, additional components add relatively little.

This is why the scree plot earns its keep in dimensionality reduction: it turns "how many dimensions should I keep" into something you can actually see and reason about, rather than guess at.

Check yourself

In a dataset, PC1 explains 62% of the variance, PC2 explains 20%, and PC3 explains 9%. What does the scree plot's cumulative line read at the PC3 bar?

Why: The cumulative line sums each component's individual share moving left to right: 62 + 20 + 9 = 91%. A common mix-up is reading the individual bar height at PC3 (9%) instead of the running total — the cumulative line and the bar chart show two different things on the same axes.

How many components are enough

Picture a dataset with 100 columns where the components explain 80%, then 10%, then 1%, then 0.6%, trailing off. How many do you keep? There is no single correct answer — that's the point. This is a business decision about acceptable loss, not a maths problem with one right solution.

01

Look for the elbow

The point where the cumulative line stops climbing steeply and flattens out. Keep components up to that bend.

02

Set a fixed threshold

Decide up front how much information you're willing to lose — commonly a 95% cumulative variance target — and keep however many components it takes to reach it.

For Meera, the decision is forced by the medium: she needs a chart for a wall screen, so she's taking exactly two components, whatever the cost. Her job isn't to find the "best" number — it's to report honestly what those two components cost her in lost information.

08 · Case study

The tumour triage board

Everything built by hand up to this point becomes about eight lines of code on the real data: 569 patients, 30 tumour measurements, from the Wisconsin breast cancer dataset.

Load and clean

Drop columns that don't describe the tumour itself — an ID column, say, or an empty column. An identifier is just a label for a patient; it has no meaningful relationship to tumour characteristics and therefore no useful variation for PCA to discover. After cleaning, 30 numerical tumour measurements remain.

Separate features from the diagnosis

Split the data into X — the 30 tumour measurements PCA will actually analyse — and y, the benign/malignant label. y stays completely out of the process because PCA is unsupervised: it never sees the diagnosis, only the patterns and variation inside X.

Standardise the features

The most important step, and the most common place to make a mistake. The 30 measurements aren't on the same scale — area runs into the hundreds or thousands, smoothness sits around 0.1. Because PCA is built on variance, an unscaled feature with a larger numeric spread can dominate the calculation regardless of its real-world importance.

z = (x − μ) / σ

After standardising, every feature has mean ≈ 0 and standard deviation ≈ 1, so PCA compares features fairly and finds directions of genuine variation — not directions dominated by measurement units.

Check yourself

Meera skips StandardScaler and runs PCA directly on the raw data. The area column, with values in the hundreds, ends up dominating PC1. Why does this happen?

Why: PCA has no concept of medical importance — only numeric variance. A column measured in the hundreds will show far larger raw spread than one measured in the hundredths, regardless of which one actually distinguishes benign from malignant tissue. Scaling puts every feature on the same footing before variance is compared; without it, units — not biology — decide the outcome.

The pipeline, in code

Everything above — load, split, scale — sets up the one line that actually does the reduction:

pipeline scikit-learn 
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

pca = PCA(n_components=2)
X2  = pca.fit_transform(X_scaled)

evr = pca.explained_variance_ratio_
print(f"PC1 explains {evr[0]*100:.1f}% of variance")
print(f"PC2 explains {evr[1]*100:.1f}% of variance")
The entire dimensionality-reduction step — because everything before it did the real work.
