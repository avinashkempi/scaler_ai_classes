Gaussian Mixture Models

Teaching a clustering algorithm to say "maybe."

The brief — Veloura, Growth & Marketing Analytics

"We have ~4–5 years of customer purchase data across ~40,000 customers, 12 behavioral features. We want to stop blasting the same discount email to everyone — segment customers so we can personalize campaigns."

You're the analyst on the Growth/Marketing Analytics team at Veloura, a mid-size online fashion & lifestyle retailer. Last time, you reached for Hierarchical Clustering — the tool you already knew. This is where it breaks, and what you reach for instead.

01Story setup

Recap: Hierarchical Clustering, and where it left off

Marketing wants to stop emailing every one of Veloura's ~40,000 customers the same 20%-off code. Last session's tool was Hierarchical Clustering — it doesn't need a pre-picked number of clusters, and it gives a dendrogram to explore structure at different granularities.

Quick recap

  • Agglomerative hierarchical clustering starts with every customer as their own cluster, and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until everything is one cluster.
  • A dendrogram records the order and distance of every merge — cutting it at a given height gives a specific number of clusters.
  • No need to decide k up front — that decision comes after looking at the dendrogram.
02The breaking point

Where Hierarchical Clustering breaks for Veloura

That flexibility looked great on 60 customers. Veloura has ~40,000. Two problems show up fast.

Problem 1 — Scale

Hierarchical clustering has to track distances between every pair of clusters as it merges. On 40,000 customers, that's on the order of ~800 million pairwise distances just to start.

At Veloura's real scale (~40,000 customers) this curve keeps climbing — both compute time and the memory needed to hold the full distance matrix.

Problem 2 — Rigid, hard-boundary assignment

Imagine a customer who spends a lot and always waits for a discount code. They sit right at the boundary between two segments. Hierarchical clustering still has to drop them fully into exactly one branch — there's no way to say "this customer is kind of both."

Two real-looking Veloura behaviors overlap in feature space: "weekend browsers" (moderate spend, come back often just to browse) and "deal hunters" (lower spend, high frequency, but only when there's a discount).

The core idea

Hard clustering: every point gets assigned to exactly one cluster, no matter how ambiguous its location is. Hierarchical Clustering and KMeans are both hard clustering methods — the assignment step gives a single, final label with no notion of "maybe." That rigidity, combined with the scale problem, is exactly the gap the rest of this article closes.

Check yourself · Q1

A Veloura customer spends a lot and always waits for discount codes. Under a hard clustering method like Hierarchical Clustering or K-Means, how would this customer be treated?

Hard clustering assigns every point to a single nearest cluster; there's no mechanism to express partial membership — which is exactly the gap GMM is built to close.
03The missing idea

Soft clustering

Remember the customer from Section 2 who spends a lot and waits for discounts? Here's the fix: instead of forcing them into one bucket, give every customer a probability of belonging to each segment.

That's soft clustering: every point gets a distribution over clusters instead of a single hard label. So — how do we actually produce these probabilities in a principled way? That's where the Gaussian distribution comes in.

04Building block

Gaussian distributions — 1D

Why a Gaussian, specifically? Most natural, continuous behavioral metrics — like a customer's average order value — cluster around some typical value and taper off symmetrically on either side. Most customers spend "around normal," fewer spend way more or way less. That empirical shape is a bell curve: the Gaussian (Normal) distribution.

Key properties: mean = median = mode, and it's symmetric around the mean.

f(x | μ, σ) = (1 / σ√2π) · e −(x−μ)² / 2σ²
  • μ (mean) — where the peak sits
  • σ (standard deviation) — how spread out the curve is

05Building block, continued

Gaussian distributions — 2D

Veloura customers aren't described by one number — spend, frequency, recency, and more, all at once. So we need the multivariate version of the Gaussian: instead of a curve, a surface over a 2D plane (say, "avg order value" × "visit frequency").

Why does the ellipse tilt for real customers?

With just a mean and variance per axis, we only get axis-aligned ellipses — that assumes spend and frequency are independent. In reality they're often correlated: deal hunters tend to have low spend and high frequency together. That correlation is exactly what covariance captures — the extra parameter that lets the ellipse rotate to match how two features actually move together.

Interactive · Signature

Weave the correlation in

Drag the correlation slider and watch the same two Gaussians rotate and stretch — this is the geometry that lets GMM tell "weekend browsers" apart from "deal hunters."

  • No correlation: contours are axis-aligned — average order value and visit frequency are independent.
  • Positive correlation: contours tilt upward — customers who spend more also tend to visit more frequently.
  • Negative correlation: contours tilt downward — customers who spend more may visit less often (deal-hunter pattern).
  • The amount and direction of tilt is controlled by the covariance (correlation) term in the covariance matrix. Modeling this lets a Gaussian capture real customer behavior instead of assuming every feature varies independently.
Check yourself · Q2

In a 2D Gaussian, why do all points on the same contour ring have the same likelihood?

A contour ring is a horizontal slice through the 3D probability surface, so by construction every point on it has identical height (likelihood) — the ring is defined by constant probability density, not by geometry alone.
06Putting it together

Gaussian Mixture Model — intuition

Back at Veloura

Remember "weekend browsers" vs "deal hunters" overlapping in Section 2? Here's the fix: instead of fitting one Gaussian to everything, fit multiple Gaussians — one per segment — and let every customer get a likelihood score under each Gaussian.

The core idea, in one line: the more likely a point is under a given Gaussian, the more it belongs to that segment. That's the intuitive definition — Expectation-Maximization (next section) is what makes it mathematically rigorous.

One constraint carries over from KMeans: you still have to choose k (the number of Gaussians / segments) up front — typically from domain knowledge, e.g. "Marketing works with 4 tiers today." A familiar constraint, not a new one.

07The engine underneath

Expectation–Maximization (EM)

So how do we actually fit k Gaussians to Veloura's customers — finding the best μ, σ, and mixing weight for each segment? EM, in four steps.

Step-by-step

Walk through EM

Click a step to read it — the algorithm repeats steps 2 and 3 until convergence.

1 · Initialization

Pick k random customers as the initial means, and initialize each Gaussian's variance from the spread of the sampled data. This is a reasonable starting guess — not arbitrary — because real customers are more representative starting points than random numbers.

2 · Expectation (E) step

For every customer, compute their likelihood under each Gaussian, then normalize those likelihoods into probabilities that sum to 1 across segments.

Why normalize? Think of it like: "given it's raining, what's the probability you're in Delhi vs. New York?" You don't just look at how likely rain is in Delhi alone — you compare it against how likely rain is in Delhi relative to New York. A customer's raw likelihood under Segment A only matters relative to their likelihood under Segments B, C, and so on.

responsibilityi,k = Pk(xi) / (P1(xi) + P2(xi) + ⋯ + Pk(xi))
Live visualizer — responsibility calculation Open in new tab ↗

3 · Maximization (M) step

Update each Gaussian's μ and σ as the likelihood-weighted average of all customers. Customers who are more confidently in a segment (higher responsibility) pull that segment's mean toward them more strongly — a weighted average, not a hard nearest-point average like KMeans' centroid update.

4 · Repeat E and M

Repeat the Expectation and Maximization steps until the parameters stop changing much — convergence.

Check yourself · Q3

In the Maximization step of EM, how is a Gaussian's mean updated?

Because the Expectation step gave every point a soft membership weight per Gaussian, Maximization uses those weights to pull the mean toward points proportional to how confidently they belong — not a hard nearest-point average like KMeans' centroid update.

KMeans is secretly a special case of GMM

Force every Gaussian to have equal variance in every direction and zero covariance (perfectly circular, same size), and a GMM's soft elliptical clusters collapse into KMeans' hard circular ones. Three differently-shaped synthetic blob sets make this concrete.

  • With little or no correlation, GMM learns nearly circular Gaussians, so its behavior looks similar to KMeans.
  • As correlation increases, GMM rotates and stretches its Gaussian components to match the data.
  • KMeans still partitions space based only on distance to centroids and cannot model tilted clusters.
Check yourself · Q4

What is the core structural difference between K-Means and GMM?

Both require k upfront; the real distinction is the assignment mechanism — nearest-centroid hard labeling vs. likelihood-based soft membership.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  • Hierarchical breaks at Veloura's scale and forces hard assignment — no way to express an ambiguous customer.
  • Soft clustering = a probability of membership per segment, not a single forced label.
  • Gaussians (1D → 2D, with covariance) are the mathematical building block — covariance is what lets clusters tilt to match real, correlated customer behavior.
  • GMM = fit k Gaussians, and use EM to iteratively estimate their parameters — the E-step scores every customer against every Gaussian and normalizes; the M-step updates each Gaussian toward the customers most confidently assigned to it.
  • GMM ⊃ KMeans: KMeans is what you get when every Gaussian is forced to be a same-size circle.
