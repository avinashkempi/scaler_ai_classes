From a smudge to a story — how two neighbourhood-preserving methods pick up exactly where PCA runs out of road.

PCA: everything blurs into one smear.

Before we start

What PCA already gave us

Meera has already met PCA. It compresses a dataset by finding new axes — directions that capture as much of the overall spread as possible — instead of simply dropping columns.

High dimensions make visualisation hard, and distances between points become less meaningful the more features you add.

PCA combines features into new axes rather than deleting any of the original columns.

PC1 captures the direction of maximum variance; PC2 captures the next most, perpendicular to PC1.

Variance explained tells you how much information each component retains — and a scree plot helps decide how many components are "enough."

Applied to a 30-column medical dataset, PCA compressed everything down to a single 2D chart. The result was a smudge, not a clean split.

The problem

A new dataset, and a new problem

Meera tries PCA again — this time on 1,797 scanned handwritten digits from NovaCare's requisition slips. Each image becomes a row of pixel features, and PCA reduces those many dimensions down to two so the digits can be plotted.

The result is encouraging at first: the 3s and 4s separate nicely. But the 1s, 7s, 8s, and 9s pile on top of each other, forming a single central smear.

PCA has preserved the directions with the most overall variation — but that isn't the same thing as preserving the local relationships between similar examples.

Meera's notes

"Straight lines aren't enough. I need something that cares about neighbours."

Key question

What if, instead of only asking which directions capture the most variation, we also cared about which points are close to each other?

That question is the doorway into neighbourhood-based dimensionality reduction — the family of methods this entire read is about.

Part I

1Where PCA stops working

Before building anything new, it's worth being precise about why PCA produced a smudge instead of an answer. Four limitations, each one tied to something already familiar about how PCA works.

PC1 has no name. A linear combination of thirty original features resists being summarised in a word — you can't point at PC1 and say "that's texture" or "that's ink pressure."

PCA can only rotate and stretch a straight axis through the data. It draws straight lines, full stop. If the true structure in the data is curved, a straight axis simply cannot follow it round the bend.This is the one that matters most today

Get scaling wrong, and PCA reports back your units, not your biology — or your handwriting.

A single wild record can drag PC1 toward itself, because outliers manufacture enormous variance — and variance is exactly the thing PCA is chasing.

Limitation 1.2 is the direct bridge into everything that follows. Rather than just stating it, let's build a dataset that only makes sense when you follow it around a curve, then watch PCA fail at exactly that job.

Seeing the limit

The Swiss roll

Picture a flat, continuous sheet of data that has been rolled up into a spiral in 3D space — a "Swiss roll." Colour marks each point's true position along that unrolled sheet, from one end to the other.

49.3%variance explained by PC1
44.9%variance explained by PC2

The colour ordering is still partly preserved, so PCA hasn't completely failed — but the geometry has changed. That's the limitation that matters: PCA chooses the flat plane that preserves the most overall variance, without knowing the data is actually a rolled-up surface.

Two different questions

PCA asks: "Which flat directions capture the most overall variation?"

We now want to ask: "Which points are neighbours on the underlying structure, and can we keep those neighbourhoods together?"

The key distinction

PCA preserves global variance. It does not explicitly preserve local neighbourhood relationships. That's the motivation for the method that follows: instead of hunting for the best straight-line projection, we'll look for a way to preserve the local geometry of the data.

Part II · How t-SNE thinks

2Decoding t-SNE

t-SNE comes from Van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008. The name tells you the whole plan, if you take it apart:

PieceMeaning
StochasticWe work in probabilities, not fixed distances.
NeighborWe only care about local company.
EmbeddingWe place points into a low-dimensional space.
t-distributedParked for now — we'll earn the t properly a bit later.

The strategy, before any maths: measure neighbourliness in 64D, throw points down randomly in 2D, then shove them around until the 2D neighbourliness matches the 64D neighbourliness.

Analogy · the party re-seating problem

You know who talks to whom in a big hall. Now you have to re-seat everyone at one long table so the same people are still within earshot. You cannot preserve every distance. You can preserve the conversations.

Part II

3Turning distance into probability

t-SNE is trying to preserve neighbourhoods — but that raises an immediate question. Our data lives in 64 dimensions, so we need a way to turn distances between points into something t-SNE can reason about as neighbourliness.

Key question

How do we decide, mathematically, whether one point is a good neighbour of another?

Raw distance isn't enough — a distance that feels small in one part of the dataset might be large in another. Instead of asking "How far away is this point?", t-SNE asks "How likely is this point to be one of my neighbours?" That's the bridge from intuition to mathematics, built here as a sequence of rejected attempts. The failures are what make the final formula obvious.

Attempt 1 — raw distance

Fails immediately: there's no upper bound, and no natural way to compare what "near" means for different points.

Attempt 2 — 1 / distance²

A better direction — close points get a big number, distant points get a small number. But it explodes as distance approaches zero, and its decay is too abrupt at the extremes.

Attempt 3 — a Gaussian centred on the point

This is the one that survives. Bounded, smooth, falls off gracefully — only the positive side of the curve matters, since distance can't be negative. Pick a point, build a bell curve centred on it, and every other point's height on that curve becomes its probability of being picked as a neighbour. Normalise so each row sums to 1, and you have a genuine probability distribution — call it Pij.

Why every point needs its own bell curve

Two points at identical distance from their respective centres don't mean the same thing. In a dense cluster, that distance describes a stranger. In a sparse region, that same distance describes a close friend — distance means different things in different neighbourhoods. So every point gets its own σᵢ (standard deviation), derived from the spread of its nearest neighbours: a dense region gets a small σ and a narrow curve; a sparse region gets a large σ and a wide curve.

Analogy

Living 5 km from your nearest neighbour means something completely different in a dense city than it does in a remote mountain village.

Perplexity

The number of neighbours to consider is a hyperparameter called perplexity — the paper calls it "the effective number of neighbours." σᵢ is solved so that each point's curve achieves that perplexity. Low perplexity favours local detail; high perplexity favours the big picture. The paper suggests a range of 5–50, with a warning: never set it near the total number of data points.

Part II

4Low dimension — random start, then push

We now have a similarity matrix in high dimensions — every point measured against every other, using each point's own Gaussian. Call this S1.

Now the low-dimensional side. These probabilities are called Qij, and two things differ here: low-dimensional data is already normalised by construction, so no σ is needed, and it uses a different distribution than the Gaussian — the second time we've dodged the t. Points start scattered randomly in 2D space, and a second similarity matrix, S2, is built from those random positions.

t-SNE's job is simple to state: move the points in 2D until S2 looks as much like S1 as possible. The rest of the algorithm is about doing that efficiently.

A question to sit with

Two points sit 5 cm apart in 3D. We move them into 2D. What happens to that 5 cm — and is there enough room for everyone else to keep their distances too?

Part II

5Crowding, and finally earning the t

The volume argument: imagine a million points packed into a cube of side a. Flatten that into a square of side a. To hold the same number of points, the 2D space has to stretch — pairs that were 5 cm apart in 3D end up much further apart once squeezed into 2D. The low-dimensional space is simply too cramped for everything to fit at honest distances.

Formally: it is impossible to preserve all neighbourhood distances when mapping high dimensions down to low ones. This is the crowding problem, and plain SNE — t-SNE's predecessor — suffered badly from it.

The fix, as a design decision: if we can't fit everyone at true distance, deliberately push the far points further away to free up room in the middle for the near ones. That calls for a low-dimensional distribution with a fatter, taller tail than the Gaussian — enter the t-distribution with 1 degree of freedom, the fattest tail in the family. The t is finally earned.

Part II

6Measuring the gap — KL divergence & gradient descent

"Make S2 match S1" is a goal, not a loss function. We need a single number — something quantitative and differentiable that an optimiser can push down, step by step, rather than something we simply eyeball.

KL divergence is that number: a measure of how far distribution Q (low-D) sits from distribution P (high-D). Zero when they're identical, larger as they diverge.

We minimise it with gradient descent — with one twist worth flagging. Normally gradient descent learns model weights. Here, the learnable parameters are the 2D coordinates of the points themselves. Every point's (x, y) position is a parameter being optimised. With n points, that means optimising 2n numbers simultaneously, and every step touches every pair — a large part of why this is expensive.

Part III

8What t-SNE costs

Three problems, each framed through Meera's actual workflow:

01

No reusable transform

PCA gave her a recipe she could apply to next month's patients. t-SNE gives her a picture of these 1,797 slips only — tomorrow's batch means starting over from scratch.

02

Slow

Two n×n matrices, and a summation in every gradient step. 1,797 points is fine. Rohit is talking about 200,000 slips.

03

Non-deterministic

Run it twice, get two different pictures — awkward when a pathologist asks why last week's chart looked different.

Meera's conclusion: the idea is right. The implementation is too expensive at the scale she actually needs — which is exactly the gap UMAP was built to close, in 2018. Same goal — preserve neighbourhoods. Completely different machinery underneath.

Part IV · UMAP

9UMAP — the graph idea and its two dials

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Its mathematical foundation is algebraic topology — worth naming, not worth deriving. We move straight past it to the intuition that actually matters.

The key structural difference from t-SNE: t-SNE computes every pairwise distance — a dense n×n matrix. UMAP connects each point only to its n_neighbors nearest points, builds a weighted graph, and finds a low-dimensional layout where that graph's edge weights are preserved. Sparse instead of dense — that alone accounts for most of the speed-up.

n_neighbors — the local / global dial

Low values

The map fragments into local shards; global shape becomes meaningless.

High values

Global structure is preserved, but fine local detail is lost.

min_dist — how tightly points may pack

Low

Dense, clumpy clusters.

High

Spread out, more even.

This dial is purely a cosmetic-but-consequential control over the final layout, not the neighbour graph itself.

Part IV

10UMAP's loss, and why it's fast

UMAP minimises a cross-entropy loss over the graph's edges.

First half

Matters when the high-D edge weight is large — a real neighbour. It forces the low-D edge weight to also be large, pulling true neighbours together.

Second half

Matters when the high-D edge weight is small — not a neighbour. The term grows large, forcing the low-D weight small, pushing non-neighbours apart.

The consequence — and the reason UMAP beats t-SNE on structure: it optimises both the attractive and repulsive terms, so it preserves small and large distances at the same time. t-SNE effectively concentrates on the local ones. That's why UMAP tends to keep global structure that t-SNE loses.

Speed summary: a sparse neighbour graph instead of all-pairs distances, plus this two-sided loss, adds up to dramatically less work per optimisation step.

Part IV

11Running UMAP, and the fine print

0.01sPCA
2.14st-SNE
0.81sUMAP

The UMAP plot shows tighter, better-separated clusters than t-SNE, with previously-overlapping groups now cleanly split — because global structure is preserved alongside local structure. t-SNE at a very high perplexity can approach UMAP's global fidelity, but the compute cost for that is punishing.

Now the honest part — things that will embarrass you in a review if you skip them
  1. 1Hyperparameters genuinely matter. Expect several attempts before you trust a layout.
  2. 2Cluster sizes are not meaningful. A big blob is not a big group.
  3. 3Distances between clusters are not meaningful. Two adjacent clusters are not necessarily similar.
  4. 4Random noise can produce apparent clusters. You can hallucinate structure in nothing.
  5. 5Same hyperparameters do not guarantee the same plot. Produce more than one before you believe any of them.

Neither "A has twice as many members," "A and B are dissimilar," nor "the distance between them is meaningful" is a safe read in t-SNE or UMAP. The only safe read: A and B are two distinct groups.

Summary

Key takeaways

Why PCA wasn't enough

  • Linear projections collapse curved structure — that's why PCA failed on the digits and the Swiss roll.
  • PCA preserves global variance, not local neighbourhood relationships.

How t-SNE thinks

  • Reframes the goal as preserving neighbourhoods, not spread.
  • Distance becomes a Gaussian-based probability; each point's own σ accounts for local density (perplexity).
  • A random low-dimensional layout is pushed, step by step, to match the high-dimensional similarity matrix.
  • Crowding is unavoidable going from high-D to low-D; a t-distribution's fat tail buys back room.
  • KL divergence is the loss; the point coordinates themselves are the parameters being optimised.

t-SNE's real-world costs

  • No reusable transform — every new batch starts from scratch.
  • Slow — dense n×n matrices at every gradient step.
  • Non-deterministic — two runs can produce two different pictures.

UMAP's answer

  • Swaps all-pairs distances for a sparse n_neighbors graph — much faster.
  • Its loss pulls true neighbours together and pushes non-neighbours apart, preserving both local and global structure.

Reading either plot safely

  • Cluster size is not reliable.
  • Distance between clusters is not reliable.
  • Apparent structure from noise is possible in either method.

The one-line version

  • PCA compresses. t-SNE and UMAP visualise. Different tools, for different jobs.
