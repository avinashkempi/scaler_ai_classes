Section 1 · 5 minYou already built the crudest possible version

Two classes ago you built a summariser. Look at what it actually does.

It fetches a page, takes the whole thing, and pastes it into the prompt. Every word. Then it asks a question about it.

That is retrieval. Specifically, it is retrieval where you retrieve everything and hope the answer is in there.

Ask LearnersYour scraper has MAX_CHARS = 12_000. What happens when someone points it at a document that is four hundred thousand characters long?
Note for InstructorLet them answer. The honest answer is that you truncate, and you throw away most of the document without knowing whether the answer was in the part you cut. That is the gap this class fills.

That approach works for one web page. It falls apart the moment the material is bigger than the prompt, and almost all real material is. A company handbook. A year of support tickets. A textbook.

So the question for today is narrow and it is the whole of RAG: when the material is too big to send, how do you find the right few pieces to send instead?

Section 2 · 8 minWhy RAG exists

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Before the model answers, you retrieve the most relevant pieces of your own material and put them in the prompt. The model answers from those.

No retraining. No fine-tuning. It is an open-book exam instead of a closed-book one.

The four problems it solves

Problem What it looks like What RAG does
Knowledge cutoff It does not know what happened after training ended Fetch today's document
Private data It has never seen your HR policy, your contracts, your tickets Search your own material
Invention When unsure, it produces something plausible Ground the answer in real text
No sources Ask "where did you get that" and there is nothing Return the passage it used

Those first two are capability problems. The second two are the trust problems from Class 3, and RAG is the main structural answer to them.

ImportantThe fourth one is underrated. In Class 3 you learned that a confident answer tells you nothing about whether it is true. A retrieved answer can show you the passage, which means somebody can check it in five seconds. That changes what you can responsibly build.

Where you have already met it

Almost every AI feature you have used is this pattern underneath.

Product What it retrieves from
Notion AI Your workspace
Glean Your company's connected apps
Perplexity Live web pages
Intercom Fin The company's support articles
Cursor and similar Your repository
Claude Projects, ChatGPT Projects The files you attached, which is Class 4
Ask LearnersWhich of those have you used? And did you ever notice it showing you a source?
Doubt by LearnerWhy not just fine-tune a model on our data instead?

Three reasons it is usually the wrong first move. It is expensive and slow to redo every time your data changes, and your data changes constantly. It still cannot cite a source. And it does not stop invention. RAG is faster, cheaper, traceable, and almost always what you want first.

Section 3 · 8 minThe pipeline, and the two phases people confuse

Strip the jargon and the whole thing is a librarian sitting between the question and the model.

You ask a friend about an 800-page book they have never read. They guess. Instead, you find the three most relevant pages, hand them over, and then ask. Now they answer from real text. The librarian who finds those three pages is the only new thing in this class.

The five boxes

Box What happens
1 Question The user types it
2 Search Find the most relevant chunks
3 Stuff Put those chunks in the prompt
4 Model Answer using them
5 Answer Plus the source it used

Boxes 1, 3, 4 and 5 you already built in Class 3. Box 2 is the entire subject of today.

The two phases

This is the distinction people get wrong most often, and everything else makes more sense once it is clear.

Indexing happens once, offline. Read your documents, cut them into chunks, turn each chunk into numbers, store them. It is slow, and you only redo it when the documents change.

Querying happens every time, online. Turn the question into numbers, find the nearest chunks, put them in the prompt, answer. Milliseconds.

ImportantNothing here retrains the model. The model is the same one you called in Class 1. You are only changing what goes into the prompt. RAG is, at its core, automated prompt engineering.
Doubt by LearnerIf indexing is slow, does my app get slower as I add documents?

Indexing does, and your users never see it. Querying barely changes, because the search structures are built to scale: going from ten thousand chunks to ten million adds a handful of steps, not a million. Section 6 says why.

Section 4 · 16 minEmbeddings: why meaning needs coordinates

Before you can search by meaning, you need meaning in a form a computer can compare. That is an embedding, and once you see what it does the rest of RAG stops being mysterious.

Why keyword search is not enough

Someone searches your shoe catalogue for "running shoes". Your best product is titled "marathon footwear". Keyword search returns nothing, because not one word matches.

That is the problem. You need a way to find things that mean the same, not things that spell the same.

The idea: meaning as a position

Forget AI for a moment. Put people on a chart with height on one axis and weight on the other. Each person becomes a point. People of similar build end up near each other, and you can now ask "who is most similar to this person" by measuring distance. You turned a description into coordinates and got comparison for free.

An embedding does exactly that for text, except with hundreds or thousands of axes instead of two, and the axes are not things you can name. Similar meanings land near each other. Different meanings land far apart.

ImportantThis is the single idea the whole class rests on. "Find the relevant chunk" becomes "find the nearest point", and finding nearby points is something computers are extremely good at.

The part that convinces people

The famous demonstration: take the vector for king, subtract man, add woman, and you land very close to queen.

That is arithmetic. On meaning. The model was never taught what royalty or gender are; those relationships fell out of learning to predict text, which is Class 1's self-supervision lesson showing up again in a different form.

Note for InstructorLive demo, 4 minutes, using the similarity meter in the visualizer rather than any tool. Type two sentences that share no words but mean the same thing, and show the score stay high. Then two that share most of their words but mean opposite things, and show it drop. That contrast is the whole lesson and it does not depend on any model behaving in a particular way.

How similarity is actually measured

Cosine similarity, which is the angle between two arrows from the origin. Pointing the same way scores 1. At right angles, 0. Opposite, -1.

Why the angle and not the straight-line distance: a long document and a short sentence about the same topic produce arrows of different lengths but similar direction. Angle captures "about the same thing" while ignoring "says more about it".

Choosing an embedding model

You do not need to build one, and you do need to pick one. The decisions that matter:

Decision What to think about
General or domain-specific A general model is fine for most things. Legal, medical and code have specialised models that do noticeably better
English or multilingual A multilingual model lets a Hindi question find an English passage. An English-only model will not
Size and dimensions Bigger vectors capture more nuance, cost more to store and compare. More is not automatically better
Hosted or local Hosted is one API call. Local keeps your text on your own machines, which matters for regulated data
ImportantOne rule that catches everybody: you must use the same embedding model for your documents and for your questions. Two models produce two different coordinate systems, and distances between them are meaningless. Change your embedding model and you must re-index everything.
Doubt by Learner (Optional)Is an embedding the same as a token from Class 1?

No, and they are easy to confuse. A token is a chunk of text the model reads, with no meaning attached. An embedding is a position in meaning-space for a whole piece of text. Tokens are about reading, embeddings are about comparing.

Section 5 · 13 minChunking: how you cut decides what you can find

You cannot embed a 300-page book as one vector. The meaning of the whole book averages out into something that is close to nothing. So you cut it into pieces first, and how you cut turns out to matter a great deal.

A chunk is the unit of retrieval. Whatever you cut is what gets found and handed to the model.

Four strategies worth knowing

Strategy How it cuts Where it fits
Fixed size Every N characters, regardless of content Simplest. Cuts mid-sentence and mid-word. Fine for uniform text, bad for most things
Recursive Tries paragraph breaks first, then sentences, then words The sane default. Respects natural boundaries while keeping sizes roughly even
Semantic Cuts where the topic changes Best quality, most expensive, because it needs a model to judge the boundaries
Structure-aware Uses the document's own headings and sections Free quality when the document has structure: markdown, HTML, a contract with clauses
Note for InstructorLive demo, 4 minutes, with the chunking visualizer. Run the same passage through all four and let the room watch the boundaries move. The moment that lands is fixed-size cutting a sentence in half, then recursive fixing it at the same chunk size.

The trade-off you will actually tune

Big chunks mean high recall and low precision: the answer is probably in there, surrounded by a lot of irrelevant text that dilutes it.

Small chunks mean high precision and low recall: when you find the right chunk it is exactly the answer, but the relevant material may be split across two chunks and you only retrieved one.

ImportantChunk size is the first thing teams tune when RAG quality is poor, and there is no universal right answer. It depends on your documents and your questions.

Overlap

Chunks usually overlap by a small amount, often fifty to a hundred characters. If the answer sits exactly on a boundary, overlap means it still appears whole in at least one chunk. Cheap insurance against an annoying failure.

Ask LearnersThink of a document you work with. Which of the four strategies fits it, and why?
Note for InstructorAdvanced learner extension, if the room is ahead: ask what the right chunk is for source code. The answer people reach is the function or the class, not a character count, which is structure-aware chunking arrived at from first principles.

Section 6 · 9 minVector databases: what they actually do

You now have a few hundred thousand chunks, each with a vector. For every question you need the nearest few, fast. That is a vector database, and that is all it is.

An ordinary database answers "find rows where the column equals this". A vector database answers "find the vectors closest to this vector". Same idea, different question.

Three operations

That is genuinely the whole interface.

Operation What it does
Add Store chunks and their vectors. This is indexing
Query Give me the k nearest vectors to this one. This is retrieval
Delete Remove chunks when a document goes away

Why it stays fast

Comparing your question against a million vectors one at a time would be slow. Vector databases build a structure that lets a query take roughly log-many hops instead of a million comparisons, which is why lookups stay in the milliseconds even at very large scale.

You will never implement this. Every vector database ships it. What you need to know is that it is approximate: it trades a tiny amount of accuracy for an enormous amount of speed, and that trade is almost always worth it.

The names you will hear

Name What it is for
Chroma Open source, runs inside your process, no server. Prototypes and small datasets
Pinecone Fully managed and paid. No operations work, scales very large
Qdrant, Weaviate Open source and production-grade. Self-host or use their cloud
FAISS A library rather than a database. The building block underneath many of the others
ImportantYou do not always need one. For a few hundred chunks, a list in memory and a loop is genuinely fine, and the dependency costs you more than it saves. Reach for a vector database when the data outgrows memory, when you need it to persist, or when many users query it at once.

Section 7 · 13 minMaking retrieval good: reranking and hybrid search

Basic RAG works. Good RAG needs two more ideas, and these are where most of the quality difference between a demo and a product lives.

Problem one: the top five are not the best five

Embedding similarity is fast but shallow. It sometimes ranks a passage that shares surface wording above one that actually answers the question.

The fix is two passes, and the analogy is hiring.

You have a thousand applicants for one role. You cannot interview all of them, and you cannot pick from a stack of CVs on instinct. So you scan quickly to get a shortlist of twenty-five, then interview those properly.

First pass Second pass
Called Bi-encoder Cross-encoder
How it works Encodes the question and each document separately, compares the vectors Feeds the question and one document in together and scores the pair
Speed Milliseconds across millions Much slower, one pair at a time
Can you precompute it Yes, encode documents once and store forever No, every score is specific to that question
Use it to Narrow a million down to fifty Pick the best three from those fifty
ImportantThe second pass is only affordable because the first pass did the narrowing. That two-stage shape shows up everywhere in search and recommendation, not just RAG.
Note for InstructorLive demo, 4 minutes, with the reranking visualizer. The moment worth pausing on is the passage that never uses the word in the question and still turns out to be the best answer. Vector search puts it fourth; the reranker lifts it to first.

Problem two: sometimes you need the exact word

Semantic search is bad at things with no meaning to speak of. Ask for "the price of SKU-4429" and the embedding sees "price" and "product" and shrugs at the code. Product codes, model numbers, legal section numbers, names and dates are all cases where you want the literal string.

Keyword search, usually BM25, is excellent at exactly this and useless at synonyms. So you run both.

BM25, the literalist Vector, the philosopher
Great at Codes, IDs, names, exact phrases Synonyms, paraphrase, intent, other languages
Blind to Meaning. "Marathon footwear" is not "running shoes" Exact strings. SKU-4429 is noise

Hybrid search sends the question to both and merges the two ranked lists. The usual merge is Reciprocal Rank Fusion: each document scores based on where it placed in each list, so anything both methods liked rises to the top, and anything one method loved strongly still gets a fair hearing.

ImportantReal questions are messy. Mostly natural language, sprinkled with a product code or a proper noun. Hybrid covers both halves automatically, which is why it is the production default rather than a clever extra.

Section 9 · 7 minWhat this looks like in code, and where LangChain sits

Note for InstructorRead-only, 7 minutes. Say clearly at the start that nobody is building this today and nobody needs to type it. It is here so the vocabulary lands on something concrete and so they recognise the shape when they meet it in the AI Engineering module.

Everything from Sections 4 to 6, in about ten lines.

# NOT a build exercise. Read it, do not type it.

# --- Indexing: runs once, when documents change -------------------
chunks   = splitter.split_text(document)        # Section 5: chunking
db       = VectorStore.from_texts(chunks, embedder)   # Sections 4 and 6

# --- Querying: runs on every question -----------------------------
hits     = db.similarity_search(question, k=3)  # Section 6: the three nearest
context  = "\n\n".join(h.text for h in hits)    # Section 3: box 3, stuff

answer   = client.chat.completions.create(      # Class 1, unchanged
    model="openai/gpt-oss-120b",
    messages=[
        {"role": "system", "content": "Answer only from the context below."},
        {"role": "user",   "content": f"{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"},
    ],
)

Two things to notice, and they are the reason this block is here.

The last call is the one you already wrote in Class 1. Same client, same three roles, same shape. RAG did not replace anything you know; it changed what goes into the context.

The system line is the Class 3 grounding rule, unchanged. "Answer only from the context below" is still the most important hallucination control in the whole system.

Where LangChain fits

LangChain is the name you will hear most around this code, so here is what it actually is: glue.

It provides one common interface over the pieces. Document loaders for PDFs, web pages and the rest. Text splitters implementing the strategies from Section 5. Wrappers for embedding models and vector databases so that switching from Chroma to Pinecone is a one-line change rather than a rewrite.

ImportantYou do not need LangChain to do RAG. Every line above can be written directly against the underlying libraries, and for a small project that is often simpler. It earns its place when you are swapping components, chaining several steps together, or want the loaders written for you.
Doubt by LearnerShould I learn LangChain?

Learn the concepts first, which is what today was. The concepts transfer to any framework and outlast all of them. Frameworks in this area change fast; chunking, embeddings and retrieval do not.

Section 10 · 4 minWrap-up and what is next

What you can do now

You can explain what RAG is without using the word retrieval-augmented: when the material is too big to send, find the right pieces and send those.

You can say why embeddings exist, and why you cannot change the embedding model without re-indexing.

You can name four chunking strategies and the recall-precision trade-off between big and small chunks.

You can say what a vector database does, and when you do not need one.

You can name the two things that separate a demo from a product, reranking and hybrid search, and say what each fixes.

And you have watched a real system show you the passage it retrieved.

The honest limit of today

You have not built one. That is deliberate. Building a production retrieval pipeline, tuning chunk sizes against real queries and measuring whether reranking helped is a course of its own, and it is what the AI Engineering module is for. What you have is the map, which is what you need to read any of these systems and to ask the right question when someone says their RAG is not working.
