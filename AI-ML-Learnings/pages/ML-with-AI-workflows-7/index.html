A case study · HomeWorth Analytics

Building a rent model that survives contact with reality

A single dataset, one very common mistake, and eight ideas — regularization, hyperparameters, and cross-validation — that turn a model which merely memorizes into one you can defend.

The brief

You've just joined HomeWorth Analytics, a startup that automatically prices rental apartments for landlords. Your manager hands you a dataset scraped from roughly 400 listings — square footage, bedrooms, building age, and a long tail of other columns the scraper happened to grab along the way. Some of it is useful. Most of it, you don't know yet.

The brief is simple: build a model that predicts monthly rent well enough to auto-price new listings the moment they're posted. It has already been split into three batches — one to train on, one to check your work against as you go, and one kept completely untouched until the very end, as a final reality check.

01

The baseline model, and the trap it walks into

Thirty candidate columns describe each listing. Five of them — square footage, bedrooms, building age, distance to the metro, and floor number — are genuine physical properties. The other twenty-five are the kind of thing an eager scraper picks up along the way: paint-color scores, doorbell pitch, rooftop bird counts. None of them should have anything to do with rent. Your manager's instinct — "more data can't hurt" — is extremely common, and, as the model is about to show, not quite true.

The simplest possible move is plain linear regression: fit it on the 140 training listings, then check it against the 150 validation listings the model has never seen.

On paper the model looks strong. On listings it wasn't trained on, performance falls off a cliff — roughly a quarter of an R² point, by the lecture's own account. Bar heights here are schematic, sized to match that described gap rather than a specific logged run.

That gap between training performance and real-world performance has a name: overfitting. With 30 candidate features and only 140 training rows, plain linear regression happily bends itself to fit the noise in columns like rooftop_bird_count, because nothing is stopping it. The manager doesn't want a model that memorizes 140 old listings — they want one that generalizes to the next thousand. What's needed is a way to restrain the model: tell it not to lean too hard on any one feature unless the data really insists.

Notice, too, that the 110-listing test batch hasn't appeared anywhere above. It stays locked away until the very last step of this article.

02

Regularization — teaching the model some restraint

Plain linear regression (OLS) finds weights that minimize the residual sum of squares on the training rows — full stop. It has zero incentive to keep weights small, so it will assign a large, meaningless weight to a noise column if doing so shaves even a sliver off the training error.

$$\text{RSS}(w,b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2 \qquad \hat{y}_i = b + \sum_{j=1}^{p} w_j x_{ij}$$
yithe actual rent of listing i ŷithe rent the model predicts for listing i bthe intercept — the base prediction when every feature is zero wjthe weight the model assigns to feature j pnumber of features — 30, in this case

Regularization changes the objective: minimize the fit error plus a penalty on the size of the weights. The intercept is left out of that penalty — regularization constrains the feature weights, not the baseline prediction.

$$J(w,b) = \underbrace{\text{RSS}(w,b)}_{\text{fit the data}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda \cdot \text{penalty}(w)}_{\text{stay simple}}$$

λ (lambda) controls the tug-of-war between the two terms, and it's the single number that shapes everything from here on.

Ridge (L2 penalty)Lasso (L1 penalty)
Penalty term$$\lambda \sum w_j^2$$$$\lambda \sum |w_j|$$
Shape near zeroSmooth, rounded — the push weakens as a weight nears zeroA sharp corner — the push stays strong all the way to zero
Effect on coefficientsShrinks every weight, proportionally, but essentially never lands on exactly zeroCan shrink a weight all the way to exactly zero — removing that feature
ConsequenceEvery feature keeps a small sayBuilt-in feature selection

Why the shapes matter: a geometric view

Think of the grey contours below as the loss function's bowl — every point on one line fits the training data equally well — and the black dot as where unconstrained OLS would land. The colored shape is the region the penalty confines the solution to. The best regularized solution sits wherever the bowl first touches that shape.

A circle (Ridge) can be touched anywhere along its smooth edge. A diamond (Lasso) has corners sitting exactly on the axes — and contours are far more likely to first touch at a corner than along a flat edge. Touching at a corner means one coefficient lands on exactly zero. That's the entire geometric reason Lasso performs feature selection and Ridge doesn't.

Reading λ: at λ = 0 there's no penalty at all — identical to plain OLS. As λ → ∞, the penalty dominates until every weight is crushed toward zero and the model just predicts the average rent for everyone. Somewhere in between is a sweet spot.

Bonus, for the curious — Elastic Net blends both penalties, $$J(w,b)=\text{RSS}(w,b)+\lambda_1\sum|w_j|+\lambda_2\sum w_j^2$$ giving Lasso's sparsity and Ridge's stability at the same time.

Why did Lasso tend to win on HomeWorth's data? This dataset has only 4 truly useful features hiding among 25 pure-noise columns — precisely the situation where Lasso's ability to zero out irrelevant coefficients pays off. On a dataset where most features carried at least some real signal, Ridge would likely take the crown instead. It genuinely depends on the data.

03

Hyperparameters: choosing λ on purpose, not by eye

Eyeballing a value for λ isn't good enough for a model going into production. The bigger question underneath it: how do you systematically choose any setting a human has to decide before training even starts?

ParameterHyperparameter
Who sets it?The learning algorithm, from dataYou, before training begins
Example hereEach βj coefficient, the intercept β0λ, the regularization strength
When decided?During .fit()Before .fit() is even called
Other examplesSplit points in a decision treeK in KNN, tree depth, number of trees, learning rate

A useful shortcut: if changing it requires re-running training from scratch, it's a hyperparameter. If the algorithm computes it while training, it's a parameter.

Six steps to choose a hyperparameter honestly

  1. Pick a candidate range of λ values (e.g. 0.01 → 10,000, log-spaced)
  2. Set aside a validation set, separate from training data
  3. For each candidate λ: train on TRAIN, score on VALIDATION
  4. Pick the λ with the best validation score
  5. (optional) Retrain on the full training data using that λ
  6. Unlock the TEST set once, and report the final, honest performance

Step 6 happens exactly once, at the very end. The moment λ gets adjusted based on test-set performance, the test set stops being an honest judge — it quietly becomes part of training.

Which metric should watch step 3? Plain R² has an annoying property: adding any feature, even pure noise, can never decrease it — so R² alone always votes for the model with the most features. Adjusted R² corrects for that.

$$\text{Adjusted } R^2 = 1-(1-R^2)\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}$$
R²the ordinary R² on the validation set nnumber of observations used to compute it pnumber of predictors in the model

Adding a useless feature increases p, which shrinks the denominator and penalizes the score — unless the feature earns its keep by improving R² enough to offset that. It's a fair referee between models built with different numbers of effective features, which is exactly the comparison different λ values create.

The interactive explorer in the previous section already plots this in real time: the small curve beneath the coefficient bars is Adjusted R² against λ, with the current slider position marked. Too small a λ underperforms — barely regularized, still overfit-prone. Too large a λ also underperforms — over-shrunk, underfit. The sweet spot sits in between.

Stress test

The leakage trap

Before trusting any validation process, it's worth a sanity check: feed it a dataset with zero real signal — pure random noise in and out — and see whether it reports anything better than roughly zero R². If it does, the pipeline is lying somewhere.

Take 120 rows, 200 columns, and a target — all independently random, no relationship whatsoever. Compare what happens when feature selection runs before splitting into folds, versus inside each fold.

Approach A reports a suspiciously positive R² on data that is mathematically guaranteed to have none. Approach B lands right around zero, where an honest score on pure noise should sit. Per-fold scores here are an illustrative simulation of the described effect, not captured output values.

With 200 candidate columns and pure randomness, a handful will always look correlated with the target purely by chance. Letting feature selection see the validation rows before picking those "winning" columns leaks information backwards — the validation rows helped choose the very features being evaluated on those same rows.

Selecting features fresh inside every fold, using only that fold's training rows, keeps each fold's validation rows genuinely unseen by every step of the pipeline.

The rule this teaches: any step that looks at the target — feature selection, scaling statistics, imputation values, hyperparameter choices — must be fit using only the training portion of whatever split is currently being evaluated. A Pipeline makes this automatic and hard to get wrong.
04

Cross-validation, and why one split can lie

Cross-validation is a family of techniques for estimating how well a model will perform on unseen data, by repeatedly splitting the training data into a piece the model learns from and a piece it's evaluated on — without ever touching the real test set.

The simplest version is exactly what Chapter 3 already did: one train/validation split. Simple and fast — but how stable is it, really? What if the split had landed slightly differently?

Same model, same λ, same underlying data — twenty different single splits report a noticeably different validation R² depending purely on which rows landed in the validation slice. Illustrative simulation of the described instability.

Get unlucky with the split, and the wrong λ gets picked entirely — or the wrong number gets reported to a manager. Worse, whichever rows land in "validation" never get used to train the final model at all. There has to be a way to use every row for both training and validation, without ever letting a row validate the model that was trained on it.

05

K-Fold cross-validation

Split the training data into K equally-sized chunks. Train K separate models, each time holding out a different fold as validation and training on the remaining K−1 folds. Average the K validation scores. Every row gets used for validation exactly once, and for training in the other K−1 rounds — no row is ever validated by a model that was trained on it.

$$CV_{(K)} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\text{Error}_i$$
Knumber of folds Errorivalidation error on fold i, from a model trained on the other K−1 folds

Compared to the single 70/30 split above: instead of one lucky-or-unlucky number, this gives K independent estimates to average — directly fixing the instability just observed — and every row contributes to training at some point too.

06

Leave-One-Out CV, and other flavors

Leave-One-Out CV (LOOCV) is K-Fold taken to its extreme: K = n, the number of samples. Each round, exactly one row is held out for validation, and the model trains on all n−1 remaining rows.

$$CV_{(n)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_{-i}\right)^2$$
ntotal number of samples ý−ithe model's prediction for sample i, trained on every sample except i
StrategyModels trainedTypical biasTypical varianceCompute cost
Single holdout1can be highhigh (unstable)lowest
5-Fold CV5lowmoderatelow
10-Fold CV10very lowlowmoderate
LOOCV140lowestcan be higher (correlated folds)highest

Pros: nearly unbiased — each model trains on almost all the data — and completely deterministic, with no randomness from shuffling. Cons: expensive for large datasets (n rows means n model fits), and the n training sets are nearly identical to one another, so the resulting error estimates are highly correlated, which can inflate the variance of the final averaged estimate.

When to reach for it: small datasets, where you genuinely can't afford to hold out a meaningful chunk for validation — 5-fold CV on 30 rows leaves only 6 validation rows per fold, while LOOCV squeezes out every last usable row.

Two other flavors, briefly — Stratified K-Fold: for classification, keeps roughly the same proportion of each class in every fold, crucial for imbalanced data. TimeSeriesSplit: for time-ordered data, where shuffling would let a model "see the future" — each fold's validation period comes strictly after its training period.
07

Automating the search

K-Fold CV gives a trustworthy way to measure how good a given λ is. But manually looping over candidates and eyeballing a plot doesn't scale — not when there's a second hyperparameter to tune, or several methods to compare. Two tools automate the entire "try candidates → cross-validate → pick the best" loop. Both run K-Fold CV internally for every candidate; they differ only in which candidates they try.

GridSearchCVRandomizedSearchCV
StrategyTry every combination in a defined gridSample a fixed number (n_iter) of candidates from a distribution
GuaranteeBest combination within the gridNo guarantee, but usually a very good combination

08

Why any of this works: bias vs. variance

Zoom out, and here's the force this whole article has been fighting. The expected error of a model on new data decomposes into three pieces:

$$\text{Expected Test Error} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible Error}$$
  • Bias — error from a model too simple to capture the true pattern, systematically wrong in the same direction. A heavily-regularized model that predicts close to the average rent for everyone has high bias.
  • Variance — error from a model too sensitive to the specific training sample it happened to see. The Chapter 1 baseline, fit on 30 noisy features and only 140 rows, has high variance — retrain it on a slightly different sample and its predictions swing wildly.
  • Irreducible error — inherent real-world noise that no model can ever explain away.

As λ increases, bias goes up — the model is allowed to fit the data less precisely — while variance goes down, since it becomes less sensitive to exactly which rows it saw. Regularization is a deliberate trade of a little bias for a lot less variance, and cross-validation is how that trade gets found empirically.
