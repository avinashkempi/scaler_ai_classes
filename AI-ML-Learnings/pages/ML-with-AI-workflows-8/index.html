SETTING THE SCENE

Rohan's cart-abandonment model

Rohan is a data analyst at Northstar Retail, a mid-sized e-commerce company. He has trained a logistic regression model to predict whether a customer who abandoned their shopping cart will complete the purchase within 24 hours if sent a reminder email. The output is binary: will convert or will not convert.

The model works, and the team is excited about it. But when Rohan presents the results in a review meeting, three uncomfortable questions come up, one after another — and a fourth arrives once the first three are settled. Each one pushes the model a little closer to something the team can actually rely on:

  • Can we trust it on new customers? — this is a question about hyperparameter tuning and regularization strength.
  • What is the model actually computing? — this is a question about log-odds and the logit.
  • Can one weird data point throw it off? — this is a question about the impact of outliers.
  • Can it choose between more than two outcomes? — this is a question about multi-class classification with One-vs-Rest.

Each section below tracks one of these questions in the order the team asked them.

QUESTION 1

"Your model looks perfect on training data. Can we trust it on new customers?"

Rohan's manager points out that the training accuracy is unusually high — almost too high. The worry: has the model memorised quirks of the training data instead of learning the general pattern of cart-abandonment behaviour? This is where hyperparameter tuning comes in.

An analogy before the maths

Analogy

Think of training a logistic regression model like preparing a student for a general aptitude exam using a set of practice questions.

If the student memorises the exact practice questions word for word, they score perfectly on those questions but struggle the moment the real exam asks something slightly different — that's overfitting. If the student ignores the practice questions almost entirely and guesses from broad instincts, they do poorly even on the practice set — that's underfitting. The goal is a student who learns the underlying pattern: general enough to transfer, specific enough to be useful. That balance is what regularization controls.

The maths behind it

Logistic regression tries to find coefficients (weights) that minimise a loss function. Left unconstrained, the optimiser can push coefficients to large values to fit the training data as closely as possible — exactly the "memorising practice questions" behaviour. Regularization adds a penalty term that discourages large coefficients. In sklearn, this is controlled by a hyperparameter called C:

C = 1λ
  • λ (lambda) is the regularization strength — a larger λ means a heavier penalty on large coefficients, pushing toward simpler, more general patterns.
  • C is the inverse of λ, and it's the parameter sklearn actually asks for. Because C is an inverse, a small C means strong regularization, and a large C means weak regularization.

There's no single correct value of C — it depends on the dataset. The standard approach is to try a range of values and pick whichever performs best on a validation set: data the model hasn't trained on, standing in for "the real exam."

# small, slightly noisy two-class dataset
X_demo, y_demo = make_classification(
    n_samples=120, n_features=2, n_redundant=0,

def show_boundary(C=1.0):
    model = LogisticRegression(C=C)
    model.fit(X_demo, y_demo)
    DecisionBoundaryDisplay.from_estimator(
        model, X_demo, response_method="predict"
    )
QUESTION 2

"What is the model actually computing internally?"

Once Rohan picks a value of C using the validation set, performance on new customers improves and the team is satisfied the model generalises well. But a second question surfaces: for the customer with a predicted probability of 0.97, what does the model actually know that makes it so confident, compared to the one at 0.55? To answer that, we need to look inside the model, at what it computes before it ever produces a probability — two related ideas: odds and log-odds (also called the logit).

Odds, before log-odds

Odds are a familiar idea outside data science. If a forecaster says there's a 75% chance of rain, they could equally say "the odds of rain are 3 to 1" — for every 1 dry outcome, there are 3 rainy ones on average. Formally, if p is the probability of an event happening (here, that a customer converts):

odds = p1 − p

Odds range from 0 (impossible) to ∞ (certain); odds of exactly 1 mean the event is equally likely to happen or not.

From odds to log-odds

Odds are asymmetric: a very unlikely event gives odds near 0, but a very likely one gives odds that can run into the hundreds or thousands. Taking the logarithm of the odds fixes this asymmetry and gives the log-odds, or logit:

logit(p) = lnp1 − p = z

Here z is exactly the same z = w·x + b — the weighted sum of features plus a bias — that logistic regression computes internally, before it ever becomes a probability. Logistic regression is a straightforward linear model in log-odds space. Log-odds can be any real number: a large positive z strongly favours converting, a large negative z strongly favours not converting, and z = 0 is a 50-50 call.

Getting back to a probability: the sigmoid

Log-odds are useful for the model's internal arithmetic, but people want a probability. The sigmoid function converts log-odds back into a probability between 0 and 1 — it's simply the algebraic inverse of the logit:

p = σ(z) = 11 + e−z

Geometrically, sigmoid is an S-shaped curve that squashes any real number into (0, 1). A very large positive z gives a probability extremely close to 1; a very large negative z gives one extremely close to 0 — but never exactly either.

QUESTION 3

"Can one weird data point throw off the whole model?"

Understanding log-odds settles the second question. But a third issue surfaces when someone on the team notices a strange record: a customer whose session length is listed as 40 hours — almost certainly a logging error. How much damage can one bad data point do?

The answer, somewhat counter-intuitively, is: it depends on which side of the decision boundary the outlier falls on.

The loss function, briefly

Logistic regression is trained by minimising the log loss (cross-entropy loss) for each data point. For a single example, if y is the true label (0 or 1) and ŷ is the model's predicted probability for the positive class:

L = −[ y·ln(ŷ) + (1 − y)·ln(1 − ŷ) ]

What matters here is the shape of this loss: it stays small when a prediction is confident and correct, and grows extremely large when a prediction is confident and wrong.

Case 1 — the outlier lands on the correct side

Suppose the odd 40-hour session belongs to a customer who genuinely did convert (y = 1), and despite the strange value the model's weighted sum still produces a large positive log-odds, say z = 6. The predicted probability ŷ = σ(6) ≈ 0.998 is close to 1, matching the true label. The loss is L = −ln(0.998) ≈ 0.002 — tiny. Even though the point looks unusual, because the model predicts it correctly it barely contributes to total error, and has very little influence on where the boundary is drawn.

Case 2 — the outlier lands on the wrong side

Now suppose a different odd record, still y = 1, but the model's weighted sum produces a strongly negative log-odds, z = −4.3, because the unusual feature value pulls the prediction the wrong way. ŷ = σ(−4.3) ≈ 0.013, and the loss is L = −ln(0.013) ≈ 4.34 — more than a thousand times larger than Case 1. Because the optimiser minimises total loss across all points, it will adjust coefficients specifically to bring this loss down, even shifting the boundary in a way that's slightly worse for the rest of the well-behaved data.

Practical takeaway

It's worth specifically checking outliers that the model currently gets wrong, since those are the ones capable of distorting the decision boundary. Outliers the model already gets right are comparatively harmless — but the safest general practice is still to investigate and, where justified, correct or remove data errors like the 40-hour session length.

QUESTION 4

"Our model only knows two answers. How do we get it to choose between three or more?"

Rohan cleans up the flagged record and retrains the model; the team is now confident the binary "will convert / will not convert" model is solid. At this point a marketing stakeholder joins with a new request: "Binary is fine, but what we actually want is to sort abandoned carts into three groups: high urgency (recover today), medium urgency (recover this week), and low urgency (unlikely to convert regardless)." This is no longer a two-class problem.

Logistic regression, as used so far, is inherently a two-class algorithm: the sigmoid produces one probability, for one class, and the other class is implied as 1 − p. To handle more than two classes we need a strategy that reuses logistic regression as a building block rather than reinventing it. The most common such strategy is One-vs-Rest (OvR).

Analogy

Imagine three specialist doctors independently examining the same patient. Doctor A only answers one question: "is this condition A, or is it not?" Doctors B and C do the same for their own conditions, each treating the other two possibilities as a single "not mine" bucket. At the end, all three report a confidence score for their own condition being present, and the final diagnosis goes to whichever doctor was most confident — regardless of what that raw number was.

The maths of One-vs-Rest

If there are K classes, One-vs-Rest trains K separate binary logistic regression models. For the k-th model:

  • The label is temporarily reassigned: examples belonging to class k are relabelled 1, and every example belonging to any other class is relabelled 0.
  • The model trains exactly like a normal two-class model, producing its own probability ŷk for "belongs to class k."

For Rohan's three urgency classes, this means training three models: "high urgency vs. rest," "medium urgency vs. rest," and "low urgency vs. rest."

Making a prediction with K models

Given a new abandoned cart, all K trained models each produce their own probability. The predicted class is whichever model produced the highest probability:

predicted class = argmaxk ŷk

This rule is applied even if none of the K probabilities individually crosses the usual 0.5 threshold — the class with the relatively highest confidence still wins, since exactly one class must be chosen.

A WIDER VIEW

This idea is bigger than it looks

The One-vs-Rest idea just covered — training several models and letting them compete on a probability score — is a close cousin of something running underneath almost every modern large language model.

When a model like a chatbot generates its next word, it isn't doing anything mystical: it computes a score for every possible next word in its vocabulary (which can be hundreds of thousands of options), converts those scores into probabilities using a generalisation of the sigmoid function called softmax, and then selects — or samples — the option with the highest probability. Softmax is, in essence, what results from extending the "multiple binary competitions" spirit of One-vs-Rest into a single unified layer that outputs a full probability distribution over every class at once, rather than K independent yes/no verdicts.

In other words: the mechanism used today to sort abandoned carts into three urgency buckets is a small, transparent relative of the mechanism a large language model uses to decide its very next word. Understanding one gives real intuition for the other.

Go further

Ask an AI assistant to explain the softmax function and how it differs mathematically from training K separate One-vs-Rest models — specifically, why softmax guarantees that all class probabilities sum to exactly 1, while One-vs-Rest probabilities generally do not (as seen in the argmax widget above). Ask for one practical scenario where that difference actually matters.

RECAP

Four questions, one more reliable model

1 · Hyperparameter tuning

The regularization parameter C (and its relationship to λ) controls the tradeoff between an overfit model and an underfit one. The right value is chosen using validation performance, not training performance.

2 · Log-odds and the logit

Logistic regression is fundamentally a linear model in log-odds space; the sigmoid function is the transformation that converts that internal log-odds score into an interpretable probability.

3 · Impact of outliers

An unusual data point only distorts the decision boundary meaningfully when the model currently predicts it with high confidence and gets it wrong. Outliers predicted correctly have minimal effect on training.

4 · Multi-class classification

One-vs-Rest extends logistic regression beyond two classes by training one binary model per class and picking whichever model reports the highest probability.

And beyond today

Softmax, a generalisation of multi-class logistic regression, is how this same underlying idea shows up inside today's large-scale AI systems.
