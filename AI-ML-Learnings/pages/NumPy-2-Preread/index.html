NumPy 2: Vectorized Analysis
Summarize, Filter and Segment

1What's Happening in This Lecture

Here's a sneak peek at everything we cover today, and why each one is worth showing up for.

TopicWhat It Covers
Aggregate Functions & axissum, mean, min and max collapse many numbers into one. On a 2D marksheet, axis decides whether you get one answer per column (subject) or one per row (student). argmax() and argmin() go a step further and tell you where the highest or lowest value sits, so you can name the topper, not just the top mark. Think AVG / MAX with GROUP BY.
Fancy Indexing & Boolean MaskingThere are two ways to select data: list the positions you want, or state a condition and let NumPy find the matches. You'll filter whole grids and whole rows, combine conditions with & and |, and answer yes/no questions with np.any() and np.all(), NumPy's version of SQL's WHERE and EXISTS.
Sortingnp.sort() returns sorted values, .sort() sorts in place, and argsort() returns the sort order itself so that entire rows can move together. That's how a rank list keeps each student's marks attached, and why sorting a 2D array along the wrong axis can quietly scramble a table.
VectorizationA function written with if/elif works on one value but fails on a whole array. You'll see why that happens, what true vectorization means, and how np.vectorize() lets a Python function accept arrays. There's a catch about its speed, which you'll measure later in the class.
BroadcastingThis is how NumPy combines arrays of different shapes: a single number, a row of per-column adjustments, or a column of per-row values. You'll learn the right-to-left shape rule that decides whether an operation works or raises an error. It's the same mechanism that made marks + 5 work in NumPy 1.
Conditional Logic with np.where()np.where(condition, if_true, if_false) labels or replaces every value in one call, like CASE WHEN … THEN … ELSE … END. Given only a condition, it does a different job and returns the positions that match.
np.select() vs np.where()np.where suits two outcomes. Three or more (grade bands, risk tiers) suit np.select, which checks a list of conditions in order. You'll race it against np.vectorize on 10 lakh values and come away knowing which tool fits which job.

2Real-World Use Cases

  • Aggregate Functions & axis: A category manager finds the average, best and worst monthly sales for each product line (axis=0) and each store's annual total (axis=1), then uses argmax to name the top store per category.
  • Fancy Indexing & Boolean Masking: A fraud analyst flags every transaction above ₹50,000 made between midnight and 5 a.m. by combining two conditions with &, then lists customers with at least one flagged transaction using np.any(..., axis=1).
  • Sorting: A sales head builds a leaderboard of reps by quarterly total with argsort, so each rep's monthly figures stay on the same row as their totals.
  • Vectorization: A credit analyst already has a Python function that assigns a risk tier to one applicant; np.vectorize applies it to a whole batch of applicants without rewriting the logic.
  • Broadcasting: A finance analyst applies a different tax rate to each product column of a monthly sales table, and a per-store discount down the rows, with plain shape-aware arithmetic.
  • Conditional Logic with np.where(): An operations team caps delivery times above 120 minutes at 120 before averaging, and labels every order "On time" or "Late" in one line.
  • np.select() vs np.where(): A marketing analyst segments 10 lakh customers into Gold, Silver, Bronze and Standard by spend using np.select, instead of nested conditions or a slow Python function.
