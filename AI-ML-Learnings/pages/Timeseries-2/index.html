02 The Scorecard

First, How Will She Even Know If a Forecast Is Good?

Rohan's ask wasn't just "forecast the sales" — it came with a number attached: keep the error under 5%. Before Aisha builds a single model, she needs a way to turn "how wrong was I" into a number Rohan can hold her to.

Analogy

Imagine guessing two people's ages. You guess 30 for someone who's actually 25 — off by 5 years. You guess 8 for a toddler who's actually 3 — also off by 5 years. Same raw error, but the second guess is far more embarrassing, because 5 years is a much bigger fraction of a 3‑year‑old's age. A percentage error captures that "relative to what?" feeling a raw error misses.

That's exactly what Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) does: for every point, it looks at how far off the forecast was as a percentage of the actual value, then averages that across all points.

MAPE = average of |actual − forecast|actual

Absolute error, divided by the actual value, averaged over every point — "on average, how many percent off were we."

Why Rohan likes MAPE as a business metric

  • It's a percentage, so it's instantly comparable across products, months, or even different businesses — a MAPE of 5% means the same thing whether sales are in the hundreds or the millions
  • Other metrics, like plain average error, need a baseline to compare against; MAPE tells its own story
One catch

If the actual value is ever exactly zero, MAPE breaks — you'd be dividing by zero. Aisha keeps this in the back of her mind, though monthly fashion sales are never actually zero.

03 An Honest Test

Splitting the Data — But Not the Way You'd Think

With a scorecard in hand, Aisha's instinct is to do what she'd do for any other ML problem: shuffle the rows and split. She stops herself. You cannot shuffle a calendar.

Analogy

Imagine studying for an exam by peeking at next month's chapter to help answer this month's question. That's what a random shuffle would do here — it lets the model "see" July while trying to predict May. In real life, Aisha will never have July's numbers when she's asked to forecast May. The test has to mimic that honestly.

So instead of a random split, time series uses a time‑based split: everything before a cutoff date is training data, everything after is testing data. Nothing from the future leaks backward. For Vastra Loop's 18 years of monthly data, Aisha holds out the last 12 months as her test set — a full year, so she can check performance across every season.

▸ Time‑based train / test split 
train_x = vastra_sales.iloc[:-12]
test_x = vastra_sales.iloc[-12:]

There's no separate train_y / test_y here, because this dataset has only one column — Sales — indexed by date. The "y" being forecast and the "x" being used both come from that same single column, just sliced by time.

▸ A reusable performance helper 
from sklearn.metrics import (
    mean_squared_error as mse,
    mean_absolute_error as mae,
    mean_absolute_percentage_error as mape
)

def performance(actual, predicted):
    print('MAE :', round(mae(actual, predicted), 3))
    print('RMSE:', round(mse(actual, predicted) ** 0.5, 3))
    print('MAPE:', round(mape(actual, predicted), 3))
Check yourself · 01
Why can't Aisha use a regular random train_test_split() on Vastra Loop's monthly sales data?

A random shuffle mixes future and past freely, so the model could "learn" from information it would never actually have at prediction time. Time series always needs a time‑based split, where training data comes strictly before testing data.

Note: train_test_split() itself isn't the problem — you can disable shuffling with shuffle=False and preserve the time order. The important point is that the training period must come strictly before the testing period.

04 Setting a Floor

Before Anything Clever — Try the Dumbest Thing First

With her scorecard ready and her split honest, Aisha is tempted to jump straight to something sophisticated. But she remembers advice from a senior analyst: always build a "dumb" baseline first. If a fancy model can't beat a stupidly simple guess, it isn't worth using. So before touching anything statistical, she tries three deliberately simple approaches.

1 · Forecast the Mean

The guess: predict the average of all past sales, for every future month.

Analogy

It's like being asked what a stranger weighs, and always answering "the average adult weight" — regardless of whether they described a toddler or a bodybuilder. Sometimes it's close. Usually it isn't, and it never adapts.

With 17 years of history, one new month barely moves the average at all — so this method's forecast is basically a flat, unchanging line. It can never predict a rise for the festive season or a dip afterward, because it isn't looking at the shape of the data at all, just its overall centre. (The same argument rules out the median, for the same reason.)

▸ Forecast: mean 
test_x['pred'] = train_x['Sales'].mean()

test_x.plot(style='-o', title='Forecast: Mean')
performance(test_x['Sales'], test_x['pred'])

Pretty bad, as expected. A MAPE around 25% is nowhere near Rohan's 5% target — but now Aisha has a number to beat, which is the whole point of a baseline.

2 · The Naive Approach

The guess: forecast next month to be exactly the same as the most recent known month.

Analogy

It's like assuming tomorrow's weather will be identical to today's. Sometimes true, often not — and it gets shakier the further out you forecast, since every future month just repeats one single data point.

▸ Forecast: naive 
test_x['pred'] = train_x['Sales'][-1]

test_x.plot(style='-o', title='Forecast: Naive')
performance(test_x['Sales'], test_x['pred'])

A little better than the flat mean, but still far from usable — it completely ignores that Vastra Loop's sales rise and fall with the seasons.

3 · Seasonal Naive Forecast

Aisha's next thought: fashion sales are seasonal — festive months spike every single year. What if she just repeats last year's same month? Forecast this March to equal last March, this December to equal last December, and so on.

Analogy

Instead of assuming tomorrow repeats today, this assumes this Diwali repeats last Diwali — a far more sensible assumption for a business with a strong yearly rhythm.

▸ Forecast: seasonal naive 
for i in test_x.index:
    test_x.loc[i, 'pred'] = train_x.loc[i - pd.DateOffset(years=1), 'Sales']

test_x.plot(style='-o', title='Forecast: Seasonal Naive')
performance(test_x['Sales'], test_x['pred'])

So why bother with these "dumb" methods at all, if none of them get close to 5% MAPE? Because they set the floor. Any model Aisha builds from here on has to beat these numbers to justify its own existence. If a complicated model can't outperform "just repeat last year," it isn't worth the added complexity.

Check yourself · 02
Which of the following statements is true?

Forecasting the mean or median always produces a flat line, since it ignores the shape of the series entirely. The plain naive method only repeats the last value — no seasonality — and seasonal naive repeats last year's pattern (seasonality, but no trend adjustment).

05 Reaching for a Familiar Tool

Borrowing an Old Trick for a New Job

None of the baselines got close to Rohan's target. Aisha remembers she already has a tool that's sort of about "predicting" a value — the moving average, which she used in Part 1 to smooth out noise and fill gaps.

The idea: if the average of the last few points was a good estimate of a missing point in the middle of the series, why not use that same average to estimate the next point at the end of the series? Forecast one step, add it to the series, average again for the step after that, and repeat.

df = vastra_sales.copy()

df = pd.concat([df, pd.DataFrame(
    index=pd.date_range(start=df.index[-1], periods=13, freq='MS')[1:]
)])
df.tail(15)
▸ Roll a 3‑month average forward, 12 times 
pred = df.Sales.dropna().values

for i in range(12):
    pred = np.append(pred, pred[-3:].mean())  # average of the last 3 points

Does this look like a good forecast? It's already a step up from the flat baselines — the level looks roughly right at first. But look closely: the line flattens out and barely moves after a few months.

Why? Every new forecasted point gets folded into the next average. Drawbacks Aisha notices
  • The forecast levels out after a few points, because it stops "listening" to anything beyond the last 3 values
  • With 18 years of data available, it feels wasteful to look only at the most recent 3 points and throw the rest away

She needs a method that still leans on recent data most heavily, but doesn't completely ignore everything older than 3 months.

06 A Fading Memory

A Memory That Fades, Instead of a Memory That Cuts Off

The moving average's flaw was sharp and sudden: the last 3 points matter, everything before that is worth nothing. Aisha wants something gentler — recent points matter more, but older points still matter a little, fading gradually instead of dropping off a cliff.

Analogy

Think of an echo in a canyon. The most recent shout is loudest, the one before it a little softer, the one before that softer still — but you can still faintly hear shouts from a while ago, just barely. Nothing is completely silenced; it just fades exponentially.

This is exactly what Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES) does: it weights every past observation, with weights that shrink exponentially the further back you go.

ŷt+1=α· yt +(1−α)· ŷt

In words: the next forecast is a blend of what actually happened most recently, and what was already expected.

The one knob to control is alpha (α), between 0 and 1:

  • A low alpha leans on the past — smoother, slower to react, like someone who takes a long time to update their opinion
  • A high alpha leans almost entirely on the latest value — jumpier, quick to react
  • At α = 1, SES quietly turns back into the naive method — the most recent value is the forecast

statsmodels has a built‑in SimpleExpSmoothing model — the smoothing_level parameter is exactly the alpha described above.

model = pd.Series(sm.tsa.SimpleExpSmoothing(vastra_sales.Sales).fit(smoothing_level=0.8).fittedvalues)

With alpha this high, the fit reacts almost instantly to every wiggle — barely any smoothing at all. Aisha tries a much smaller alpha, using a common rule of thumb: 1 / (2 × seasonality length). Since Vastra Loop's seasonality repeats every 12 months, that gives alpha = 1/24.

▸ SES with alpha = 1/24 
model = pd.Series(sm.tsa.SimpleExpSmoothing(vastra_sales.Sales).fit(smoothing_level=1/(2*12)).fittedvalues)

model.plot(label='SES fit (alpha=1/24)')
vastra_sales.Sales.plot(label='original')
plt.legend()

That's a much smoother fit, and it catches the general level of the series nicely — a real improvement over the moving average, since it isn't limited to the last 3 points.

▸ Forecast 12 months out 
model = sm.tsa.SimpleExpSmoothing(vastra_sales.Sales).fit(smoothing_level=1/(2*12))
pred = model.forecast(steps=12)

vastra_sales.Sales.tail(100).plot(label='original', style='-o')
pred.plot(label='SES forecast', style='-o')
plt.legend()
▸ Score it against the real test set 
model = sm.tsa.SimpleExpSmoothing(train_x.Sales).fit(smoothing_level=1/(2*12))

test_x.plot(style='-o', title='SES — Performance Check')
performance(test_x['Sales'], test_x['pred'])
Drawback

SES is smarter than the moving average — it weighs the whole history sensibly instead of a hard 3‑month cutoff. But the forecast plot flattens into a near‑straight line for the future. SES only ever tracks the current level of the series — it has no concept of "the series is generally climbing" (trend) or "December is always bigger" (seasonality). It's still missing pieces.

Check yourself · 03
In Simple Exponential Smoothing, what happens to the forecast as alpha (α) approaches 1?

At α = 1, all the weight sits on the most recent observation, and nothing on the past. That's exactly the naive method Aisha tried earlier — SES contains the naive method as a special case.

07 Holt's Method

Adding a Slope — Double Exponential Smoothing

SES's forecast flattening out bothers Aisha, because she knows Vastra Loop's sales aren't flat — they've been climbing for years. SES tracks the level, roughly "where is the series right now," but has no idea the series is also drifting upward.

Analogy

SES is like tracking a hiker's current altitude — useful, but it tells you nothing about whether they're walking uphill or downhill. To predict where they'll be next, you also need their rate of climb.

Double Exponential Smoothing, also called Holt's method, does exactly this: it keeps two fading memories instead of one — one for the level, and a second one (with its own smoothing knob, beta) for the trend, i.e. how much the level itself changes each month. Holt's method suits series with a clear trend but no strong seasonality of their own — it still has nothing built in for a repeating seasonal shape.

▸ Fit Holt's method (trend='add') 
model = sm.tsa.ExponentialSmoothing(vastra_sales.Sales, trend='add').fit(smoothing_level=0.4)

model.fittedvalues.plot(label='fitted')
vastra_sales.Sales.plot(label='original')
plt.legend()
▸ Forecast 12 months out 
pred = model.forecast(steps=12)

vastra_sales.Sales.tail(100).plot(style='-o', label='actual')
pred.plot(style='-o', label='predicted')
plt.legend()

Notice the forecast now slopes upward instead of flattening — Holt's method carries the trend forward. Time to check whether that actually improves the MAPE score.

▸ Score it against the real test set 
model = sm.tsa.ExponentialSmoothing(train_x.Sales, trend='add').fit(smoothing_level=1/(2*12))
test_x['pred'] = model.forecast(steps=12)
test_x.plot(style='-o', title="Holt's Method — Performance Check")
performance(test_x['Sales'], test_x['pred'])
Go further

Progress — but Aisha notices something: the forecast still slides through the festive‑season spikes and post‑festive dips as if they weren't there. Holt's method fixed the slope problem, but Vastra Loop is a fashion business with a real, repeating seasonal rhythm. One more ingredient is still missing.

08 Holt-Winters

Adding a Season — Triple Exponential Smoothing

If SES tracks level, and Holt's method adds trend, the next step writes itself: add a third fading memory for seasonality — how much higher or lower a given month tends to run compared to the overall level, repeating every 12 months.

Analogy

Think of a shop that's slow every Monday and busy every Saturday — a real, repeating weekly rhythm, not the general upward trend of a growing shop. Triple Exponential Smoothing, or Holt‑Winters, tracks all three things at once — level, trend, and this repeating seasonal rhythm — each with its own smoothing knob: alpha for level, beta for trend, and a new one, gamma, for seasonality.

This method can:

  • Handle level, trend, and seasonality together
  • Adapt dynamically as new data comes in
  • Be used whenever a series has both a trend and/or seasonality — which describes Vastra Loop's sales almost exactly
▸ Fit Holt‑Winters (trend='add', seasonal='add') 
model = sm.tsa.ExponentialSmoothing(vastra_sales.Sales, trend='add', seasonal='add').fit(smoothing_level=0.4)

model.fittedvalues.plot(label='fitted')
vastra_sales.Sales.plot(label='original')
plt.legend()
▸ Forecast 12 months out 
pred = model.forecast(steps=12)

vastra_sales.Sales.tail(100).plot(style='-o', label='actual')
pred.plot(style='-o', label='predicted')
plt.legend()

Now the forecast actually rises and falls with the festive‑season pattern, instead of a flat or purely diagonal line. Time for the real test.

▸ Score it against the real test set 
model = sm.tsa.ExponentialSmoothing(train_x.Sales, trend='add', seasonal='add').fit(smoothing_level=1/(2*12))
test_x['pred'] = model.forecast(steps=12)
test_x.plot(style='-o', title='Holt-Winters — Performance Check')
performance(test_x['Sales'], test_x['pred'])

This is the best result Aisha has seen yet — MAPE drops to roughly 4%, comfortably under Rohan's 5% target. Level, trend, and seasonality, all tracked together, finally get her there.

Check yourself · 04
What does the gamma parameter control in Holt‑Winters (Triple Exponential Smoothing)?

Gamma is the smoothing parameter for the seasonal component, just as alpha controls the level and beta controls the trend. Together, the three let Holt‑Winters track level, trend, and seasonality independently.

09 A New Question From Rohan

A Steady Racetrack: Stationarity and the Dickey–Fuller Test

Holt‑Winters gets Aisha under her 5% MAPE target, and she's ready to celebrate — until Rohan mentions the team is also planning to try more advanced forecasting models down the line, like ARIMA. There's a catch: those models don't work directly on any time series. They expect the series to be stationary first — meaning its statistical properties don't change over time.

Analogy

Think of a running track. A stationary series is like a flat, perfectly circular track — no matter which lap you're on, running it feels the same. A series with trend and seasonality is like a track that keeps changing shape and elevation lap after lap. Before Aisha can hand her data to those models, she needs to "flatten the track."

A stationary series has:

  • No long‑term upward or downward drift (no trend)
  • No repeating calendar‑based pattern (no seasonality)
  • Roughly the same spread of ups and downs throughout, instead of growing wilder or calmer over time

Checking stationarity — the Dickey–Fuller test

Eyeballing a plot only gets you so far. Aisha wants a proper statistical check, so she reaches for the (Augmented) Dickey–Fuller test.

Analogy

Think of it like a lie‑detector test for "does this series have a memory of trend." It gives back a p‑value — small (typically under 0.05) means "no, this series looks stationary"; large means "there's still a lingering trend or drift here."

▸ Run the ADF test 
sm.tsa.stattools.adfuller(vastra_sales.Sales)[1]
Output
np.float64(0.9789233920172065)

The p‑value comes back around 0.98 — about as far from "stationary" as it gets. Unsurprising, since the series already has both trend and seasonality baked in. Aisha wraps the test in a small helper she can reuse.

▸ A reusable stationarity helper 
def adf_test(dataset):
    pvalue = sm.tsa.stattools.adfuller(dataset)[1]
    if pvalue <= 0.05:
        print(f'p-value = {round(pvalue, 4)} -> Series looks STATIONARY')
    else:
        print(f'p-value = {round(pvalue, 4)} -> Series looks NON-STATIONARY')

adf_test(vastra_sales.Sales)
Output
p-value = 0.9789 -> Series looks NON-STATIONARY

10 Fixing It

Flattening the Track: De‑trending and De‑seasonalising

Now that Aisha has a way to check stationarity, she needs a way to fix it — to strip out the trend and seasonality and see what's left underneath.

De‑trending

Analogy

If you're walking up a hill and want to know your "true" step‑to‑step effort ignoring the slope, you'd look at the change in altitude each step, not the altitude itself. That's exactly what differencing does — instead of looking at the raw value, look at how much it changed from the previous point.

▸ First difference 
detrend = vastra_sales.Sales.diff()
detrend.plot(title='De-trended (1st difference)')
▸ Check stationarity after de‑trending 
adf_test(detrend.dropna())
Output
p-value = 0.0 -> Series looks STATIONARY

The series looks flatter now, and the ADF test agrees the trend is mostly gone. But look closely at the plot — there's still a faint repeating wave. That's the seasonality, still sitting in there untouched.

De‑seasonalising

To remove a repeating yearly pattern, Aisha does the same trick, but compares each point to the same point one full cycle (12 months) ago instead of the point right before it — this is called seasonal differencing, or "m‑differencing," where m is the season length.

▸ Seasonal (12‑month) difference 
deseas = vastra_sales.Sales.diff(12)
deseas.plot(title='De-seasonalised (12-month difference)')

Removing trend or seasonality alone still leaves a faint trace of the other behind. So Aisha combines both — first differencing to strip the trend, then a 12‑month difference on top of that to strip the seasonality.

▸ Combine both: diff(1) then diff(12) 
stationary = vastra_sales.Sales.diff(1).diff(12)
stationary.plot(title='Fully differenced series (trend + seasonality removed)')
▸ Check stationarity on the fully differenced series 
adf_test(vastra_sales.Sales.diff(1).diff(12).dropna())
Output
p-value = 0.0 -> Series looks STATIONARY

Now the p‑value is tiny — the series is genuinely stationary. Aisha has removed both the trend and the seasonality.

Worth flagging

Here, it was visually obvious the seasonal cycle length (m) was 12, since Vastra Loop's calendar pattern repeats yearly. In other datasets it won't always be this obvious — finding the right value of m becomes its own small investigation.

Check yourself · 05
Why does Aisha apply both diff(1) and diff(12) to the series, instead of just one of them?

A single first difference removes trend but can leave a seasonal wave behind, and a 12‑month difference removes the yearly pattern but can leave drift behind. Combining both strips out trend and seasonality together, confirmed by the much smaller Dickey–Fuller p‑value afterward.
