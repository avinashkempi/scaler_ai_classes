The DailyMart Files

From shopping carts to smart recommendations

Meet Nisha. She has just joined DailyMart, a retail chain with stores across India, as a data scientist. On her first day, her manager walks up to her desk with a simple ask.

"Nisha, our sales have plateaued. We think customers are buying things, but we're not placing products smartly, and our app doesn't recommend anything personal to them. Can you fix that — with data?"

DailyMart, Day One

Nisha opens a fresh notebook and gets to work. Over the next few hours, her journey will take her from "which products go together in a basket?" all the way to "what should we show this specific customer on their app home screen?" This is her story, and the ideas she picks up along the way.

Start with the basket problem
01

The Basket Problem

Nisha's manager hands her a laptop with a folder called store_transactions.

"Suppose you are the data scientist here. We want to know: which products do customers tend to buy together? If we know that, we can place those products near each other, bundle them, or offer combo discounts."

DailyMart, briefing Nisha

This is a classic retail problem, faced by every store from a neighbourhood kirana shop to the largest supermarket chains.

QHow many products are we even dealing with?

At a large supermarket, products span many categories — daily essentials, food items, beauty products, toys, and more. Even so, that's usually a few hundred to a few thousand distinct products — tiny compared to an e-commerce catalog, which can run into the lakhs or millions. Call the total number of distinct products n, and the full collection of past purchase records — the transaction log — D.

QHow do we even approach solving this?

The transaction log is huge — hundreds of thousands of rows. There's no way to eyeball it and spot patterns manually.

Analogy Imagine trying to find out which two ingredients show up together most often across every recipe ever posted on the internet — by reading each recipe one by one. That's simply not feasible by hand. We need an automated, systematic way to scan through all the transactions and count co-occurring items.

Nisha decides the first step is simple: look at what the data actually looks like. Her dataset — a real, anonymized retail invoice log that mirrors the kind of data DailyMart itself would have — turns out to be sizeable.

02

The Apriori Algorithm

QHow can we systematically find the frequent item combinations?

The simplest idea: for every possible combination of items, count how many transactions contain it. Combinations with a high count are "frequent" — likely to reflect a real relationship.

QCan we optimize this instead of checking literally everything?
Analogy Imagine you're a detective trying to find suspicious duos who always show up together at the scene. Instead of cross-checking every possible group of people — which explodes very quickly as the group size grows — you first shortlist people who show up often enough on their own, and only then check combinations among that shortlist. If someone barely shows up at all, there's no point checking who they hang out with.

This is exactly the intuition behind Apriori: if an item — or item-set — doesn't appear often enough on its own, none of its bigger combinations can possibly be frequent either, so we can safely skip checking them. We define a minimum support threshold: a cutoff frequency below which we simply stop caring about a combination.

The Apriori algorithm was developed around 1994–95. It's elegant and simple — but as the number of products n grows, checking combinations still gets expensive fast (its worst case still scales roughly with 2n combinations). That's why, for catalogs with millions of products, pure Apriori isn't practical on its own — later algorithms like FP-Growth improve on the idea.

QDoes this idea only apply to retail?

Not at all — the same "frequent combination hunting" idea shows up well beyond the supermarket aisle.

Bio-informatics

If two gene sequences frequently occur together in a species, that hints at a biological relationship worth investigating.

Medicine

If two symptoms or two drugs frequently co-occur in patient records, that pattern is worth flagging for further study.

Nisha realizes this "shopping basket" trick is really a general-purpose pattern-finder in disguise.

QHow do we implement Apriori in code?

No need to write it from scratch — the mlxtend.frequent_patterns library ships a ready-made apriori() function. Nisha just supplies a min_support threshold and lets it do the heavy lifting.

That tells Nisha what's popular. It doesn't yet tell her what's strongly linked together — that's the next piece of the puzzle.

Quick check

Why does Apriori first filter out infrequent individual items before checking bigger item-set combinations?

Why: This is the core Apriori principle — if an item-set is infrequent, every superset containing it must be at least as infrequent. Apriori prunes away entire branches of combinations early, instead of blindly checking every possible group of products. That's what makes it smarter than brute-force checking, even though it can still get slow for very large catalogs.
03

Association Rules

Nisha now knows which item combinations are frequent. But her manager asks a sharper question.

"Okay, but if I place two products together, how confident are we that customers who buy one will actually buy the other?"

DailyMart, pushing further

This is where association rules come in — they measure the strength of a "bought together" relationship, not just how often it occurs.

Analogy Picture ordering at a pizza place. Both of these patterns exist in the data: {Pizza} → {Coke} and {Pizza} → {Garlic Bread}. Both are "true" in the sense that some customers do this. But intuitively, pizza + coke feels like a much stronger pairing than pizza + garlic bread. We need actual numbers to back up that gut feeling — that's what the three metrics below give us.

Support — how common is this combination overall?

Support just tells you what fraction of all transactions contain a given item or item-set — nothing more, nothing less. It's like asking: out of every 100 shopping trips, in how many did the customer buy both milk and bread?

Support(X) = transactions containing X / total transactions

This is the same idea Nisha already used as the "minimum support threshold" back in Apriori.

Confidence — if they bought X, how likely are they to also buy Y?

Confidence answers a directional question: given that someone already has item X in their basket, what fraction of the time do they also pick up item Y? It's like asking, of everyone who bought milk, what percentage also bought bread.

Confidence(X→Y) = transactions containing X and Y / transactions containing X
high support, low confidence

Looks common, isn't reliable

The pair shows up a lot in the raw data — but knowing someone bought X doesn't reliably predict they'll also buy Y.

high confidence, low support

Reliable, but rare

Whenever it happens, it happens together almost every time — it's just an uncommon pattern overall.

Both numbers matter, and neither alone tells the full story.

Lift — is this pairing actually meaningful, or just a coincidence of popularity?

Here's the catch with confidence alone: {Toothbrush} → {Milk} might also show high confidence — simply because milk is bought so often by everyone, regardless of what else is in the cart. Confidence alone can mislead when the "then" item is just generally popular.

Analogy If 95% of people you meet also happen to have eaten breakfast today, "meeting you" doesn't really cause or predict "eating breakfast" — pretty much everyone did, breakfast is just common. Lift corrects for this by comparing the observed co-occurrence to what we'd expect if the two items were totally unrelated.

Worked example — a store's records show:

600,000total transactions
7,500with Bread — 1.25%
60,000with Milk — 10%
6,000with both — 1.0%

If Bread and Milk were completely unrelated, we'd expect about 1.25% × 10% = 0.125% of transactions to have both. We actually observe 1.0% — eight times higher than chance would predict.

That "how many times more than expected" number is exactly what lift measures:

Lift(X→Y) = Confidence(X→Y) / Support(Y)
Lift < 1 — negatively associated Lift = 1 — independent Lift > 1 — positively associated
Two more metrics Nisha bookmarks for later

Leverage — similar to lift, but measures the absolute difference (not the ratio) between observed and expected co-occurrence. Useful because, unlike lift, it isn't skewed by very rare items.

Conviction — compares how often the rule is wrong (X happens without Y) to how often we'd expect it to be wrong if X and Y were unrelated. High conviction means Y really does depend strongly on X.

QWhat can Nisha conclude from DailyMart's real data?

Running the same calculation with mlxtend on the cleaned UK transactions, the top rule that emerges is:

14.6× {Green Regency Teacup and Saucer} → {Pink Regency Teacup and Saucer} — the highest lift in the dataset. A customer who buys the green set is 14.6 times more likely to also buy the pink one than a random customer would be.

The business strategy Nisha can now propose to her manager:

Item placement

Put the green and pink sets near each other for easy access.

Bundling

Sell them together at a small discount.

Targeted discounts

Offer a coupon for the pink set at checkout, whenever the green one is purchased.

Quick check

A rule {Umbrella} → {Bottled Water} has a high confidence of 85%, but a lift of exactly 1.0. What should Nisha conclude?

Why: A lift of exactly 1 means the two items are statistically independent — buying the antecedent doesn't change the likelihood of the consequent at all, no matter how high the confidence looks. High confidence combined with lift ≈ 1 is the classic sign of a generally popular item inflating the confidence score, not a genuine relationship.
04

From Baskets to Individuals

Nisha presents her basket-analysis findings, and her manager is impressed — but pushes further.

"This is great for store layout. But our app shows the same homepage to every customer. Can we make it personal — like Netflix or Amazon does?"

DailyMart, raising the bar

This is a fundamentally different kind of problem. Market basket analysis found patterns across all customers, for one shopping trip. Now Nisha needs to find patterns for one specific customer, across their entire history, and predict what that individual is likely to want next. This is the world of recommendation systems.

QWhat exactly is a recommendation system?

A recommender predicts how much a specific user might like a specific item — a rating, or the likelihood of interaction — ranks all possible items by that predicted score, and shows the user the top ones. Netflix's "Because you watched…", YouTube's homepage, Amazon's "customers also bought," Spotify's Discover Weekly — all the same underlying idea.

QHow do we formally set this problem up?

Suppose there are n users and m items — products, movies, songs, whatever DailyMart sells. The goal: for each user Ui, suggest a ranked list of items they're likely to enjoy, based on historical data.

QHow do we represent all this data?

Picture a giant spreadsheet: every row is a user, every column is an item, and each cell tells us how that user interacted with that item — a star rating, a "liked" flag, a percentage of a song listened to. Call this matrix A, where Aij is user i's interaction with item j.

Analogy Think of it as a giant, mostly-empty attendance register, where every student (user) has attended only a handful of classes (items) out of the thousands on offer. If DailyMart's app hypothetically had a billion users and a hundred million products across categories, this matrix would technically have 1017 cells — but any single user has realistically interacted with maybe a thousand items. The matrix is almost entirely empty: what we call sparse.

Nisha's real task, then: how do we fill in the blanks of this mostly-empty matrix?

05

Collaborative Filtering

"Wisdom of the crowd"

QWe're just given this mostly-empty matrix A. How do we recommend items to a user from that alone?
Analogy This is exactly what happens when you ask a friend, "I loved this movie — what should I watch next?" You're relying on the idea that people with similar taste tend to like similar things — that's the entire philosophy behind collaborative filtering. No product descriptions, no demographics, just behaviour.
QHow do we even get a "profile" for a user or item out of matrix A?

Just like text can be represented as a "bag of words" vector, a user can be represented as a vector — one number per item, capturing their interaction with it. Two users with similar vectors have similar taste. Two items with similar vectors tend to appeal to the same kind of person.

Item-item similarity

Nisha already knows the items a given customer has bought — or rated highly — in the past. So what if she finds other items that are similar to those?

Analogy If a listener frequently plays Lata Mangeshkar, and we know from everyone's listening patterns that people who play Lata Mangeshkar also tend to play Kishore Kumar, then Kishore Kumar becomes a solid recommendation — even if this particular listener has never heard of him.

User-user similarity

Alternatively — what if Nisha finds other customers whose purchase history looks a lot like this customer's? Once she's found such "twins," she can recommend things they bought that this customer hasn't tried yet, using cosine similarity between user vectors to measure how alike two shopping histories are.

item-item similarity

Compare products to products

Stable when there are far more users than items, and item behaviour doesn't drift much over time.

user-user similarity

Compare people to people

Finds a "taste twin" directly — but recomputing similarity across a huge, fast-growing pool of users is expensive.

QWhen should Nisha use user-user vs. item-item similarity?
Rule of thumb When there are far more users than items (n ≫ m), and item preferences don't drift much over time, item-item similarity tends to be more stable and efficient — items don't change their "personality" as often as the fast-growing pool of users does.
Quick check

DailyMart has 50 million app users but only 4,000 distinct products, and product categories are fairly stable month to month. Which similarity approach should Nisha lean towards, and why?

Why: The rule of thumb: when n ≫ m (many more users than items) and item ratings don't shift dramatically over time, item-item similarity is the more practical and stable choice — you compute similarities among a small, slowly-changing set of items rather than a massive, constantly-changing set of users.

06

The Cold Start Problem

Nisha demos her similarity-based prototype, and her manager immediately spots a gap.

"This is great for existing customers. But what happens the moment someone downloads our app for the very first time? We know nothing about them yet!"

DailyMart, spotting the gap
Analogy This is like being the new kid at school on day one. Nobody knows your taste in music yet, and you don't have a friend group to be compared against. Everything about collaborative filtering breaks down here — there's no history, no vector, no "taste twin" to find.

Since a brand-new user's row in matrix A is essentially empty, there's no behavioural data to compute similarity from. Every recommender system needs some strategy for this exact moment — usually by falling back on other kinds of information, which is exactly where Nisha heads next.

07

Content-Based Filtering

Falling back on profiles

QHow can we get around the cold start problem?

A naive fix: just recommend the most popular items to everyone new. It works a little, but it isn't personal at all.

Instead — even though DailyMart knows nothing about how a new user behaves, it usually still knows something about who they are: signup location, device type, maybe age group. This is the idea behind content-based filtering: build a profile of the user (and of each item) using descriptive attributes, then match users to items with a similar profile, rather than relying purely on behaviour.

QWhat are the trade-offs?

Advantages

  • Doesn't need any data from other users — works from day one for a single user, so it scales easily
  • Can surface niche items that few other people are interested in, since it isn't relying on "the crowd"

Disadvantages

  • Item and user features often need to be hand-engineered — someone has to decide what attributes matter
  • Tends to over-specialize — if you only ever liked comedy, you may never get shown a great thriller
Quick check

Priya just signed up on the DailyMart app 5 minutes ago. She has zero purchase history, but she entered her location — a city known for a health-food-conscious population — during signup. Which approach can give her a reasonably personalized recommendation right away?

Why: Content-based filtering relies on descriptive attributes — location, declared preferences, demographics — rather than behavioural history, which is exactly why it's the standard fallback for the cold start problem. It can make a reasonable first guess for Priya using her city profile alone, before she's ever clicked "buy."

One more angle Nisha considers: if she already has feature vectors for users and items from content-based profiling, she could concatenate a user's vector with an item's vector and treat the known rating as the label — turning "predict the rating" into a standard regression (or classification, for a binary like/dislike) problem, solvable with the same tools she already knows.

Quick recap: the three flavours of recommenders

1

Content-based

Uses item and user attributes — location, category, gender, and so on. Great for cold start.

2

Collaborative filtering

User-user or item-item — works purely off behaviour: "people or items like this."

3

Hybrid models

Combine both, and are what most production systems actually use today.

User-user and item-item similarity are both flavours of the same collaborative-filtering family.

08

Matrix Factorization

The engine under the hood

Nisha's manager is happy with the prototypes, but asks one final, practical question.

"This is nice for small demos, but our real matrix has millions of users and products, and it's almost entirely empty. How do real companies actually fill in a matrix that huge?"

DailyMart, thinking at scale

This is where matrix factorization enters the picture — the technique that famously powered the winning solution of the Netflix Prize competition, 2008–09.

Analogy Think back to basic factorization — 6 = 2 × 3. Now imagine doing something similar for taste itself: every user's preferences can be described as a short "flavour profile" — how much they like comedy, action, spice, sweetness, budget-friendliness — and every product or movie can be described along the same set of flavour dimensions. A user's predicted rating for an item is then just how well their flavour profile matches the item's flavour profile.

Formally, the giant matrix A (n×m, users by items) gets approximated as the product of two much smaller matrices:

A[n×m] ≈ B[n×d] · C[d×m]

B holds a compact "taste profile" — d numbers — for every user, and C holds a compact "flavour profile" for every item. The number of hidden flavours, d, is usually much smaller than n or m — that's exactly what makes this compact and computable.

QHow does this help fill in the missing cells?

Here's the elegant part: Nisha doesn't need every cell of A filled in to find B and C. She can use the relatively small number of ratings she does have — say, out of 10 million cells, only 100,000 are filled — to learn good values for B and C using an optimization technique. In practice, this is done with stochastic gradient descent: nudging the profile numbers bit by bit until predictions match the known ratings as closely as possible.

Once B and C are learned, filling in a missing cell — say user 3's predicted rating for item 10 — is as simple as taking the dot product of user 3's profile with item 10's profile. No separate lookup needed; the model has learned to generalize.

A quick detour: PCA and SVD

Nisha's manager asks one last thing: "we used PCA in a previous project — is that related to this at all?"

QHow does PCA connect to matrix factorization?

Recall that PCA works with a covariance matrix, which must be square and symmetric — it's essentially another way of finding a small number of "hidden dimensions" that summarize a dataset well, very much in the same spirit as the flavour-profile idea above.

QWhat about SVD — Singular Value Decomposition?

SVD does something similar to PCA, but it doesn't require a square matrix — it works directly on a rectangular data matrix.

PCA

Needs a square, symmetric matrix

Like summarizing a story by its main characters — but it only works if the "cast" and "chapters" happen to be the same size.

SVD

Works on any rectangular matrix

A more flexible summarizer: a small number of "essential ingredients" — singular values — that capture most of what matters, discarding the rest as noise.

A special case, truncated SVD, is a powerful feature-engineering tool for exactly this kind of user-item matrix, and it's closely related to the matrix factorization idea above.

Quick check

Why does matrix factorization work well even when the ratings matrix A is 99% empty?

Why: Matrix factorization learns two much smaller matrices — B for users, C for items — using only the ratings that are actually known. Because these learned profiles generalize across the whole matrix, predicting a missing rating is simply a dot product of the relevant user and item profile vectors, with no need for that exact cell to have ever been observed.

Wrap-Up

Nisha closes her notebook and walks over to her manager's desk. What started as "which products go together in a basket?" has turned into a working plan for personalizing DailyMart's entire app home screen — one customer at a time.

01

Market basket analysis finds product combinations that occur frequently together across all transactions.

02

Apriori prunes the search space efficiently: if a small combination isn't frequent, no bigger combination containing it can be either.

03

Support, confidence and lift each answer a different question — how common, how predictive, and how meaningful beyond chance a pairing is.

04

Recommendation systems predict a personalized score per user-item pair, from a matrix that's almost entirely empty.

05

Collaborative filtering — user-user or item-item — works purely off behaviour: "people or items like this."

06

Cold start is solved by falling back on content-based filtering — profile attributes instead of history.

07

Matrix factorization compresses the giant matrix into compact user and item "profile" vectors, learned via optimization, enabling predictions for cells that were never observed.

08

PCA and SVD are closely related dimensionality-reduction ideas — SVD is the more flexible cousin, usable on any rectangular matrix.
