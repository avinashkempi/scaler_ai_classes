Building data relationships

Hierarchical clustering post read

One tree, every level of detail: how nested groups let brand teams read the broad view and category teams read the fine one, from a single fit.

The problem

Three segments or six?

Anjali at ShopSphere gave Vikram, head of customer relations, a segmentation that finally spoke the language of probabilities. Then two emails arrived on the same morning.

Brand marketing director: "For national campaigns we need three broad customer groups. Anything more is too much to plan around."
Category heads (electronics, fashion, grocery): "Three is far too coarse. To decide what to stock and who to target, we need five or six distinct groups."

Anjali's first thought was to run her model twice, with three clusters and with six. The results didn't line up: a customer in segment 2 of the three-way split could land in any of several segments of the six-way split. The two teams would get two unrelated pictures of the same customers.

Vikram sees it immediately: "What I want is for the six groups to be pieces of the three. Like chapters inside parts of a book. Brand marketing reads the parts, the category teams read the chapters, but it is one book."

K-Means and GMM both force you to fix the number of groups first, and every new K gives a fresh, unrelated answer. What Anjali needs is one tree, from which any level of detail can be read off afterwards. That method is hierarchical clustering.

1 · The idea

Why a tree?

K-Means and GMM answer "give me exactly K groups". Change K, and you get a new clustering, not a refinement of the old one. Hierarchical clustering builds a structure where groups are nested:

↑At the topEveryone is in one big group.
↕Going downGroups split into smaller groups.
↓At the bottomEach customer is alone.

Any horizontal slice through this structure gives a clustering, and a deeper slice refines a shallower one. We don't commit to K up front. We pick it afterwards by looking at the tree.

One tree, any K

A simulated dataset of five blobs. Slide K and compare hierarchical clustering (Ward) with K-Means. The check underneath tests whether every cluster at K sits entirely inside one cluster at K−1.

Two ways to build the tree

Agglomerative (bottom-up)Start with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merge the two closest clusters. This is the popular, widely used approach, and the focus of this post.
Divisive (top-down)Start with one cluster and repeatedly split it. The exact version is impractical: there are about 2n−1 ways to split n points into two groups. Real implementations use heuristics, such as repeatedly splitting with K-Means at K = 2 (bisecting K-Means). Divisive methods are much rarer.
2 · The algorithm

Agglomerative clustering, step by step

  1. Start: every point is its own cluster. With n points there are n clusters.
  2. Merge: find the two closest clusters and merge them. Now there are n − 1 clusters.
  3. Repeat until only one cluster is left.
Each merge removes exactly one cluster, so going from n clusters to 1 takes n − 1 merges.

The proximity matrix

To find "the two closest clusters" we keep a table of distances between every pair of clusters: the proximity matrix. For n points it is an n × n table with zeros on the diagonal. After each merge, the rows and columns of the two merged clusters are replaced by one new row and column, and the new cluster's distances to the others are recalculated, which needs a rule for distance between clusters (the linkage, covered below).

Let's do it by hand on five customers described by two features: two close pairs (A, B) and (C, D), and one loner E.

Code: merge function written by hand 
def agglomerate(points, linkage="single"):
    """Merge the two closest clusters again and again, and record each merge."""
    D = squareform(pdist(points))
    clusters = {i: [i] for i in range(len(points))}
    next_id, merges = len(points), []
    while len(clusters) > 1:
        best = None
        keys = list(clusters)
        for i in range(len(keys)):
            for j in range(i + 1, len(keys)):
                A, B = clusters[keys[i]], clusters[keys[j]]
                block = D[np.ix_(A, B)]        # all distances between members of A and B
                d = {"single": block.min(), "complete": block.max(), "average": block.mean()}[linkage]
                if best is None or d < best[0]:
                    best = (d, keys[i], keys[j])
        d, a, b = best
        merges.append(("".join(names[k] for k in clusters[a]), "".join(names[k] for k in clusters[b]), d))
        clusters[next_id] = clusters.pop(a) + clusters.pop(b)
        next_id += 1
    return merges
3 · Reading the tree

The dendrogram: the whole history in one picture

A dendrogram records every merge. Its leaves at the bottom are the individual customers. Going up, branches join when clusters merge, and the height at which two branches join is the distance between the two clusters at that moment.

  • Branches that join low are very similar. Branches that join high are dissimilar.
  • Long vertical lines mean "these groups were far apart when they merged", so they are natural places to stop.

Choosing K by cutting

Draw a horizontal line across the dendrogram. The number of vertical lines it crosses is the number of clusters. Cutting high gives few, broad clusters; cutting low gives many, fine clusters. That one tree supports every level of detail.

Dendrogram of five customers

Drag the cut line. Branches below the line are coloured by the cluster they belong to. The table is the linkage matrix for the chosen linkage.

The linkage matrix

scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage returns an (n−1) × 4 matrix Z. Each row is one merge:

ColumnMeaning
0, 1The two clusters merged. IDs 0 to n−1 are the original points. A cluster created by merge number m gets the ID n + m (so 5, 6, … in the toy example; 2500, 2501, … for ShopSphere).
2The distance (height) at which they merged.
3The number of points in the new cluster.

With average linkage and a cut at height 4, the lecture's result is clusters {A, B}, {C, D} and {E}. In the table above, IDs 5 and 6 are the clusters made by the first two merges (A+B and C+D).

4 · Distance between clusters

Linkage: what "close" means for groups

Between two single points the distance is clear. Between two clusters we must choose a rule, called the linkage. Let A and B be two clusters.

LinkageDistance between A and BBehaviour
SingleThe closest pair of points, one from eachCan follow elongated or curved shapes, but suffers from chaining: a thin bridge of points can chain two clusters together
CompleteThe farthest pair of points, one from eachCompact clusters of similar diameter; sensitive to outliers
AverageThe mean of all pairwise distances between points in A and points in BA compromise between single and complete
WardThe increase in total within-cluster variance that merging would causeMerges the pair that keeps clusters tight, much like K-Means. Tends to give compact, similarly sized clusters
Δ(A, B) = |A| |B| / (|A| + |B|) · ‖μA − μB‖²Ward linkage. (scipy reports a scaled square root of Δ as the height, but it grows with Δ, so the picture reads the same way.)
Common confusion. Average linkage is not "distance between cluster centres". Using only the centres is a different method (centroid linkage).

Same two datasets, four linkages

Two interlocking half-moons, and two round blobs joined by a thin bridge of noisy points. Each tree is cut into 2 clusters. Computed live on freshly simulated, smaller versions of the lecture's datasets; the numbers under each plot are the cluster sizes.

  • Moons: single linkage can follow each crescent because it only needs a chain of close neighbours. Ward, complete and average cut the moons with a roughly straight boundary, as K-Means would.
  • Blobs with a bridge: single linkage tends to be fooled by chaining: the bridge links the two blobs, so it may merge them into one huge cluster and split off a tiny group instead. Ward and complete, which look at overall compactness, separate the blobs more sensibly.
  • There is no best linkage in general. Single follows shapes but chains through noise. Ward is stable and compact but assumes round-ish groups. Choose based on the shape you expect, and check results. (These are typical results; check the plots from your own run.)

Nested by construction

Cutting the same tree into 3 broad groups and 5 fine groups, then cross-tabulating, shows each fine group has customers in one broad column only. That is the nesting guarantee: finer cuts of one tree always refine coarser cuts. It is exactly what Vikram asked for, and what running K-Means or GMM twice could not provide. (scipy and sklearn agree exactly on the five-group cut, ARI = 1.0, since they build the same Ward tree.)

Code: build the tree and cut it twice 
df = df.set_index("ID")
features = df.select_dtypes("number")
X = MinMaxScaler().fit_transform(features.values)
Z = sch.linkage(X, method="ward")            # (2499, 4)

def cut_height(Z, K):
    return (Z[-K, 2] + Z[-(K - 1), 2]) / 2

broad = sch.fcluster(Z, t=3, criterion="maxclust") - 1
fine  = sch.fcluster(Z, t=5, criterion="maxclust") - 1

ct = pd.crosstab(pd.Series(fine, name="fine"), pd.Series(broad, name="broad"))
(ct.gt(0).sum(axis=1) == 1).all()            # True: every fine group lies inside ONE broad group
6 · How many groups?

Evidence for the cut

Three lenses, as before: the gaps between merge heights, the silhouette, and business sense.

Gap rule. If the merge that takes us from K clusters to K−1 happens at a much greater height than the merge from K+1 to K, then the K clusters were truly distinct, and stopping at K is natural.

Gaps and silhouette by K

Switch metric, then hover or tab to a point. The silhouette is computed on the scaled customer features only, with no labels in the matrix.

Hover or tab to a point.
  • A large gap and a high silhouette at the same K both point to a natural number of groups. If they disagree, the business need breaks the tie.
  • Here the largest gap is at k=3 (4.913) and the silhouette is highest at k=2 (0.269), with k=3 close behind (0.249). The gap at k=5 is tiny (0.066), so the five-group fine cut rests on business need rather than on a natural break.
  • For a tree we do not need a single answer. Brand marketing wants a small K and the category teams want a larger K, so we choose two cuts from the same tree.
Evaluation pitfall. In an earlier version of the notebook, cluster labels were written back into the same DataFrame that the silhouette function used, so the labels leaked into the features and inflated the scores. Keep labels out of the feature matrix when evaluating, otherwise the evaluation is meaningless.

A visual check

Projecting the fine segments to 2D with PCA and UMAP gives a view of the tree's structure. If each fine segment forms a visibly distinct region, and the fine segments of one broad group sit near each other, the tree matches the structure in the data. Overlaps at the borders are normal, and for those customers the segment is a judgement call. Remember that gaps and sizes in UMAP pictures are not meaningful.

9 · Honest limits

What the tree cannot do

1. Cost grows quickly with data size

The proximity matrix stores a distance for every pair of points, so memory grows as O(n²). A straightforward implementation takes O(n³) time (about n merges, each scanning a matrix of size n²). Priority queues reduce this to O(n² log n), and optimised libraries get close to O(n²) for common linkages, but memory stays quadratic.

When n doubles, time grows by a factor of about 4 or more (the measured ratios are irregular, which is normal for small timings). Memory explains why hierarchical clustering is practical for thousands of customers but not for millions. For ShopSphere's real customer base, Anjali would cluster a sample or a set of pre-clustered summaries (for example, many small K-Means clusters), and then build the tree on those.

2. Merges are greedy and cannot be undone

Once two clusters merge, they stay together, even if a later merge would have suggested a better arrangement. Nothing in the algorithm revisits earlier decisions, and no global objective is optimised by the whole tree. (Ward greedily minimises the variance increase at each step, but does not guarantee the best clustering overall.)

3. Noise and outliers

Outliers either merge very late (complete, Ward, average) and appear as tiny branches, or distort results through chaining (single). Cutting the ShopSphere tree into 12 groups:

Cluster sizes at 12 groups

Dashed line: 2% of customers (50). No group falls below it; the smallest has 87 customers (3.48%).

Hover or tab to a bar.

Small branches that stay separate until very high in the tree are unusual customers. The tree handles them by giving them a branch of their own or attaching them to a group, but it has no way to say "this customer belongs to no group". They remain part of the tree.

4. No predict for new points

Hierarchical clustering produces labels for the customers it was fitted on, and nothing else. A new customer cannot be dropped into the tree without refitting, or without an extra rule such as "assign the new customer to the nearest cluster centroid". Refitting changes the tree, and with it the segments, so a common practical approach is nearest-centroid assignment to the existing segments, refitting the tree periodically.

10 · Wrap-up

Back to Vikram's desk

Anjali presents one tree to both teams.

Brand marketingReads the tree at the top: three broad segments for national campaigns.
The category headsRead it lower down: five finer segments, each sitting inside exactly one of the three broad ones. The two views never contradict each other.

Then the fraud team reports a new pattern: resellers and scripted bots sit right next to real customers in feature space, and form odd-shaped trails, not neat round groups. Anjali sees that two things are needed: a method that can follow curved, irregular shapes, and one that can say "this point is noise". Neither Ward nor K-Means nor GMM can.

Key takeaways

  • Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of nested groups, so K is chosen after fitting, by cutting the tree.
  • Agglomerative (bottom-up): start with n clusters and merge the two closest, for n−1 merges. Divisive (top-down) splitting is rarely used, because exact splitting is intractable.
  • The proximity matrix holds all pairwise distances. The dendrogram records every merge, with the height equal to the distance at which two clusters merged. Cutting at a height gives a clustering.
  • Linkage defines distance between clusters: single (closest pair, can chain), complete (farthest pair), average (mean of all pairwise distances), Ward (smallest increase in within-cluster variance, compact and K-Means-like).
  • A finer cut of one tree always refines a coarser cut. K-Means and GMM at different K do not give this.
  • Limitations: about O(n²) memory and at least quadratic time, so it is impractical for very large data. Merges are greedy and irreversible. It is sensitive to noise and outliers, and has no predict for new points.
  • Keep labels out of the feature matrix when evaluating.

Where this leads

Finding dense regions and spotting noiseThe fraud team's problem calls for DBSCAN, which finds clusters as dense regions of any shape and explicitly labels points in sparse areas as noise, with no need to choose the number of clusters in advance.
