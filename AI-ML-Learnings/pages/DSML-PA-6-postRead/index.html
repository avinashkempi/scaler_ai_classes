Setting the scene

A Problem With No Dataset Attached

🎮 The manager's ask

"Games 24/7 is launching a new shooting-game tournament. Winner gets a lakh, runner-up gets 50k. What should we charge players to enter?"

"I need a number by tomorrow — and there's no historical data, because the game doesn't exist yet."

There's no query to run. No table to SELECT from. And yet a confident, defensible number is expected by morning — not a shrug, not "we need more data."

A guesstimate is not a wild guess, and not a statistically rigorous estimate either — it's the thing in between: built from logic, a few reasonable assumptions, and the courage to reach a number and defend it.

The what & the why

What Is a Guesstimate — and Why Interviewers Love It

Definition

An informed guess where there's enough information to eliminate a wild guess, but not enough to make a statistically sound estimate.

Two tools crack almost any of them: a feel for large numbers, and the ability to make rough, common-sense estimates from a few basic facts.

Why interviewers ask

They probe a blend of mental math, logical thinking, problem-solving, and background knowledge.

Like the product-sense rounds from Lecture 1, there's no single correct number. Two candidates can land on very different figures and both score well — the reasoning chain is judged, not the digit at the end.

The method

The Guesstimate Approach

A repeatable loop: Clarify → Analyze → Breakdown → Calculate → Validate. Step through it.

1
Step 1 of 5

Clarify the problem

Guesstimate questions are often vague. Always clear up your doubts before you calculate — take buy-in for assumptions, but never ask questions that hand you the answer.

"Flights from Delhi?" — clarify first
  • →Passenger, cargo, or both?
  • →Domestic or international?
  • →Only IGI, or Safdarjung / Hindon too?
2
Step 2 of 5

Analyze what you have

Take stock of the information already in hand and work out how it can be used to solve the problem — before inventing new assumptions.

Anchor against references you know. For the game, that means real tournaments: BGMI's pool prize ≈ ₹1.5 Cr, CoD similar — a sense-check before any maths.

3
Step 3 of 5

Breakdown into segments

Break the big question into subproblems. Estimating on a segment level is far more accurate than guessing the overall. Smaller units are easier to deal with.

Delhi flights, broken down
Local / international / layoverPassenger / cargoPeak / normal / non-op hoursWeekday / weekend
4
Step 4 of 5

Calculate with assumptions

Note assumptions and use them as thumb rules. Numbers needn't be exact — give a range. Every assumption must be defendable with logic (a toothbrush lasting 6 vs 4 months changes the whole answer).

🌟 The golden rule: beautiful numbers
18,686,902→20,000,000

Round for easy math; work in percentages; give the most weight to the 15–40 group in a young country.

5
Step 5 of 5

Validate

Consolidate the pieces, then sense-check the total against the business metric and your experience. Account for edge cases. Answer in ranges, not false precision.

Think out loud, drive toward a conclusion through increasingly specific analyses — and keep validating intermediate numbers along the way.

The assignment

Games 24/7 — Price the Tournament Entry

Games 24/7 is launching a new first-person shooting game (new map, new features). As their Product Analyst, set a pricing strategy where the winner gets ₹1 lakh and the runner-up ₹50k per event.

What should the per-game entry price be for a single participant?

Checks it tests
  • How structured is your approach?
  • Comfort with estimating numbers
  • Back-of-mind calc & validation
Prerequisites
  • Market share of the game
  • Region of the estimate
References (anchors)
  • BGMI pool prize ≈ ₹1.5 Cr
  • CoD similar scale
  • Our prize money is fairly low vs these
⚓ Anchoring against BGMI & CoD before crunching a number is the guesstimate version of a sense-check. If your final entry fee implied a bigger-than-BGMI prize pool, that's a red flag before you finish the maths.

Logic building — the business model

Arcade mode (free)
  • Practice lobby, no entry fee
  • No prizes — used to upskill & climb rank
Tournament mode (paid)
  • Open challenge; players pay an entry fee
  • This is the fee we're pricing

Royalty Cards: paid aesthetic upgrades (weapon skins, outfits) for both free & cash players — purely cosmetic, no gameplay advantage. Thumb rules: event is in India; individual participants only (no teams).

Quiz 2 · Balancing the guesstimate equation
The entry price x is solved by setting Total Income equal to Total Expenditure. Why is this the right way to price the fee, rather than picking a number that "feels right"?
Answer: B. A guesstimate should be defendable with logic, not a gut feeling. Setting income = expenditure ties the price to what the business genuinely needs to break even, given its other revenue (Royalty Cards, ads) and its costs (prize money, maintenance, servers, promotion). That's the "Calculate" and "Validate" steps working together.

Same equation, different scale (established game)

Only the assumptions change — the approach is identical. Bigger user base, more tournaments, higher fixed costs… and yet the fee drops.

₹45
Newly launched · 5k users · 1 tournament/day
₹15
Established · 25k users · 3 tournaments/day
# Established: 25k users, 25% paid = 6.25k, 3 tournaments/day Income = 562,500·x + 62.5 L (royalty) + 30 L (ads) = 562,500·x + 92.5 L Expense = 135 L (prize) + 1.5 L (promo) + 15 L (maint) + 25 L (server) = 176.5 L 562,500·x = (176.5 − 92.5) L = 84 L x ≈ ₹15
🔎 Scale changed almost everything — more users, more tournaments, higher fixed costs — but the price fell, because a much larger paying base spreads the fixed costs over far more players. The answer wasn't obvious until we built it up piece by piece.
Market-sizing · the funnel pattern

How Many iPhone Users Are There in India?

🧩 Analogy
🚪
Market sizing = a crowd through gates
Everyone enters; each gate lets only a fraction through
Start with the whole population, then pass it through gate after gate — old enough, can afford it, actually chose this brand. Because each gate acts only on whoever survived the last one, the fractions multiply, they don't add.

Quiz 3 · Market-sizing funnels
In the iPhone guesstimate, why do we multiply successive percentages (eligible × can-afford × market-share) instead of adding them?
Answer: B. Each step asks "of those remaining, what fraction qualifies next?" — 60% of everyone, then 70% of those, then 8% of those. That's a chain of conditional filters, which compounds multiplicatively (1B × 0.6 × 0.7 × 0.08 = 33.6M), not additively.
Market-sizing · the stock-to-flow pattern

How Many Refrigerators Are Sold in India Each Year?

🧩 Analogy
🛁
Stock-to-flow = a bathtub
The tub is what's owned; the trickle is what sells each year
A big stock of fridges already sits in homes. Each one lasts ~12 years, so every year a slice wears out and must be replaced — that steady trickle out (and top-up in) is the annual flow. Sales per year ≈ total owned ÷ lifespan.
🛁
Water in the tub
→
Total fridges owned (≈ households)
⏳
How long each lasts
→
Replacement period ≈ 12 years
💧
The yearly trickle
→
Annual sales = stock ÷ lifespan
🏷️
Whose trickle it is
→
Split by manufacturer market share
Worked example — one leading manufacturer
# Households: 248 M · replacement: 12 yrs · one maker's share: 30% (30% × 248 M) ÷ 12 = 74.4 M ÷ 12 = ≈ 6.2 M sold/yr

Then repeat for each major manufacturer (Samsung, LG, Whirlpool…) and sum for a rough national total.

Factors that sharpen it
Total households (~248 M, 2022) Replacement period (10–15 yrs) Manufacturer market shares Single-door vs double-door mix Regional preferences Economy & tech advances

Pattern recognition is the skill: iPhones used a narrowing funnel (population → eligible → afford → share). Refrigerators use a stock-to-flow pattern (total owned ÷ replacement period). Spotting which one fits a question is half the battle.

Interview corner

Practice Questions

Frame your own answer first — then reveal the approach.

Q1 — How many pizzas are sold in Bengaluru in a day?
Funnel pattern
Clarify: dine-in + delivery + frozen? city population scope?
Funnel: pop ≈ 1 Cr → % who eat out/order → % who pick pizza on a given day → avg pizzas per order.
Validate: sense-check against number of outlets × avg daily orders; answer as a range.
Q2 — How many cars are sold in India per year?
Stock-to-flow
Stock: households × % that own a car = cars on the road.
Flow: stock ÷ avg replacement (~10–12 yrs) = replacement sales; add first-time buyers for growth.
Sense-check: compare to known annual auto-sales scale.
Q3 — Why answer a guesstimate as a range, not a single exact number?
Concept
Every input is an assumption with uncertainty; a single figure implies false precision.
A range (e.g. market share 1.5–2.5%) honestly reflects that, and shows you understand where the estimate is sensitive.
Q4 — An assumption you took buy-in on turns out shaky. What do you do mid-answer?
Defending assumptions
State the sensitivity out loud (toothbrush at 6 vs 4 months changes everything), then flex the number and show how the answer moves.
Interviewers reward the awareness and the recovery — the reasoning chain matters more than the digit.

Key Takeaways

1
A guesstimate lives between a wild guess and hard data

Built from logic and defendable assumptions. There's no single right answer — the reasoning chain is judged.

2
Clarify → Analyze → Breakdown → Calculate → Validate

Same "clarify then break into pieces" instinct as RCA. Segment-level estimates beat overall guesses.

3
Price by balancing income against expenditure

Set income = expenditure and solve for the fee — ₹45 for the new game, ₹15 for the established one (scale spreads fixed costs).

4
Market sizing has two patterns

A narrowing funnel (multiply filters — iPhone → 33.6M) and stock-to-flow (total owned ÷ lifespan — refrigerators).

5
Use beautiful numbers, ranges, and buy-in

Round for easy math, answer in ranges, take assumption buy-in, and always sense-check against a known anchor.
