The problem that changes shape

In Class 1, you met Meridian Retail.

What looked like one problem—a support assistant behaving badly—quickly became several problems at once. A customer was told that an unresolved issue was fixed. A support agent saw information they should not have seen. Similar questions produced inconsistent answers. And leadership wanted to know whether the pilot should continue.

Now imagine that you are the engineer called into that situation.

Where do you begin?

You could inspect the software. You could check what information the assistant received. You could trace how customer data moved through the system. You could speak with the support team to understand what really happened. You could help leadership decide whether the workflow should remain paused.

All of those answers can be reasonable.

That is what makes this kind of work difficult. The customer may describe one problem, but solving it can demand several different kinds of ability. The work refuses to remain inside one neat box.

The engineer who solved the wrong problem

Consider a smaller situation.

A support team says its AI assistant is not being used enough. The obvious assumption is that the answers are not accurate. So an engineer spends two weeks improving the instructions, testing different models, and making the responses better.

The answers improve. Usage does not.

Eventually, someone sits beside a support agent and watches the real process. The reason becomes obvious: checking the assistant’s answer requires opening three different systems and copying information between them. The response was rarely the problem. The workflow around it was.

The engineer improved the part that was easiest to see, but not the part that was stopping adoption.

This pattern appears everywhere. A system can work technically and still fail because it does not fit the user’s workflow. It can produce good answers and still be unsafe because the wrong information reaches it. It can pass a demo and still be impossible to operate when something goes wrong. It can solve the immediate customer request and still leave nobody clear about ownership, risk, or the next decision.

Strong Forward Deployed Engineers learn to notice the whole shape of the problem before deciding what kind of work it requires.

One situation, several capabilities

Suppose Meridian asks you to help improve its support-assistant pilot.

During the same week, you may need to:

  • understand what the support team is actually trying to achieve;
  • build or change part of the software;
  • decide which behaviour should come from AI and which should be enforced by ordinary rules;
  • connect the solution to the right data while respecting access boundaries;
  • test what happens when information is missing or systems fail; and
  • explain to leadership what has been learned, what remains uncertain, and what should happen next.

These are not six unrelated jobs. They are different capabilities appearing inside the same customer outcome.

That does not mean an FDE must be the deepest expert in everything. It means they must recognise which kind of problem is in front of them, know which capability should lead the next move, and understand when deeper expertise is needed.

What capabilities allow an FDE to move through a customer problem that refuses to stay inside one technical box?

The five lenses

In Class 2, we will organise the work into five capability lenses:

01

Engineering

What must be built, tested, debugged, or made reliable?

02

AI & Machine Learning

How should the AI behave, and how will we judge whether that behaviour is good enough?

03

Integration

Which systems, data, identities, or permissions must connect correctly?

04

Delivery

What is the real problem, what should happen next, and who owns it?

05

Communication

What does another person need to understand or decide from the work?

Do not treat these as five departments or five separate job descriptions. In real work, several lenses may be active at the same time. One may help you decide where to start; another may reveal why the first answer was incomplete.

Class 2 will make this map concrete. You will see how a small customer situation can expose questions about expected behaviour, missing information, sources of truth, safe failure, human judgment, and production readiness.

The aim is not to memorise a framework. It is to start seeing problems differently.

What will Class 2 do?

Class 2 is a working session. By the end of it, you should be able to:

  • recognise the major capability families involved in FDE work;
  • identify which capability should guide the next move in a customer situation;
  • see why a technically successful prototype is not the same as a production-ready system;
  • understand how security, operations, and discovery cut across the work;
  • locate the capability where you already have evidence of strength; and
  • choose one area you want to develop next.

You will not be expected to arrive as an expert across all five areas. Nobody does. The more useful question is whether you can build enough breadth to recognise the whole problem while developing credible depth in one or two areas.

What to bring to class

Nothing to install. Nothing to submit. Bring three things in your head.

01

Think of a project that was technically correct but was still difficult for people to adopt. What got in the way—the software, the workflow, the data, the ownership, the communication, or something else?

02

Return to Meridian’s support-assistant incident. If you were called in tomorrow morning, what would be your first move? Hold onto that answer. During the class, you may find that your instinct reflects a capability you already possess.

03

Think about your own experience. Where can you point to real evidence—not just familiarity—that you can independently solve a problem? It might come from engineering, testing, integration, customer discovery, delivery, or communication.
