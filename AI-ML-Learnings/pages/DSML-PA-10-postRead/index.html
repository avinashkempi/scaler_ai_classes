Setting the scene

"Can We Just Ship It to Everyone?"

The last two lectures gave us segments to target. This one answers the next question every product team faces: you've built a new feature — how do you prove it actually works before it reaches everyone?

🎨
The design team

"We want to let people add coloured gradient backgrounds to their Facebook status updates — we think it'll make people post and engage more. Can we just ship it to everyone?"

Aditi's answer: "let's find out, properly"

"We think it'll help" is a hypothesis, not a fact. Ship it to 100% of users on a hunch and, if it backfires, it backfires on everyone — with no clean control group left to compare against.

Treatment (50%)

Gets coloured backgrounds

Control (50%)

Current status box

The difference between them, measured rigorously, is the evidence for whether to launch.

What carries over: the North Star / guardrail pairing from AAAERR, and the Clarify-first instinct from every framework so far (CRIED, the guesstimate loop, RFM). A/B testing turns that same instinct into statistical proof.

Step 1 · The case

Clarify the Goal

Case study

How would you test if Facebook adding coloured backgrounds to statuses improves user engagement?

Every A/B test starts the way every guesstimate and RCA did: clarify before touching a single metric.

🎯 The objective

What do we hope to achieve with this feature?

🤔 Why this feature

Why this specific update for that goal, and not another feature?

🔁 Prior evidence

Has this been tried before? Are other product lines doing it? Is the hypothesis backed by past experiments, industry insights or reports?

👥 Who it's for

Is the feature for a specific user group, or for everyone?

🧭 These are the same "clarify" instincts from Product Strategy's CEO/PM/Data-Scientist conversation and the guesstimate approach: understand the business context and objective before proposing a solution. A/B testing doesn't skip that step — it adds statistical rigour on top of it.
Step 2

Discuss the Metrics

List the metrics the feature should move and the data available — then pick the one metric you'll test significance on. For coloured backgrounds, the primary impact is on engagement.

Definition

User engagement

% of active users who engaged with Facebook in some way — likes, comments, saves, reactions.

Definition

Daily active users (DAU)

Number of unique users who log on to Facebook each day. We expect it to rise with the new feature.

⭐ North Star
% of users with engagement — the metric we test significance on
Supporting
Daily active users
🛡️ Guardrail
Must not degrade for the feature: % of media content (assumed to give more value), or the average number of posts people interact with — big coloured statuses take up space and could crowd out other posts
Step 3a · Hypothesis testing — just enough for A/B testing

Is the Gap Real, or Just Luck?

One question drives this whole section: is the gap between treatment and control real, or just chance? Intuition only — no formulas.

1. Why we can't just compare two numbers

Should we launch? Not yet. Even if the feature did nothing, two random groups never match exactly. This natural wobble is random noise.

Flip two fair coins 100 times each and you might get 52 vs 47 heads. Neither coin is "better" — the gap is pure chance.

2. Null vs alternate hypothesis

Null (H₀)

The feature has no effect; any gap is noise.
Coloured backgrounds don't change engagement.

Alternate (H₁)

The feature changes the metric.
Coloured backgrounds change engagement.

⚖️ Like a courtroom: we assume "no effect" (innocent) and reject it only when the evidence is strong enough.

3. The p-value and the 0.05 rule

p-value: the probability of seeing a gap this big just by random chance, if the feature had no real effect. The smaller it is, the stronger the evidence that the effect is real. You don't calculate it by hand — a tool does it, and how it's calculated comes in a later module.

p-valueMeaningDecision
< 0.05A gap this big rarely happens by chanceStatistically significant — reject H₀, the effect is real
≥ 0.05A gap like this often happens by chanceNot significant — no evidence the feature works

The 0.05 cut-off is the significance level: 5%, i.e. 95% confidence.

4. Z-test for proportions — the tool that gives the p-value

A statistical test turns the data into a p-value. There are several (Z-test, t-test, chi-square and more); which one you use depends on what you're comparing. Use the Z-test for proportions when comparing two percentages — engagement %, conversion rate, CTR, open rate. It compares the gap between groups against the gap noise alone would create. Excel, Python or an online calculator does the maths; you supply the data and read the p-value.

Scenario (40K users / group)ControlTreatmentp-valueDecision
A45.0%46.2%≈ 0.001Significant — move to the launch discussion
B45.0%45.5%≈ 0.16Not significant — the gap is within noise

5. Two settings you'll see in sample size

Significance level · 5%

The risk we accept of launching a feature that doesn't actually work.

Power · 80%

The chance we detect a real effect if one exists.

Quiz 1 · Treatment engagement is 45.4% vs control 45.0%, p-value = 0.25. The PM says "Treatment won, let's launch." What's the right response?
Answer: B. A result is significant only when p < 0.05. D is a trap: failing to reject H₀ is not the same as proving it — we simply don't have enough evidence of an effect.
Step 3b · Design the experiment

Hypothesis, Test, Sample, Duration

With the intuition in place, design the coloured-status test. Treat the statistical inputs as settings you specify; a tool does the calculation.

Set up the hypotheses

H₀: there is no significant difference in user engagement between the treatment and control groups.

H₁: there is a significant difference in user engagement between the treatment and control groups.

Choice of test

We're comparing two ratios (engagement %), so we use the Z-test for proportions.

Choose control & treatment subjects

Who is the experiment run on — all users on the platform, or a segment for whom the test is particularly well suited?

🎯 "A proper segment" is the RFM instinct from Customer Segmentation resurfacing: you wouldn't run this test on Hibernating or Lost customers who barely open the app — you'd pick a segment where a real behavioural signal is even possible to detect.

Sample size calculation

Baseline

User engagement before launch: about 45%

Minimum detectable effect

The smallest change worth acting on — stakeholders hope for a 1% increase

Significance

Usually 95% confidence

Power

Usually 80%

With these inputs, you'd need roughly 40K users in each group for a statistically sound experiment.

Experiment duration

Sample size = 40K × 2 groups = 80K users
Traffic ≈ 5K users per day (assumed)
Duration = 80K ÷ 5K = 16 days

Test significance on the North Star metric only after reaching the required sample size — and keep monitoring the supporting and guardrail metrics throughout.

Quiz 2 · The team could peek at results every day and stop the test the moment treatment is ahead. Why does the framework insist on running the full 16 days?
Answer: B. The sample-size calculation exists to guarantee a result at a chosen significance level (95%) and power (80%). Stopping early because an early lead looks promising is a classic trap — early data is noisy, and "peeking" inflates the chance of declaring a false winner. The 16 days are the evidence threshold, not a suggestion.
Step 4a · Experimental design bias

Primacy and Novelty: When Early Weeks Lie

Even a well-designed test can mislead in its first weeks. Two classic biases distort the treatment group's own numbers over time — in opposite directions.

Primacy effect · starts low

Users used to the old status UI are reluctant to change, so they post less at first. As they see others using coloured statuses, they slowly come round.

Judged on Weeks 1–2, the test could show no significant difference — even though the feature does get users more engaged once it stabilises.

Novelty effect · starts high

Some users get excited by the new feature and engage more for the first two weeks — then the excitement dies down.

Judged on Weeks 1–2, the feature looks like a bigger win than it really is.

Why it matters: neither effect is long-term, but both can exaggerate or understate the treatment result at the start. Don't compare the first two weeks against control.

How to handle it:

· Run the experiment longer than required, if possible, to see the effect wear off
· Run the test only on first-time users
· Compare first-time vs experienced users within the treatment group to estimate the size of the effect

Quiz 3 · Treatment engagement is 65% in Week 1, 68% in Week 2, then settles at 52% from Week 3. Which effect is this, and what would comparing only the first two weeks against control lead the team to wrongly conclude?
Answer: B. High-then-falling (65% → 68% → settles at 52%) is the novelty pattern: excitement about something new that fades. Reading only Weeks 1–2 overstates the feature's true, stabilised impact and leads to an overly optimistic launch decision.
Step 4b · Group interference

When Treatment Leaks Into Control

Primacy and novelty distort a group's own numbers over time. A trickier bias is when the two groups contaminate each other. Interference between variants is common, so the sample must be chosen so it can't bias the result.

Treatment user posts a coloured status
→
Their friend in control sees it
→
Control friend posts more too
→
Control engagement rises
→
Measured gap shrinks
Network effect

The positive effect in treatment spills over to control, so the measured difference underestimates the true effect.

The real lift may be more than 1%, but because actual effect > measured treatment effect, the test can wrongly conclude the feature didn't significantly move the North Star.

Outcome bias

Watch for other design or system issues that cause the measured treatment effect to understate or overstate the actual effect.

In practice: whenever a product connects people (feeds, friends, chats, marketplaces), treatment and control users are rarely fully isolated. That's why large platforms often randomise by cluster — by friend group or geography — rather than by individual user, to contain the spillover.

Step 5 · Recommendations

Launch or Not?

A significant result isn't a launch decision. Link the result to the goal and to business impact — then weigh the costs.

Worth it

A 1% lift in engagement that adds $20M in ad revenue is probably worth launching.

Maybe not

The same 1% lift adding only $50K may not justify the effort and cost.

When results conflict

The ideal outcome is a significant lift in the success metric with no change in the guardrails. Often it isn't that clean — for example, engagement among active users rises while daily active users fall:

MetricBeforeAfterChange
Daily active users5K3K▼ 2K users
User engagement45%65%▲ 20 pts

Translate it into impact on users and the business: is the higher engagement among remaining users bringing in enough revenue to balance the lost daily active users? If revenue rose despite the drop, the feature may be worth considering — with a strategy to retain daily active users as the next step.

Short term vs long term

⏱️ Short-term risk

A short-term rise in impressions can conflict with the brand image or the company's mission in the long run.

📈 Long-term view

Even with fewer daily active users, coloured backgrounds could bring more engaged users to the platform, and over time the benefit may outweigh the drawbacks.

⚖️ This final judgment — does the business case outweigh the cost, short-term and long-term — is the same conclude-with-a-recommendation habit from Product Strategy and RCA. A/B testing gives rigorous evidence; turning it into a launch decision is still a call the analyst has to make explicit.
Use cases

The Same Method, Four Different Products

In every case, the right metric is the one that directly reflects the job of the thing being tested.

🛒 E-commerce checkout button colour · Half the visitors see a green "Buy Now" button, half see a red one. What could be the goal?
Answer: A. A checkout-button test is about conversion: does one colour get more people to click and buy? Satisfaction and time-on-page are plausible side metrics, but not what a "Buy Now" colour test is designed to answer.
✉️ Newsletter subject lines · A media company sends two versions of its newsletter, each with a different subject line, to a subset of subscribers. What's the most appropriate action?
Answer: D. A subject line's job is getting the email opened, so open rate is the metric. Surveys measure something self-reported; changing font or frequency mid-test adds new variables that contaminate the experiment.
📄 Product page layout · One version shows the description at the top, the other shows customer reviews at the top. What's the most appropriate next step?
Answer: B. A layout test exists to find which arrangement drives more purchases; click-through and conversion are the direct behavioural evidence. Shares measure virality, and a preference survey is a subjective signal separate from what people actually do.
🎬 Streaming recommendations · One group gets recommendations from the current algorithm, the other from a new one. What's the most appropriate next step?
Answer: C. A recommendation algorithm's job is getting users to watch and enjoy more, so watch time and ratings are the direct measure. Counts or variety of recommendations say nothing about whether users acted on them; rivals answer a different question.

Key Takeaways

1
"We think it'll help" is a hypothesis, not a fact

Test on a treatment and a control group before shipping to everyone.

2
Clarify the goal before picking metrics

Objective, why this feature, prior evidence, and who it's for.

3
Test significance on one North Star — and watch the guardrails

Supporting metrics add detail; guardrails catch damage elsewhere.

4
A gap is only real if it beats noise

p < 0.05 means significant; p ≥ 0.05 means no evidence — not proof of no effect.

5
Sample size sets the duration — don't peek and stop early

Baseline, minimum detectable effect, significance and power give the sample; sample ÷ daily traffic gives the days.

6
Discount the first weeks

Primacy starts low, novelty starts high; wait for results to stabilise.

7
Watch for spillover between groups

Network effects make the measured effect smaller than the real one; randomise by cluster when users are connected.

8
Significant isn't the same as worth launching

Tie the lift to business impact, handle conflicting metrics, and weigh short- vs long-term effects.
