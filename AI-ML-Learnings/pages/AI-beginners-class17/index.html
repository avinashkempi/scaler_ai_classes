§ 1

Vibe Coding, Plainly

⏱ 12 min

What the phrase actually means, the three shapes it comes in, and — the part people skip — what you're left holding at the end of each one.

Vibe coding

Describing what you want in ordinary language, letting an AI produce the working software, and judging the result by whether it does the thing rather than by reading the code. The phrase was coined by Andrej Karpathy in early 2025, half as a joke — the idea being that you "give in to the vibes" and stop looking at the code at all.

It stopped being a joke about eighteen months ago. It is now a normal way that non-developers ship small internal tools — and a normal way that developers ship first drafts.

🍰
The Analogy

Three ways to end up with a cake. You order one from a bakery — fastest, arrives finished, and you cannot change the recipe. You bake it yourself with a chef standing beside you — slower, but it's your kitchen, your ingredients, and you can taste as you go. You hand a caterer a brief and go do something else — they plan it, make it, check it, and hand you back the cake plus notes on what they did. Same cake. Completely different relationship with it. Today you do all three.

1 · Chat in the browser
Lovable, AI Studio Build

You type a description into a website. It builds the app, shows you a live preview beside the chat, and hosts it for you at a real URL. You may never see a file.

You end up with: a link.
2 · Assistant in your editor
GitHub Copilot in VS Code

The files sit on your own laptop. The AI is a panel on the right. You ask, it proposes a change, you look at it, you accept or you don't.

You end up with: a folder.
3 · Agent-first
Google Antigravity

You give it a goal, not an instruction. It writes a plan, edits several files, opens a browser to test its own work, and hands you evidence.

You end up with: a folder, and a report.
🎙 Instructor Says

"Read those three footers again, because that's the whole section. A link. A folder. A folder and a report. That is what you own when the AI stops typing, and it decides almost everything else — whether you can hand it to somebody, whether you can fix it at 11pm, whether you can prove it works."

"People argue about which of these tools is best. That's the wrong argument. They're not competing — they're different shapes, and each one is embarrassingly bad at what the other two are good at. The next simulator makes that happen in front of you, so let's not take my word for it."

One honest limitation before we go further. Every tool today writes real software, and none of them understands your business. It will happily build a beautiful sign-up form that stores nothing, or one that stores everything forever with no way to delete it. Whether that matters is a judgement you make. We come back to this properly in §6.

SIM 1 · The Same Change, Three Places

Nikhil has already built the sign-up page. Now something needs to change. Pick a change request and watch what it actually costs in each of the three shapes — steps, minutes, and what you're trusting.

Pick a change request

Nikhil builds the sign-up page in a browser tool, and it works. A month later he wants to check whether anyone's phone number is being stored somewhere he didn't expect. What is the actual problem he runs into?

ABrowser tools can't store phone numbers at all, so there's nothing to check.
BHe owns a link, not a folder — so to answer the question he has to ask the tool and believe the answer, rather than look for himself.
CHe'd need to upgrade to a paid plan to see anything about his own app.
DNothing — a month is too long, the app would have been deleted.
B. This is the real trade in shape 1, and it has nothing to do with price. Chat-in-the-browser gives you speed by taking the files out of your hands. For a sign-up sheet for a book club, that's a fine trade. For anything where you might one day need to prove what the software does — with real people's contact details in it — "I asked it and it said it was fine" is not an answer. Notice that the fix isn't "never use browser tools." The fix is knowing, on day one, which shape the job needed.

Build 1 — Three Prompts, One Live URL

⏱ 18 min

Nothing to install, nothing to configure. In about ten minutes everyone in this room owns a working web address they can send to their mother.

🎙 Instructor Says

"We start here on purpose, and I want to be honest about why. Later today we install things, and installs go wrong — somebody's laptop is on the wrong version of something, somebody's office network blocks a download. That's normal and we'll handle it. Before the first prompt: the credit budget

Lovable's free plan gives you five build credits a day, capped at thirty a month. Roughly, one message to Lovable spends one credit. That sounds tight, and it is — but it's tight in a useful way, because it kills the worst habit beginners have with these tools: firing off ten one-line messages in a row and burning an afternoon.

Today's budget5 credits · we are spending 3

Three prompts. Build. Fix. Ship. That's the whole session, and two credits held back for whatever goes sideways.

If you've already used Lovable today and your credits are gone, don't sit it out — open ai.studio/build instead and use the same prompts. It's Google's browser builder, it's free, it needs no card, and it's the same shape of tool. You'll get a slightly different app and exactly the same lesson.

Getting in

Go to lovable.dev and sign up free — email, Google, GitHub or Apple. You land on a dashboard with one big box in the middle that says "Ask Lovable to create…". That box is the entire product. Type into it and you're building.

Once it starts, the screen splits: chat on the left, live preview of your app on the right. The preview is real — click things in it, it responds. There's a device toggle above the preview to see the phone layout, which we're going to need.

This is the prompt that matters. Read it out loud in class before pasting it, slowly, because the shape of it is the lesson.

Why that prompt worked — five things it did that a bad prompt doesn't

It saidWhich stops the AI from
"a bookshop in Bengaluru… reading club"Guessing at a generic startup landing page with fake testimonials on it
"the next three sessions as cards… date, title, author, seats left out of 12"Inventing its own idea of what a session is, with fields you don't want
"when they submit, seats-left goes down by one"Building a form that looks perfect and does absolutely nothing
"if zero seats, show Full and don't let them sign up"Leaving out the one rule that caused the actual problem in the shop
"cream, serif, one orange accent, must work on a phone"Handing you a purple gradient with a rocket emoji, which is its default mood

The pattern to teach: who it's for → what's on the screen → what happens when someone uses it → what must not happen → how it should feel. Five lines. If a learner's app comes out wrong today, it will almost always be because one of those five was missing.

SIM 2 · Say It Better

Three real bugs in Nikhil's page. For each one you choose how to report it — and the preview above genuinely changes based on what you said. One of the four options makes things worse every time. Watch the preview, not the score.

https://chapter-one-reading-club.lovable.app

Which of these four messages is most likely to get a browser-based build tool to fix the right thing on the first try?

A"The form is broken, please fix it."
B"Fix the form, and while you're there make the design more modern and add a photo gallery."
C"I filled in the form and pressed Save my seat twice. Seats left stayed at 4 both times. It should have gone to 3, then 2."
D"Please review the entire codebase for bugs and fix all of them."
C — and it's worth naming exactly why, because this generalises far beyond today. C contains three things: what I did ("pressed it twice"), what happened ("stayed at 4"), and what I expected ("3, then 2"). A has none of them. B has one, buried under two new feature requests that will eat a credit and introduce fresh bugs. D asks for something no tool can meaningfully do and will produce a lot of confident-sounding changes to code that was fine. Same three parts you'd give a human colleague. Turns out the AI wants the same information a person does.

🎙 Instructor Says

"Now — I could build this in Lovable in four minutes and it would be very pretty. I'm deliberately not going to, and here's the reason: this thing prints. It goes on a shelf, in a shop, and Tara is going to want to tweak the spacing at eleven at night when she's got a stack of forty cards to make. "So this build has exactly one new idea in it, and it isn't a tool. It's this: the code was always real. It's a file. It sits in a folder. You can open it with a text editor and read it like a document. Whether you understand every line is a completely separate question from whether you're allowed to look."

Setting it up — four steps, about six minutes

  1. Make a folder on your Desktop called chapter-one. In VS Code: File → Open Folder, choose it. The left sidebar is now that folder — empty for the moment.
  2. Click the Extensions icon in the far-left bar (the four-squares one), search GitHub Copilot, press Install. Install GitHub Copilot Chat too if it doesn't come along automatically.
  3. Sign in with your GitHub account when prompted, and choose the free plan. No card. You get 2,000 code completions and 50 chat requests a month — we will use about four of the fifty today.
  4. Open the chat panel: the Copilot icon in the top bar, or Ctrl/Cmd + Alt + I. A panel opens on the right. That panel is the whole product, the same way that one box was the whole of Lovable.
If the sign-in loop misbehaves — and on locked-down work laptops it sometimes does — VS Code will offer to copy a code and open github.com in your browser. Paste the code there, come back, and it connects. If a learner's machine refuses entirely, put them next to someone whose worked. Watching this one is nearly as good as doing it.

🛠 Do This Live — 3 min

"Everybody open the file in VS Code and scroll through it. Don't read it, look at it. Find three things you recognise — the word 'STAFF PICK' will be in there, the number 9cm will be in there, the word 'Print' will be in there. That's it, that's the exercise."

"I'm not teaching you to code. I'm breaking the belief that this is a sealed box. It isn't. It's a document with rules in it, and you can find things in a document."

Then change one thing — and watch the diff, not the chat

Ask for something small: "Make the cards 10cm wide instead of 9, and put the picker's name in italics." Copilot doesn't just do it — it shows you a diff: the old lines in red, the new lines in green, side by side, with Accept and Discard buttons.

That moment is the entire reason this shape exists. In the browser tool, the change simply happens and you find out what it did by looking at the result. Here, the change is a proposal. You are the one who says yes.

Read a diff like this, without knowing the language: red lines are what it wants to remove, green lines are what it wants to add. You are not checking whether the code is elegant. You are checking one thing only — did it remove something I asked it to keep? That question needs no programming knowledge whatsoever, and it catches an astonishing share of real problems.

SIM 3 · Accept, Line by Line

Nikhil asked Copilot to "sort the cards by which shelf they're going on." This is what came back. Every line has a plain-English note beside it — click each line to keep it or drop it, then apply. Three of these should not go in.

shelf-talker.html — proposed change0 of 8 judged

You're not a programmer, and Copilot shows you a diff with fourteen changed lines. What's the most useful thing you can actually do with it?

ANothing useful — reviewing code requires knowing the language, so just click Accept.
BScan the red lines for anything being removed that you asked for, and look for anything mentioning an address, a key, or somebody else's website.
CCount the lines — more changed lines always means a worse suggestion.
DCopy the diff into a different AI and ask whether it's good.
B. There are two questions a non-programmer can answer about a diff as well as anyone: "is it deleting something I wanted?" and "is it sending my stuff somewhere?" Both are readable in plain English, because file names, web addresses and the words you typed yourself all show up as ordinary text. That's not the whole of code review — a developer would catch things you won't — but it is very far from nothing, and it's the reason we don't just click Accept. C is superstition: a big diff is often just tidy formatting. D isn't crazy, but it replaces one thing you're trusting blindly with another.

Getting Antigravity

Go to antigravity.google and download it — Mac, Windows and Linux. It's a full application, not an extension, and it looks a lot like VS Code because it's built on the same foundation. Sign in with a Google account. It's free in public preview for individuals, no card.

Do not quote a usage number in class, including whatever number you saw last month. Antigravity's free preview is metered by a quota that refreshes on a rolling five-hour cycle under a weekly ceiling, and Google has tightened it several times since launch. Open the usage indicator in the app on the day and read out your own live figure. Same rule we used for Gemini's free tier last class, for exactly the same reason: a confidently stated stale number is worse than saying "let's look."

Two surfaces, and it's worth naming both:

The Editor

Looks like an ordinary code editor with an AI panel — same shape as Build 2. Everything you just learned still applies here.

The Manager

Mission control. Start several agents on several jobs across several folders, then walk away and come back to read what they did. This is the part that's genuinely new.

🎙 Instructor Says

"Watch what changes about my typing here, because that's the lesson and it's easy to miss while everyone's admiring the interface."

"In Lovable I described a screen. In Copilot I asked for a change. Here I'm going to describe a problem and a test — what's wrong, and how I'll know when it's fixed. Then I'm going to shut up and let it plan. And the plan is the thing I actually review. Not the code. The plan."

The artifacts — what it hands back instead of a wall of tool calls

This is Antigravity's real idea. Rather than making you watch it think, it produces things you can actually check:

ArtifactWhat it's for
Task listWhat it intends to do, in order, before it starts. Your chance to say no.
Implementation planThe slightly longer version — which files it expects to touch and why.
ScreenshotsThe page as it actually looked after the change, not as it was supposed to look.
Browser recordingIt opening your page, clicking your buttons, and the result. This is the test you asked for, on video.
WalkthroughA written summary of what changed and how to check it — the thing you'd send to Sarita.

Why this matters more than it sounds: the hardest thing about letting an AI change several files at once isn't the changing. It's knowing what happened. Artifacts are the tool's answer to that, and reviewing them is a real skill — the same skill as reading a diff in §3, one level up.

If a learner's download is stuck or blocked, they lose nothing today. Have them watch a neighbour and spend the time on the simulator below instead — the plan-review sim is the transferable half of this section. The install is not.

SIM 4 · Approve the Plan

The agent has written its plan for Tara's request. Nothing has run yet. Approve, edit, or reject each step — and if something's missing, add it. Then let it run and live with what you allowed.

Anything missing? Add one step

An agent-first tool shows you a six-step plan before it starts. Why is that moment worth more of your attention than the code it writes afterwards?

ABecause the plan is written in code and the changes aren't.
BBecause the plan is in plain English, so you can judge it without programming knowledge — and it's the last point where stopping something costs you nothing.
CBecause agents can't change their plan once it's approved.
DBecause the plan is the only artifact the tool produces.
B, and both halves matter. The plan is the one part of an agent's work that's written in your language rather than the computer's — "delete the existing file and rebuild it" is a sentence anyone in this room can have an opinion about. And it's cheap: rejecting step 3 before it runs costs one click, while noticing the same problem after eleven files have changed costs you an afternoon. C is just wrong — agents revise plans as they go, which is exactly why you also read the walkthrough at the end.

The point of this section

In the six months before you sat down here, one of these tools was renamed, one was shut down with two weeks' notice, and one changed its free limits repeatedly. That is not chaos to be endured until things settle. That is the settled state.

Which is why this class is organised around three shapes and not three brands. A link, a folder, a folder and a report. When Lovable is replaced by something else — and it will be — you'll recognise the replacement in about ninety seconds, because you'll know which shape it is and therefore what it's good at, what it's bad at, and what you'll own at the end.

§ 6

The Ravi Line

⏱ 14 min

Three tools down. Now the part that turns this from a hobby into a professional skill: knowing what you should not build.

"I want to start this section by defending vibe coding, properly, because the next ten minutes are going to sound like a warning label and I don't want you to leave with the wrong impression."

Three questions before you ship anything

🩹
Question 1

Who gets hurt if it's wrong?

Nobody? Ship it. Me, and I'd notice within a day? Ship it. A customer, quietly, without anyone noticing for a month? Stop.

🗄
Question 2

What is it holding?

Nothing, or things you'd happily put on a whiteboard? Ship it. Other people's phone numbers, addresses, money, or anything you'd have to apologise for losing? Get it read.

🔌
Question 3

Can you turn it off?

If it goes wrong at 9pm on a Saturday, is there a switch, or does the shop stop working? Anything that other things now depend on has quietly become somebody's job.

Where the line actually falls

Build it yourself, todayBuild it — then have someone read itDon't build this
Internal forms and sign-up sheetsAnything with logins and accountsTaking payments yourself
Calculators and convertersAnything storing customers' personal detailsAnything medical, legal or safety-related
Rotas, checklists, trackersAnything that writes into another systemAnything where being wrong is expensive and silent
Prototypes to show someone an ideaAnything staff outside the room will rely onAnything that must keep running while you're on leave
The middle column is the one people forget exists. It isn't "give up and hire a developer." It's build the whole thing yourself, then buy a developer a coffee and get thirty minutes of their eyes on it. Ravi doesn't want to build Nikhil's form. He's happy to look at it for half an hour. That trade is available to almost everyone and almost nobody asks.

Two things that actually happened

◆ Reported · June 2025

The tables with no lock on them

A vulnerability catalogued as CVE-2025-48757 described apps built on a popular browser builder whose databases were being created without row-level security switched on — the setting that stops one user reading another user's rows. Researchers reported more than 170 live applications affected; in the worst cases, anyone with a browser's developer tools could read or delete records. Names, emails, phone numbers.

What to take from it: not "browser builders are unsafe." The apps worked perfectly. Every screen looked right. The failure was invisible from the front — and that's precisely the category the middle column exists for.

◆ Study · 2025

1,072 apps, scanned

A security firm scanned over a thousand publicly reachable vibe-coded applications and reported that 98% had at least one security flaw; only 26 came back clean. Common findings were the dull ones — keys left in the page, data readable without logging in, no limits on who could delete what.

What to take from it: these are not exotic mistakes. They're the defaults. An AI builds what you asked for and nothing you didn't — and almost nobody thinks to ask "and make sure strangers can't read the sign-up list."

So say it in your prompt. "Only I should be able to see the list of sign-ups; visitors should only be able to add their own" is a sentence you can write with no technical knowledge at all, and it changes what gets built. It doesn't replace the middle column — but it's free, and it takes eight seconds.

SIM 5 · Ship It, Get It Read, or Walk Away

Five things people ask Nikhil for over one term. For each one, pick a column from the table in §6 above — then the calendar jumps forward and you find out how it went. Both kinds of mistake count against you here: shipping what you shouldn't, and refusing what you easily could.

Each round scores 0–2 (best call = 2, defensible-but-costly = 1, miss = 0) — watch for the badge right after you click, before the calendar plays out.

Nikhil builds an internal page where the four staff log in and see their own shift history, including their phone numbers. It works perfectly. What's the right call?

AShip it — it's internal, only four people, and it works.
BShip it, but get someone who builds software for a living to spend half an hour looking at it first.
CDon't build it at all — anything with a login needs a professional from the start.
DShip it, and ask the AI to check its own work for security problems.
B. It has the two features from the middle column — logins and other people's personal details — and "only four people" is not the protection it sounds like, because the page is reachable by anyone who finds the address, not just by the four people it was meant for. That's precisely the shape of the June 2025 incident above.

But look at why C is also wrong, because over-caution is the mistake this course is more likely to cause. Refusing to build it means four people carry on with a paper sheet, and the actual risk — a login page reachable from the internet — was addressable with thirty minutes of somebody's attention. D is the seductive one: asking the AI to audit itself is genuinely better than nothing, and it will find some real problems, but you are asking the thing that made the mistake whether it made a mistake.
§ 7

Take-Home — Build Your Own Small Annoying Thing

⏱ 6 min

Three challenges, one per shape. Every one of them uses only what you opened today, and the thing you build is yours — the one you typed in the first five minutes of class.

C1

Ship your own thing to a real URL, in three prompts

▸
Mission

Take the spreadsheet / WhatsApp group / piece of paper you named at the start of class and build it in Lovable — or in ai.studio/build if your credits are gone. Three prompts, no more. Build, fix, ship. Write the first prompt using the five lines from §2: who it's for → what's on the screen → what happens when someone uses it → what must not happen → how it should feel.

Done when

You've sent the live URL to one other human being who wasn't in this class, and they used it without you explaining anything.

The bit that teaches you the most

Before you write prompt 1, write down what you expect to get. Then compare. The gap between those two things is your prompt-writing skill, and it closes fast once you can see it.

C2

Make one single-file thing you can open by double-clicking

▸
Mission

In VS Code with Copilot, build one small thing as a single HTML file, no frameworks, no build step — a calculator, a checklist, a name-picker, a label printer, anything. Then ask for one change and read the diff before accepting it.

Done when

The file is in a folder on your machine, it runs by double-clicking with no internet, and you have rejected at least one suggestion — even a small one. Rejecting something is the skill; accepting everything is the habit we're breaking.

Stretch

Open the file in a plain text editor — Notepad, TextEdit, anything — and find three words in it that you typed in your prompt. That's the whole "it's just a document" lesson, done with your own hands.

C3

Give an agent a goal, and reject at least one step of its plan

▸
Mission

Open your C2 folder in Antigravity and give it a goal in the §4 shape — a problem plus a test. "It loses everything when I close it. Make it remember. Check it by adding two things, refreshing, and showing me both are still there." Then read the plan properly before letting it run.

Done when

You've read its walkthrough at the end and can say, in one sentence, what it changed. Not what it claims it changed — what you can see it changed.

The real question to answer in writing

Two sentences: for your thing, which of the three shapes was actually right, and what would have gone wrong with the other two? "The first one was fine and the other two were overkill" is a completely valid answer — that judgement is the thing this class is really teaching, and it's the thing an employer is actually paying for.

If you're setting one of these as graded homework: C1 is safe for everyone and needs no install at all. C2 depends on VS Code and a GitHub account. C3 depends on C2 and a download that some managed laptops will block — so make it optional, or accept a written alternative: "describe the plan you would have approved, and the step you would have rejected."
One instruction to give the room out loud: do not build the thing you think will impress me. Build the thing that annoys you. Every learner who builds their own irritation finishes it; most people who pick an impressive-sounding project abandon it on day two.
§ 8

Wrap-Up — Key Takeaways

⏱ 6 min

Three things built, three shapes learned, one line drawn.

Six Things You Can Now Do

SkillWhat you can now do
Prompting for softwareDescribe an app in five lines that produce the thing you meant, not a purple gradient

Bug reports that workSay what you did, what happened and what you expected — and get it fixed first try

Key Takeaways

You're learning three shapes, not three brands. A link. A folder. A folder and a report. Two of the tools on this class's original list changed identity in the six months before you sat down; the shapes didn't move at all.

The first prompt does most of the work. Who it's for, what's on screen, what happens when someone uses it, what must not happen, how it should feel. Nearly every "the AI built the wrong thing" story is one of those five lines going unsaid.

A good bug report beats a good tool. What I did, what happened, what I expected. Three parts, same as you'd give a colleague — and it will save you more credits than any trick you'll read online.

You can review work in a language you don't speak. Is it deleting something I asked for? Is it sending my stuff somewhere? Is this step in the plan something I actually want? None of those need programming, and all of them catch real problems.

The failure you should fear is the invisible one. The app that looks perfect from the front and leaves the back door open. That's why the middle column exists — build it yourself, then get thirty minutes of somebody's eyes.

Refusing to build is also a mistake. This class will make some of you too careful. A form is a form. Ship the form.
