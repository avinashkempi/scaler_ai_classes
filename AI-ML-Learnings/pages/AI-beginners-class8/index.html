Why This Isn't "Just Ask ChatGPT"

Here's the beginner mistake this whole class exists to correct: treating every one of these three jobs as the same prompt into the same box. A partner-facing pitch deck, a formal season-readiness document, and a week of Instagram captions are three different products with three different failure modes. A deck that reads like a caption looks unprofessional in front of hoteliers. A caption that reads like a formal report gets zero engagement. And a document with one wrong number in it is worse than no document at all, because someone will act on that number.

Today you learn to reach for the right tool for each job — and, just as importantly, to check the output like an editor would, not just accept the first draft because it reads smoothly.

Why this matters for you — 2026 job reality

  • "AI content ops" is now a real, listed job function — someone who runs a content pipeline through several AI tools daily, not someone who writes everything from scratch.
  • Every one of today's four tools shipped a genuinely new workflow in the last year. Employers assume you know the current version, not the one from a 2024 tutorial.
  • The valuable skill is never "can you get an AI to write something." It's choosing the right tool for the content type, and catching what it got wrong before it ships.
  • Every tool today has a free tier that looks generous and a very specific number where it stops being free — same arithmetic habit from last class, different tools.
Insider fact — say this, it reframes the whole session: As of 2026, roughly 88% of marketers now use an AI tool daily for some part of content work — that's no longer the differentiator. Here's the gap that actually matters: only about 19% of them track any KPI on what that AI output actually did — no fact-check pass, no performance read, nothing. Everyone reached for the tool. Almost nobody built the discipline around it. That discipline is the entire second half of this class, and it's the part your resume can actually claim that "I used ChatGPT" can't.
Instructor — before you teach this live: All four tools here ship changes constantly, and two of them changed a core interaction model in the last few months — ChatGPT retired Canvas in favour of in-chat writing blocks (May 2026), and Canva folded four separate AI tools into one conversational Magic Studio box. Everything in this script was verified in August 2026. Open each product yourself the morning of class — this is exactly the kind of class where a stale screenshot gets noticed immediately.

§ 1

Framework — Structure, Substance, Style

⏱ 12 min

One framework for the whole class, same as last time. Three tools, three genuinely different jobs — and one question that sorts them.

✍️
The One Analogy

Think about three ways to bring in help with writing something. One: you hand a slide designer your bullet points and a deadline, and they build the whole deck's structure and visuals themselves — you review the finished thing. Two: you sit with a co-writer, passing a document back and forth, marking up the paragraph that's wrong and asking for a specific fix. Three: you hand a junior copywriter a stack of your best old posts and say "sound exactly like these, every time, for the next year." All three produce writing. They are not interchangeable, and which one you reach for depends on whether you need it structured, whether you need it accurate, or whether you need it to sound like you, consistently, at volume.

The One Question That Sorts All Three

"What's the hard part of this piece of content — how it's organised, whether it's factually right, or whether it sounds like us?" Whichever answer dominates tells you which tool to open. Gamma is built to solve organisation and visual structure. Claude and ChatGPT are built to solve depth and factual accuracy in prose. Canva's Magic Write is built to solve consistent voice, fast, at volume. Most real content jobs lean on more than one — a deck needs structure and a voice; a report needs substance and a bit of structure.

Tool Demo — Gamma

⏱ 20 min

Structure first. Kabir has four days, a full set of facts about the Solitude Peaks pitch, and zero design skill. Gamma's entire premise is "point me at the facts, I'll handle the visual thinking."

What Is Gamma?

An AI content platform that generates presentations, documents and simple web pages from a prompt, an outline, or existing material — decks are its headline use, but it's positioned as "more than slides." You give it a brief; it drafts a structured, multi-slide document in under a minute, picks layouts, and pulls in contextually relevant images. From there you theme it, edit it, and export or share it as a live link.

What changed — say this out loud: Since late 2025, Gamma ships an Agent layer on top of generation — a conversational editing mode where you type an instruction like "make slide 3 more concise and add a chart" and it executes across the whole deck, understanding context rather than treating each request as isolated. It's also web-connected, so it can pull in live research and visualise data you paste in, styled to match your deck. Generation itself hasn't slowed down — still a full draft in under a minute.

Nothing new was written for this — it's Farah's own notes from Sunday night, lightly organised. This is exactly the shape of brief Gamma expects.

Step-by-step demo (share screen)

Step 1 — Go to gamma.app, login and set up. Then go to Home, click Create new, and then Generate.

Step 2 — Paste the brief above into the input box and proceed. Gamma proposes an outline first — one line per slide. Review it before generating the full deck; this is the cheapest place to catch a missing or misplaced point.

Step 3 — Click Generate. In under a minute you have a full 7-slide draft with layouts chosen and images pulled in automatically.

Step 4 — Open the Agent panel and type a conversational edit — narrate this live, it's the newest and most useful part of the tool.

Step 5 — Apply Wanderline's brand kit (logo, colours, fonts) so the deck doesn't look like a generic template in front of a client.

Step 6 — Before exporting, open the numbers slide and manually check every figure against Farah's original brief — do not skip this, run SIM 4 here first.

Step 7 — Export as PDF for email, or share the live Gamma link — Kabir can see analytics on who opened it and how far they scrolled, useful before walking into the room Friday.

Gamma Danger — Free Credits Are One-Time, Not Monthly

Gamma's Free plan gives 400 credits, once, ever — they don't refresh, and free decks cap at 10 cards. Plus is $12/month ($8/month billed annually) for 1,000 refreshing monthly credits and 20 cards per deck. Pro is $25/month ($15/month annually) for 4,000 monthly credits, 60 cards per deck, custom branding, analytics and API access. A single image-heavy deck with several Agent edit rounds can burn a meaningful chunk of a free allowance in one sitting — treat the free tier as "enough to learn the tool," not "enough to run an agency's deck output." Exact per-action credit costs vary and shift with product updates — verify the live numbers before quoting a client a "free" deliverable.

Claude & ChatGPT Danger — Free Tiers Are Rate Limits, Not Message Counts

Claude Free gives access to Sonnet and Haiku-class models with roughly 30-100 messages per day, resetting every 4-8 hours — the exact number moves with how busy Anthropic's traffic is that day. Claude Pro is $20/month ($17/month annually) and raises the ceiling substantially, with all models unlocked. ChatGPT Free caps around 10 messages per 5-hour window on GPT-5.5 Instant before dropping to a lighter model, and now shows ads on some free-tier answers. ChatGPT Plus is $20/month, removes ads, lifts the limit, and unlocks GPT-5.5 Thinking and Deep Research. For a real content-ops job drafting several documents a day, the free tier of either tool runs out inside a single working session — budget for a paid seat the same way you'd budget for any other tool of the trade.

🗞️ Real Case — CNET, 2023

This isn't a hypothetical. Starting in late 2022, tech outlet CNET quietly published 77 AI-written financial explainer articles under the byline "CNET Money Staff" — no human writer credited, minimal disclosure. When another outlet noticed and started checking the work, CNET ran its own audit and had to issue corrections on 41 of the 77 articles — more than half. One single article on compound interest alone contained at least five significant inaccuracies. A few corrections even read "we've replaced phrases that were not entirely original," meaning some language had been lifted, not generated.

Notice what actually failed here: not the writing quality — reviewers said the drafts read smoothly and confidently, exactly like SIM 5's price-flip. What failed was the missing step between draft and publish. CNET didn't stop using AI afterward — it paused, added disclosure, and rebuilt a human fact-check pass into the pipeline. That pass is the whole point of this section, and it's cheaper to build in from day one than to retrofit after 41 corrections go public.

What Is Canva Magic Studio?

As of 2026, Canva folded four previously separate AI tools — Magic Write, Magic Edit, Magic Design and Magic Switch — into one conversational input box called Magic Studio. You type what you want in plain language and it routes the request to the right underlying model itself. Magic Write specifically now generates copy in under two seconds, supports 18 languages, and — the part that matters most today — can be trained on your own brand voice from past examples, rather than re-describing your tone in every single prompt.

🗞️ Real Case — Sports Illustrated, 2023

A different failure mode than CNET's, and worth knowing precisely because it's about voice, not facts. Sports Illustrated was found to be publishing product-review articles under bylines for writers who didn't exist — invented names, with profile photos bought from an AI-headshot marketplace. As the story broke, authors quietly vanished from the site and their articles were silently reattributed to other invented authors, with no editor's note explaining the switch. The publisher's parent company fired its CEO within days.

Notice this is the opposite mistake from a wrong number — nothing here was necessarily factually incorrect. The deception was in the identity, not the content. Training Magic Write on Wanderline's real voice is exactly the responsible version of this: the captions sound like Wanderline because they are Wanderline's own words, fed back in, published under the account that actually wrote them. The line this class draws is simple — use AI to sound like yourself, faster. Never use it to invent a "someone" who isn't.

Canva Danger — "Free" Covers a Surprisingly Small Number of AI Actions

Canva's Free plan bundles a limited number of Magic Studio AI actions per month alongside 5GB of storage — good enough to test the voice-training workflow, not enough to run a daily content calendar. Canva Pro is $15/month ($120/year), and raises AI usage substantially alongside 1TB of storage, brand kit and template locking. As with Gamma, the exact monthly AI-action ceiling on each tier moves as Canva iterates Magic Studio — verify the live number on Canva's pricing page the morning you teach this, the same way you would for any of today's four tools.

Quiz 4 · Why train Magic Write on Wanderline's old captions instead of just describing the brand's tone in words inside every new prompt?

ABecause describing tone in words is technically impossible for an AI tool to follow
BBecause training it once produces consistent voice across dozens of future posts without re-explaining the tone every time — and real examples capture nuance ("cheeky but plain English, concrete over generic") that's hard to fully spell out in adjectives
CBecause trained voice output requires no human review before publishing
DBecause untrained Magic Write cannot generate captions at all, only trained
Why B? A word-description of tone is lossy — "warm and cheeky" doesn't capture the exact rhythm and specificity of "0 regrets" as a closing line the way three real examples do. Training it once means every future prompt inherits that voice without re-briefing it, which is the entire point when you need volume. A is false — described tone works, just less precisely. C is dangerous — SIM 6 and the policy-reminder note both show trained output still needs a read-through. D is false — untrained Magic Write writes fine captions, they're just generic ones.
§ 5

Prompt Strategy — Tone, Audience, Format & Editing Like a Pro

⏱ 12 min

Not a new tool — a cross-cutting skill that makes all three of today's tools better. And a discipline for the moment after generation, which is where most of the actual professional judgment lives.

↩︎ You Have Done This Before — TAF Is RCTFC, Renamed For Content Work

Class 2 gave you five elements — Role, Context, Task, Format, Constraints. Content-generation prompts lean hardest on three of them, and content professionals have their own shorthand for that subset:

Content-prompt elementWhere it maps back to Class 2's RCTFC
TonePart of Format/style — how it should sound, not just what shape it takes
AudienceContext — who's reading this changes almost everything else about the prompt
FormatFormat — length, structure, medium, exactly as before

TAF isn't a new framework — it's RCTFC with Role and Constraints assumed (you already know you're briefing a copywriter, and the constraint is usually "don't invent facts"). Recognising the shape under a different name is the same transferable skill Class 2 built.

Same request, with and without TAF

VAGUE PROMPT

"Write something about our Kerala backwaters package."

→ Generic. Could be a blog paragraph, a brochure line, or a caption — the tool has to guess, and it guesses safely bland.

TAF PROMPT

"Write an Instagram caption for our Kerala backwaters package. Tone: warm, a little cheeky, plain English, one emoji max. Audience: young couples scrolling Instagram, not researching yet. Format: 2-3 short lines, no hashtags, one concrete sensory detail."

→ Specific enough to sound like a real post on the first try, because every ambiguous choice was made by you, not guessed by the model.

Key Takeaways

  1. Not every AI problem is a chatbot problem. Wanderline's content backlog needed structure, substance and style — none of it needed a conversation.
  2. One question sorts all three tools: what's the hard part of this content — organising it, getting it factually right, or making it sound like you at volume?
  3. Structure, Substance, Style. Gamma turns a decided brief into a visual document. Claude/ChatGPT turn messy notes into accurate prose. Canva Magic Write turns a trained voice into consistent volume.
  4. TAF is RCTFC, renamed. Tone, Audience, Format — the same shape you learned in Class 2, aimed at content instead of chatbots.
  5. Fluent is not the same as correct. Every tool today can produce a confident, well-organised, wrong sentence. SIM 5's price-flip is exactly what that looks like in the wild.
  6. The five-item editor pass is not optional. Facts against source, tone against audience, no invented data, binding numbers untouched, format matched to channel.
  7. Two of today's four tools changed their core interaction model in the last year — ChatGPT retired Canvas for in-chat writing blocks, Canva merged four tools into one Magic Studio box. Verify before you teach or pitch either.
  8. Do the arithmetic. People × actions × days, against the actual published limit. Every free tier here breaks at a specific, calculable point.
