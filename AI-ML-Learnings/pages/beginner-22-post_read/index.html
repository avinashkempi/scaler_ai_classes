§ 1

Why AWS Exists — and What "Free" Actually Means There

⏱ 10 min

Vercel, Render, and Railway solved "get this online." AWS exists to solve a different, later problem: "prove to someone else's procurement office that this is solid."

🏢
The Analogy

Render and Railway are like renting a serviced kitchen — someone else handles the building, you just cook. AWS is like owning the whole building yourself: every wall, every wire, every lock is one you configured — more work up front, but nobody can tell you "sorry, our policy doesn't allow that" when a client asks for something specific. Enterprises reach for AWS not because it's fancier, but because it's the landlord that lets them set the rules.

What enterprises actually ask forWhy a single shop rarely needs itWhy a 9-store chain suddenly does
Compliance certifications (ISO 27001, SOC 2, etc.)Nobody audits a single shop's Instagram-link tool.A procurement office needs paperwork proving the platform underneath meets a standard, not just a promise.
Data residency (data must stay in a specific country)One shop's data has no legal residency requirement.Member and order data across nine stores may fall under real regulatory scope the moment it's "the chain's" data, not just Chapter One's.
Guaranteed uptime, backed by a contract (an SLA)Render's free tier makes no uptime promise, and nobody's paying for one.A paying client's contract can require a specific, documented uptime percentage — with financial consequences for missing it.
Predictable scale under real loadA single shop's traffic is small and personal.Nine stores' worth of staff using the same tool at once is a different order of magnitude than one counter.
🎙 Instructor Says

None of Render, Railway, or Vercel is "worse" than AWS. They're the right tool for a different job — get one small thing online, fast, free, with almost no configuration. AWS trades that simplicity for control: you configure more, but you can prove more. Today's licensing conversation was the first time in Chapter One's story that trade actually starts to make sense.

The AWS Free Tier — What's Genuinely Free, and For How Long

AWS's free tier is not one flat offer — it's a mix of always-free service limits (a fixed monthly amount that never expires, like Lambda's free request allowance) and time-limited free amounts for new accounts (like a set number of EC2 hours, typically capped at 12 months from account creation). The genuine gotcha: once either the monthly cap or the 12-month window is hit, AWS doesn't gently degrade the way Render does — a misconfigured resource can start billing immediately and quietly, which is exactly the kind of surprise a personal-account, no-guardrails setup produces.

Curriculum honesty flag, same spirit as Class 21's Railway note: "AWS free tier" is a genuinely more complicated claim than "Render free tier." Some services are free forever within a monthly cap (small Lambda usage, a small DynamoDB table). Others are only free for the first 12 months on a new account (a chunk of EC2 and RDS hours). Both halves matter — treating it as one uniform "free tier" is the single most common AWS billing surprise reported by first-time users, worth naming plainly rather than glossing over.
§ 2

Core AWS Services — Five Plain-Language Analogies

⏱ 20 min

Five names come up constantly in JDs and architecture diagrams. None of them need jargon to make sense — each one maps onto something Chapter One already has a version of.

🖥️
EC2

A rented computer in the cloud. You get a machine — choose its size, install what you want, SSH into it like it's sitting on a desk. Full control, full responsibility: you patch it, you scale it, you manage it.

🗄️
S3

A filing cabinet anyone can access via a URL. Object storage — drop a file in, get a link back. Perfect for anything that just needs to sit there and be fetched, like the shelf-talker's book-cover images.

⚡
Lambda

A function that runs only when called — no server to manage. Nothing is "on" between requests. It wakes up, does one job, and goes back to costing nothing. Ideal for something like tagging one restock request's urgency.

🌱
Elastic Beanstalk

A deployment manager that handles EC2 for you. You hand over code; it provisions and manages the actual server underneath, including scaling and health checks — the "kitchen manager" version of EC2's "rent a kitchen yourself."

🗃️
RDS

A managed, proper database. Not something touched hands-on in this course, but JD-relevant: backups, patching, and failover handled for you, instead of Restock Radar's in-memory list that vanishes on every restart.

🎙 Instructor Says

Notice the shape of the pattern: moving from EC2 toward Lambda trades control for convenience — EC2 gives you the whole machine, Lambda gives you almost nothing to manage at all. Elastic Beanstalk sits in between, deliberately: same underlying EC2 machine, but AWS handles the fiddly parts. That's the exact same trade Class 21 taught between Vercel, Render, and Railway — just with more knobs, because AWS assumes you might need every one of them eventually.

§ 3

IAM & Least Privilege — Answering Class 21's Cliffhanger

⏱ 18 min

"Who besides Nikhil has access, and how is it controlled?" is a real procurement question with a real AWS answer: IAM.

IAM — Identity and Access Management

AWS's system for controlling exactly who can do exactly what. Three pieces matter: a user is one identity (a person, or a service acting on someone's behalf); a role is a bundle of permissions a user can be granted, temporarily or permanently; a policy is the actual document spelling out what's allowed — "can view billing," "can redeploy this service," "can delete this database." Nothing in AWS runs with implicit trust — every action is checked against a policy, every time.

🔑
The Analogy

A personal-account setup is one master key that opens every door in the building, held by exactly one person. IAM is what happens when the building gets a proper set of keys cut: Tara gets a key to the till, the auditor gets a key to the ledger room only, and nobody gets a key to a door they never needed to open in the first place. Least privilege is just the rule that decides how many doors each key actually opens — as few as the job requires, never more "to be safe."

MistakeWhat actually goes wrong
Too little accessLegitimate work gets blocked — Tara can't update a key she's now responsible for, so she texts Nikhil anyway, and the whole point of scaling access was lost.
Too much access ("just in case")A misconfigured IAM policy handing out broad permissions by default is documented as one of the most common real AWS security mistakes — an account that only ever needed to view billing can now delete a production database, whether or not anyone intended to use that power.
The fix: least privilegeGrant exactly the permissions a role needs for its actual job, nothing more — reviewed and adjusted as that job changes, not set once and forgotten.
🎙 Instructor Says

This is the direct answer to Ravi's question from last class. It's not "give Tara Nikhil's password" — that's the master-key problem all over again. It's not "never let anyone else touch anything" either — that just means Nikhil's still the single point of failure. IAM's whole design is the middle path: give each person exactly the access their actual job needs, and nothing about that requires trusting anyone blindly or bottlenecking on one person forever.

Q2. The chain's IT auditor only ever needs to confirm a real SLA-backed billing history exists. Nikhil's first instinct is to just hand them a Full Admin login "to be safe." What's the actual problem?
ANothing — more access is always the safer default for anyone reviewing a setup.

§ 4

Regions & Availability Zones — Why Mumbai Matters

⏱ 14 min

"Where is this data hosted?" has a one-word answer once you know AWS's regions — and it changes both speed and the law that applies.

Region vs. Availability Zone (AZ)

A region is a real geographic area — Mumbai (ap-south-1), Singapore, N. Virginia — where AWS runs data centers. Choosing a region decides where data physically lives and which users get low latency. Inside every region are multiple Availability Zones — physically separate data centers with independent power and networking. Spreading a deployment across more than one AZ means a single data-center failure doesn't take the whole app down with it — the same idea as not keeping every backup on one hard drive.

🎙 Instructor Says

For the nine-store chain, region isn't a technicality — it's two of Tara's four questions at once. "Where is data hosted" is directly answered by the region name. And if any of it counts as data that must legally stay inside India, Mumbai isn't just the fastest option for Indian users, it may be the only compliant one. AZs are the quieter half: a single-AZ setup is a single point of failure at the data-center level, no different in spirit from everything living on Nikhil's one laptop.

Q3. The chain's member data must legally stay within India, and nearly all its stores are in Indian cities too. Which region argument actually settles this, and how?
AMumbai — physically inside India, and closest to most users too.
BSingapore, because "nearest region outside India" is usually close enough to count as a safe, defensible reading of any data-residency requirement a client's procurement office might raise.

DIt doesn't matter which region, as long as Availability Zones are switched on somewhere inside it.
Correct answer: A. Region choice settles both questions at once here — Mumbai satisfies a same-country residency requirement directly, and geographic proximity to Indian users is a genuine latency win on top of it, not a separate trade-off.

Reading a Simple Architecture Diagram

This is the fourth thing Tara's procurement contact asked for, and it's the easiest to satisfy once the vocabulary above makes sense — trace a request the same way you'd trace a delivery, hop by hop.

Browser → Load Balancer → App server (checked by IAM) → S3 for files / RDS for structured data

Read it the same way every time: 1) where does the request enter — a Load Balancer spreading traffic across servers. 2) what actually runs the app — EC2, or Elastic Beanstalk managing EC2 for you. 3) who's allowed to act — IAM checks this on the way through, not as an afterthought. 4) where does data actually live — S3 for files, RDS for structured records. Four boxes, four questions, and Tara's fourth procurement question is answered without writing a line of new code.

Key Takeaways

1. AWS isn't "better" hosting — it's a different trade. More configuration, in exchange for more proof: compliance, data residency, contractual SLAs.

2. EC2, S3, Lambda, Elastic Beanstalk, and RDS all map to something you already understand. A rented computer, a filing cabinet, an on-call function, a deployment manager, a proper database.

3. IAM's least-privilege principle answers last class's cliffhanger directly. Access doesn't have to mean "share the master key" or "stay the single point of failure" — it can mean exactly the right amount, per person, per job.

4. Region and Availability Zone are two different questions. Region answers "where does data live" — often a compliance question. AZ answers "what happens if one data center goes down" — a resilience question.

5. A live URL was the right answer for last class's problem. It isn't the answer to this one. "Where's it hosted, what's the SLA, who has access, show me the diagram" needs AWS's vocabulary — even before it ever needs AWS's console.

Nikhil can now name the service, explain the access model, point at a region, and sketch the diagram. Nobody has opened the AWS console yet — that's still one step further down the road than today needed to go.
