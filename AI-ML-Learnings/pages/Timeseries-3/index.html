Choosing a lag, data-first

ACF: how far back does a pattern echo?

The ARIMA family looks back at past values and past errors to make a forecast. But how many months back should it look? Guessing isn't a strategy — the data itself can answer that, through the Auto-Correlation Function (ACF).

Imagine asking: how similar is this month's sales to last month's? To two months ago? To twelve months ago? ACF answers exactly that — it's the correlation of the series with a delayed copy of itself, checked at every lag.

The echo in an empty hall

Clap once in an empty hall and the sound you hear a moment later is how much of that original clap is still bouncing around. ACF is the "echo strength" of the data at each lag — a strong echo at lag 12 means the pattern from twelve months ago is still influencing today. Tall bars mean a lag still matters; bars inside the shaded band mean it's basically noise.

Reading this plot

  • Lag 1 shows a strong negative correlation (around −0.5): when sales run higher than usual in one month, the following month tends to run lower, and vice versa.
  • Most other lags fall inside the confidence band — not strong enough to call statistically significant.
  • Lags 11 and 17 show positive spikes, but they're isolated; there's no clear repeating pattern across seasonal lags like 12 or 24.
  • Overall: once the series is stationary, there's some short-term dependence at lag 1, and little strong autocorrelation further out — recent months carry some predictive information, but the longer-term dependence is weak.

Removing the middleman

PACF: what does this lag add on its own?

ACF has a blind spot. If lag 1 is strongly correlated with today, lag 2 will also look correlated with today — partly through lag 1, not because lag 2 itself adds new information. What's needed is the direct effect of each lag, with the in-between lags' influence stripped out — the job of the Partial Auto-Correlation Function (PACF).

The family group chat

Your uncle's opinion might correlate with yours — but is that because he influences you directly, or because he influences your mom, who then influences you? PACF asks: once I already know what Mom thinks, does Uncle add anything new? It isolates the direct link at each lag.

Together, ACF and PACF give a data-driven way to choose how many past values (for AR) and how many past errors (for MA) a model should include — no guessing required.

Reading this plot

  • Lag 1 has a strong negative partial correlation (around −0.5) — the previous month's value has a strong direct relationship with the current month.
  • Lag 2 is also significantly negative, with several early lags showing smaller negative effects.
  • Lag 11 shows a noticeable positive spike, alongside a few smaller significant spikes later.
  • PACF is what guides the AR term — the significant spikes help pick the p parameter.

Quick aside

Correlation doesn't mean causation

Before building on top of ACF/PACF, it's worth being honest about something: a strong correlation at some lag just means the numbers moved together historically — it does not mean last month's sales figure caused this month's.

Ice cream and sunscreen

Ice cream sales and sunscreen sales rise and fall together every year. That doesn't mean ice cream causes sunscreen purchases — a third factor, summer, drives both. A strong lag-12 correlation in sales is most likely both months being driven by the same yearly cycle — festive season, weather, school holidays — not last year's sales literally causing this year's.

ACF/PACF are excellent for picking model settings, but they're not evidence of a causal mechanism — a distinction worth keeping in mind before building AR and MA models next.

Model 1 · past values

AR — Auto-Regression

Time to build the first real model. The PACF plot already showed which lags have a direct relationship with today's sales — an AR model uses exactly that.

An AR(p) model forecasts the next value as a weighted mix of the last p values.

Your grade next week

Your grade next week probably looks like a weighted mix of your last few weeks' grades. If you've been consistently scoring well, next week's guess leans high; if you've been slipping, the guess leans down. AR does the same thing with sales numbers instead of grades.

Minimal formula — recognize it, don't memorize it

today's value ≈ (weight₁ × last value) + (weight₂ × value 2 months ago) + … + noise

p — how many past values to use — is picked by reading where the PACF bars stop being significant: the "how many past weeks' grades actually matter" answer.

Model 2 · past errors

MA — moving average, the ARIMA kind

A naming clash worth clearing up first

This isn't the same "moving average" used as a baseline earlier (which just averaged the last few raw values). This MA is a component of the ARIMA family that works on forecast errors, not raw values.

An MA(q) model forecasts the next value by adjusting for how wrong the last q forecasts were.

Guessing a friend's arrival time

Adjust your guess based on how wrong you were last time, not what the value itself was. If you guessed a friend's arrival time and they were ten minutes late, you don't just repeat your last guess — you nudge your next guess by roughly that same error, expecting a similar pattern of lateness.

Minimal formula — recognize it, don't memorize it

today's value ≈ average level + (weight₁ × last error) + (weight₂ × error 2 steps ago) + …

q is picked from the ACF plot — the lag where the "echo" of past shocks fades out.

26.44%
Worse than AR(2)'s 20.83%.

What the graph shows

  • The forecast quickly settles around 10,600 and stays almost flat, while actual sales keep fluctuating between roughly 12,000 and 16,000.
  • A MAPE of 26.44% — worse than AR(2).
  • Using only past errors isn't enough to capture the changing pattern in this series.

Model 3 · both signals combined

ARMA — combining both

There are now two lenses on the same data: AR looks at past values, MA looks at past errors. Why choose one? ARMA(p, q) simply uses both at the same time.

Grade trend plus last week's miss

Predicting next week's grade using both your recent grade trend (the AR part) and how far off your last prediction was (the MA part) — combining "what's been happening" with "how wrong was I last time" usually beats using just one signal.

The catch

ARMA still assumes the input series is already stationary — no trend, no seasonality left in it. That's exactly why it's fit on sales_diff, the differenced series from Part 2, and not the raw sales numbers.

Model 4 · differencing, automated

ARIMA — removing the stationarity requirement

There's an annoying manual step in what ARMA required: the data has to be differenced by hand before ARMA will work, and then that differencing has to be "undone" to get forecasts back on the original sales scale. That's exactly the gap ARIMA closes.

ARIMA(p, d, q) = ARMA(p, q) + automatic differencing. The "I" stands for Integration — a fancy word for "differencing, done internally, and then undone automatically when producing the final forecast."

A built-in de-wobble dial

Think of d as a built-in "de-wobble" dial. Instead of manually flattening the trend before modeling and un-flattening the forecast after, ARIMA just needs to be told how many times to difference — usually d=1 for a trend, sometimes d=2 for a stronger one — and it handles the flattening and un-flattening on its own. That means the raw, non-stationary sales series can be fed in directly.

ARIMA(2,1,2) · 12-month MAPE
8.79%

Model 5 · seasonality, added back

SARIMA — adding back seasonality

The playground makes one thing obvious: even a well-tuned ARIMA forecast tends to flatten out and miss Vastra Loop's festive-season bumps. That's because plain ARIMA has no concept of a repeating seasonal pattern — it only differences away trend.

SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q, s) is ARIMA plus a second set of AR/I/MA terms, applied at the seasonal lag s — 12, for monthly data with a yearly cycle.

Two trackers running together

Think of ARIMA as tracking "how this month compares to recent months," and the seasonal part as a second, independent tracker asking "how does this month compare to the same month last year, and the year before?" SARIMA runs both trackers together and blends their signals into one forecast.

The seasonal period is set to s = 12, based on the seasonality confirmed back in Part 1's decomposition and re-confirmed by the ACF plot's lag-12 spike earlier today.

SARIMA · 12-month MAPE
3.98%
Clears the 5% target for the first time.

What the graph shows

  • SARIMA extends ARIMA by adding seasonality, letting the model learn patterns that repeat every 12 months.

Beyond a single number

Prediction intervals — how confident are we?

R

"Okay, but how sure are we about this number? Give me a range, not just a single line."

Rohan

A fair ask — a single forecasted number hides how uncertain the model actually is, especially further into the future. A prediction interval is a range around the forecast — "sales will likely land between X and Y" — instead of one exact number.

A more honest weather forecast

A weather forecast that says "32°C" feels falsely precise. One that says "30–34°C, most likely around 32°C" is more honest, and more useful for planning. The interval usually widens the further out you forecast, because uncertainty compounds with each additional step — the same effect that made multi-step AR forecasts get shakier over time.

Reading this chart

  • The forecast line is SARIMA's point forecast; actual sales run alongside it.
  • The shaded region is the 95% prediction interval — the range within which the actual value is expected to fall roughly 95% of the time.
  • The interval becomes wider further into the future, because uncertainty increases the further ahead the forecast reaches.
  • Most actual values fall inside the shaded region, suggesting the model's uncertainty estimates are reasonably well calibrated.

From notebook to production

From a static CSV to a live forecast

R

"This all makes sense on last year's CSV. But our POS system pushes a new month of sales the moment it closes. Are you refitting the whole SARIMA model from scratch every time one new number shows up?"

Rohan

Fair question, and the answer matters for production. Refitting a SARIMA model on 18 years of data every time a single new month lands is wasteful and slow. What's actually needed is a way to fold new observations into the model cheaply, without re-estimating every coefficient from zero.

Statsmodels' .append(refit=False) does exactly that: it updates the model's internal state — its recent lagged values and residuals — using the same (p,d,q)(P,D,Q,s) coefficients, instead of re-estimating them from scratch. Simulating the sales arriving as a live monthly stream and comparing the original 12-month-ahead SARIMA forecast against a rolling, one-step-ahead streaming forecast:

Because each streaming forecast only ever has to look one month ahead instead of twelve, the errors don't get the chance to compound the way a multi-step forecast's do — which is the same compounding effect seen earlier with AR.

But what if the pattern itself changes?

The streaming forecast above tracked well because nothing about the underlying sales pattern actually shifted — .append() was just feeding the same pattern one point at a time. That's the easy case.

A

Vastra Loop is opening a new store format next quarter — one expected to permanently lift the sales level. This isn't noise, and it isn't a one-month blip. It's a structural break: a genuine shift in the process generating the data, not just another draw from the same distribution the model was fit on.

Aisha, working through the implication

Why .append() alone can't catch this

.append(refit=False) only updates the model's internal state. It never re-estimates the (p,d,q)(P,D,Q,s) coefficients themselves — and those coefficients are where the model's belief about trend and seasonal strength actually lives. That belief was frozen the moment .fit() ran on historical data. So when the new store format pushes sales onto a higher level:

  • The model has no mechanism to notice the shift is permanent.
  • It keeps forecasting off the old level, one step behind reality.
  • Because the seasonal component reuses last year's swing size, it under-predicts by roughly the same margin month after month — the gap doesn't close on its own.

Append-only forecast

Keeps folding new months in with .append(refit=False), but never touches the coefficients. Stays anchored near the old sales level, and the gap to actual sales widens for months — bottoming out over 150 units below actual before slowly narrowing as more data outweighs the stale fit.

Quarterly-refit forecast

Periodically re-estimates the SARIMA coefficients on the latest window. Picks up the new sales level within one quarter and tracks actual sales far more closely from then on.

.append() is built to absorb new observations, not new behavior — and a store format change is a change in behavior, not just a new data point. The real question isn't "does the model see the new number?" (it does). It's whether the model's coefficients can ever adapt to the fact that the story has changed. With refit=False, the answer is no — which is exactly the gap that periodic refitting closes.
