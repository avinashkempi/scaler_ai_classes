Ticket #3, from the Head of HR
“We are losing delivery partners and warehouse staff faster than we can hire. Every exit costs us: the departing person often asks for a counter-offer, new hires need training, and it takes weeks to fill a role. Our own attrition history is still thin, so we have pulled a benchmark employee dataset from a similar-sized company to prototype a model. Can you help us find out who is likely to leave, and why?”

Why does attrition matter here?

Counter-offers

Trying to retain a departing employee often means a counter-offer negotiation. That is a cost in itself, and it does not always work.

Costlier replacements

A replacement is often hired at today’s higher market rate, so backfilling a role can cost more than retaining the original person would have.

Training time

New hires need training before they are fully productive.

Recruiting effort

Recruiting itself costs time and money.

Why a straight line will not work here

Why not Logistic Regression?

It draws one straight decision boundary. Looking at the plot, the “Left” employees are not on one consistent side of any single line, so it will struggle here, exactly like it struggled with our three traffic tiers in Class 1.

Why not kNN?

kNN actually works reasonably well on data like this. It rests on the same assumption we used in Class 1: neighbourhoods should be homogeneous, and that holds here too. But kNN has a real drawback, one we spent all of Class 2 on.

Mission 3

A different idea: split the space with straight cuts

Instead of one line, what if we used several straight cuts, each parallel to an axis, to carve the plot into small, pure regions? Each cut can be written as a simple rule, like Age < 35. Stack enough of these rules and you get a set of nested if-else conditions that separates the classes, even though the overall boundary is not a straight line.

Grow a tree on the toy data, one cut at a time

These cuts show one way the splits can go on the toy data. The last two rounds of cuts each isolate a single employee, a hint of how a tree can start memorising rather than generalising.

From rules to a tree

Drawing the nested if-else conditions as a diagram gives a tree shape, which is exactly what the explorer above builds.

Root node

Sits at the top and holds the first split.

Internal nodes

Hold further splits. Also called decision nodes.

Leaf nodes

Sit at the bottom and hold the final prediction.

Two ways to read the same tree

Geometrically, a decision tree is a combination of axis-parallel cuts: every split is a vertical or horizontal line in the plot above.

Logically, it is a combination of nested if-else conditions. The tree from the explorer reads:

if Age < 35:
    if Overtime == 1:  predict "Left"
    else:
        if Age < 25.5:  predict "Stayed"
        else:            predict "Left"
else:
    if Overtime == 0:  predict "Stayed"
    else:
        if Age < 49:  predict "Left"
        else:          predict "Stayed"
Mission 4

Pure nodes, and how confident should we be?

At prediction time, a leaf node predicts whichever class is the majority among the training points that landed there. Compare two possible leaf nodes after a split:

Comparison of two leaf nodes by class counts and confidence
Positive classNegative classConfidence
Option 1 (purer)9010High: 90%
Option 2 (less pure)6040Low: 60%

Neither option is actually pure. A pure node contains only one class, with zero mixing, like 100 positive and 0 negative. Option 1 is simply purer than Option 2: a stronger majority, so more confidence in the prediction. That is exactly why we split the data into purer and purer regions, ideally all the way to pure leaves.

How do we measure purity mathematically?

We measure the opposite of purity: impurity. The most common measure is entropy, borrowed from information theory, where it measures randomness.

The four-jar thought experiment

Imagine four jars, each filled with red and blue balls. You pick one ball from a jar without looking. Which jars give you the most confidence about the colour you will pick?

Jars 3 and 4

These are pure: you already know exactly what colour you will get.

Jar 1

The most impure. It is a near-even mix, so you have the least confidence.

The lesson

Confidence grows as a jar gets purer. Entropy puts a number on that.

For a binary outcome, entropy is defined as:

H = −p log2(p) − (1 − p) log2(1 − p)

where p is the proportion of one class in the node.

  • Entropy is maximum (equal to 1) when p = 0.5: a perfectly impure, even mix.
  • Entropy is minimum (equal to 0) when p = 0 or p = 1: a pure node.

Fill a jar, watch the entropy

Change the number of red balls out of 20.

The curve peaks exactly at p = 0.5 and touches zero at the two ends. That matches the jar experiment: maximum uncertainty at an even mix, zero uncertainty at a pure jar.
def entropy(p):
    """Entropy for a binary outcome with probability p for one class."""
    if p == 0 or p == 1:
        return 0.0
    return -p * np.log2(p) - (1 - p) * np.log2(1 - p)

p_values = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 200)
entropy_values = [entropy(p) for p in p_values]

plt.plot(p_values, entropy_values)
plt.xlabel('P(class = 1)')
plt.ylabel('Entropy')
plt.title('Entropy for a binary outcome')
plt.axvline(0.5, linestyle='--', color='gray')
plt.show()
Mission 5

Choosing the best split with information gain

We know we want purer child nodes after a split. Information gain measures exactly how much purer a split makes things:

Information Gain = Entropy(parent) − Weighted average entropy(children)

We use a weighted average, not a simple average, because a child with 98 points should count for more than a child with 2 points.

A worked example

Say our root node has 100 employees: 60 who left, 40 who stayed. We consider splitting on Gender: the Male child has 70 employees (50 left, 20 stayed) and the Female child has 30 (10 left, 20 stayed).

Information gain calculator

Starts on the Gender example. Change any number, or try the presets.

Child B is filled in automatically: whatever is left over from the parent after Child A.
# Parent node: 60 left, 40 stayed, out of 100
p_parent = 60 / 100
entropy_parent = entropy(p_parent)

# Split by Gender
# Male child: 70 employees total, 50 left, 20 stayed
p_male = 50 / 70
entropy_male = entropy(p_male)

# Female child: 30 employees total, 10 left, 20 stayed
p_female = 10 / 30
entropy_female = entropy(p_female)

# Weighted average entropy of the children
weighted_child_entropy = (70/100) * entropy_male + (30/100) * entropy_female

information_gain = entropy_parent - weighted_child_entropy

Splitting on Gender barely helps: entropy drops only slightly, giving an information gain of about 0.091. Splitting on Age < 35 for the same root node gives 0.257, noticeably higher.

A decision tree always picks the split that gives the maximum information gain (equivalently, the maximum drop in entropy) at each node, then repeats this process on each child node until it reaches pure, or nearly pure, leaves.

What happens with more than two categories?

Classic algorithm versus scikit-learn

The classic version of this algorithm, as described so far, would create one child branch per category for a feature with more than two unique values, calculating weighted entropy across all of them in the same way.

A caveat for the tool we’re about to use: scikit-learn’s DecisionTreeClassifier doesn’t do this. It only ever makes binary splits, even for a categorical feature. A multi-category field is converted to a numeric column first (during preprocessing), and the tree searches for a threshold on that number, exactly as it would for Age. In the next section, JobRole appears as a single numeric column, split on a threshold rather than branching once per role.

We now understand how a tree decides where to split, in principle, and how scikit-learn’s implementation differs in practice. Time to grow a real one on the actual attrition data.

Mission 6

Growing a real tree

We use scikit-learn’s DecisionTreeClassifier with entropy as the split criterion, exactly as we just worked out by hand.

from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

model = DecisionTreeClassifier(criterion='entropy', random_state=42)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

print('Train accuracy:', round(model.score(X_train, y_train), 3))
print('Test accuracy :', round(model.score(X_test, y_test), 3))

Train accuracy is a perfect 1.0, but test accuracy is much lower. A model that memorises its training data but does noticeably worse on new data is overfitting: the tree has fit noise and quirks of individual employees rather than patterns that generalise.

What to notice

Even showing just the top 3 levels, this tree is already complex. The full tree goes much deeper, memorising individual employees rather than learning general patterns. That is a textbook sign of overfitting, and it explains the gap between train and test accuracy. We will tackle it with pruning-style hyperparameters in Decision Trees-2.

Bonus

Entropy is not the only option

DecisionTreeClassifier also supports the Gini impurity criterion. It is an alternative way to measure impurity that is often just as effective and slightly cheaper to compute, since it skips the logarithm.

Gini = 1 − Σi pi2

Like entropy, Gini is 0 for a pure node and highest for an even mix. The two usually pick very similar splits in practice.

Entropy and Gini side by side

For a two-class node, Gini simplifies to 2p(1 − p).

model_gini = DecisionTreeClassifier(criterion='gini', random_state=42)
model_gini.fit(X_train, y_train)

print('Entropy tree - test accuracy:', round(model.score(X_test, y_test), 3))
print('Gini tree    - test accuracy:', round(model_gini.score(X_test, y_test), 3))

From tree to business answer

Remember HR’s real question: why are people leaving, not just who. A trained tree can tell us which features it relied on most, using feature_importances_.

How the importance chart is built

Follow the code, one step at a time.

importances = pd.Series(model.feature_importances_, index=X_train.columns)

The trained tree exposes one importance score per feature. Pairing the scores with X_train.columns labels each score with its feature name.

top_features = importances.sort_values(ascending=False).head(10)

Sort from most to least important and keep the top ten.

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
top_features.sort_values().plot(kind='barh')
plt.xlabel('Importance')
plt.title("Top 10 features driving the tree's predictions")
plt.show()

Wrap up

  1. Straight-line models like Logistic Regression struggle with data like this; even kNN, which handles it reasonably well, does not scale, as we saw in Class 2.
  2. A decision tree splits the feature space using axis-parallel cuts, which combine into a tree of root, internal and leaf nodes.
  3. A leaf predicts the majority class of the training points that land there; purer leaves give more confident predictions.
  4. Entropy measures impurity: 0 for a pure node, 1 (maximum) for an even 50-50 split.
  5. Information gain is the drop in weighted entropy from a split; a tree always picks the split with the highest information gain at each node.
  6. A fully grown tree can reach perfect training accuracy while doing much worse on test data. That gap is overfitting, and we will fix it with pruning-style hyperparameters in the next class.
  7. Gini impurity is an alternative to entropy, usually giving very similar trees.
  8. feature_importances_ turns a trained tree into a ranked list of what actually drives its predictions, connecting the model back to the original business question.
