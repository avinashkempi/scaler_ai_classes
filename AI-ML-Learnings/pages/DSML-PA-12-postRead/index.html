Setting the scene

One Red Number in the 6 PM Dashboard

It's Day 3 of the Great Indian Festival. The sale is going well — or so everyone thinks. Traffic is at a record high. Then, at 6 PM, the day-to-date dashboard lands in your inbox, and one number is red.

Your role

Product Analyst, Amazon India

The 6 PM dashboard compares Day 3 so far (12 AM – 6 PM) with the same hours on Day 1:

MetricDay 1Day 3
Sessions1.00 crore1.35 crore▲ 35%
Conversion (orders ÷ sessions)3.1%2.4%▼ 0.7 pts
Orders & GMV——▲ only ~5%
🧑‍💼
Your manager

"Traffic is up 35%, so why aren't sales? Find out what's going on."

35% more traffic produced roughly 5% more orders. Somewhere between the extra visit and the completed order, something is swallowing demand.

Common first instincts

Where most first answers start

Faced with this, most people jump to a suspect — Flipkart's sale, or the new users from the influencer campaign — or say they'd check the data first.

Each instinct catches part of the story; none catches all of it. The rest of this case shows how the method puts the pieces together.

First, name the question type. Is this a metric definition question or a metric change question?
Answer: B — a metric change question. Conversion is already defined and already tracked; it moved. That means root cause analysis: we run CRIED and then Conclude. Every checkpoint in this case study maps to one step of it.
The framework

CRIED, then Conclude

The spine for every metric-change question. The first four steps gather evidence; the fifth turns evidence into a decision.

C

Clarify

What exactly was measured, and is the comparison fair?

R

Rule Out

Is the number even real? Then list the high-level causes.

I

Internal Data

Slice our own data with TROPiCS to localize the drop.

E

External Data

Competitors, banks, the festival itself — checked last, not first.

✓

Conclude

State the root cause, size it, and recommend a fix.

Inside the I: TROPiCS

T — Time

When did it start? A cliff means something broke; a drift means behaviour shifted.

R — Region

Is it everywhere, or concentrated in some cities?

O — Other related features

Did something adjacent change at the same moment?

P — Platform

iOS, Android, web — a platform cause leaves a platform mark.

C — Cannibalization

Is another surface or feature absorbing the activity?

S — Segmentation

New vs existing, device, payment method, category.

ℹ️ The lowercase i in TROPiCS is only there to make the word pronounceable — it isn't a seventh check.

All numbers in this case study are illustrative. The reasoning is what transfers to an interview, not the figures.

Checkpoint 1

Clarify the Problem

In an interview, jumping straight to causes without clarifying is the fastest way to lose marks. But you can't ask a hundred questions either. So which one matters most?

1
Framework step · Clarify
You get exactly one clarifying question
You can ask your manager only one clarifying question first. Which one?
💡Before you investigate a drop, make sure you know exactly what was measured — and that the comparison is fair.
Answer: C — how is conversion defined, and is the comparison like-for-like?

The dashboard arrives at 6 PM, so Day 3 is only part of a day. If it were compared with a full Day 1, the "drop" could simply be a timing artefact — evening shoppers haven't arrived yet. Clarifying the definition and the comparison window is always step one.

Why the others fail
  • AUseful business context, but it doesn't help explain why conversion dropped.
  • BCompetitor performance is an External Data question. We check it later, not first.
  • DOwnership doesn't explain the drop — and it assumes the checkout is guilty before we have evidence.

The manager's answers

Definition

Conversion = orders placed ÷ sessions, measured the same way on both days.

Comparison window

Day 3 from 12 AM to 6 PM vs the same hours on Day 1, all platforms, all of India. The comparison is fair.

Changes on Day 3

No product launches. Planned festival promotions continued as usual: lightning deals, bank offers, influencer campaign.

Problem now clear: over the same hours, conversion fell from 3.1% to 2.4%, defined identically on both days. Next, make sure the data itself isn't lying to us.

Checkpoints 2 & 3

Rule Out the Boring Explanations

A drop that turns out to be a logging bug sends the whole analysis chasing a ghost. Before looking for real causes, check whether the number is even real — then list what could be causing it.

2
Framework step · Rule Out
Is the number real?
Which of these is a Rule Out check?
💡Rule Out is about making sure the number itself is real, before asking why it moved.
Answer: A — check the tracking, and check that the traffic is real.

Conversion is orders ÷ sessions, so anything that inflates sessions on paper lowers conversion without any real change in behaviour. Two things deserve a check: the iOS app release on Day 2 (a new version can change how sessions are counted) and the 35% jump in sessions during an influencer campaign (bots or duplicates could inflate it).

Why the others fail
  • BA competitor comparison is an External Data check.
  • CBreaking down by city is Internal Data — the R in TROPiCS.
  • DSurveys are slow, and come much later in an RCA, if at all.

Results of the checks

✓ Analytics intact

The Day-2 iOS release didn't change the analytics code, and session counts per platform match the server logs.

✓ Traffic is real

Bot and duplicate sessions are steady at about 2% of traffic — same as Day 1. The extra sessions are real shoppers.

✓ Orders reconcile

Dashboard orders match the payment and order systems, with no missing data hours.

The drop is real. Customers really are converting less. Now list what could be causing it.

3
Framework step · Rule Out — list the high-level causes
List hypotheses, then decide how to test them

The Rule Out step also means listing the possible high-level causes before touching more data, so nothing obvious gets missed. Think about things inside Amazon (product, pricing, checkout, marketing) and things outside it (competitors, banks, the festival itself).

Internal hypotheses

  • ·Checkout or payment bug
  • ·The new iOS release
  • ·Deals going out of stock
  • ·Price changes
  • ·The influencer campaign bringing in shoppers less likely to buy
  • ·A slow site under heavy load

External hypotheses

  • ·Flipkart's Big Billion Days
  • ·A bank or payment-gateway outage
  • ·Shoppers waiting for bigger Diwali deals
We now have more than ten possible causes. What's the most efficient next step?
💡Different causes leave their mark at different steps of the funnel.
Answer: D — localize the drop in the funnel first.

Shoppers who are less likely to buy would show up in the upper funnel (fewer product views and add-to-carts). A payment problem would show up at the last step. One funnel check tells us which whole group of hypotheses to look at first.

Why the others fail
  • ASlow — and popularity isn't evidence.
  • BDuring a 10-day sale, every lost day is lost revenue.
  • CSurveys are slow and only reach a few users.
📋 Keep the hypothesis list visible. We'll cross each one off as the evidence comes in.
Checkpoint 4

Localize the Drop in the Funnel

A conversion rate is the end of a long funnel. Before slicing by segment, find which step broke.

4
Framework step · Internal Data — Funnel
Predict it before you see it
Make a prediction. At which step of the funnel do you think the biggest drop happened?
💡Think about what is different about this festival day: who is visiting, and what has to happen at each step for an order to go through.
Answer: D — Checkout started → Order placed.

The biggest drop is at the very last step: an 11-point fall in payment success. These users have chosen a product, added it to cart and started checkout. They clearly want to buy — something is stopping the order from going through. The upper funnel also weakened slightly; hold on to that.

Why the others fail
  • A/BThese steps dipped a little (2–4 points), but nowhere near the fall at payment.
  • CThis step is flat — users who add to cart are still starting checkout.

Step-to-step conversion, 12 AM – 6 PM

Funnel stepDay 1Day 3ChangeRead
Session → Product view60%56%−4 ptsSlight weakening
Product view → Add to cart30%28%−2 ptsSlight weakening
Add to cart → Checkout started20%20.5%flatHealthy
Checkout started → Order placed86%75%−11 ptsThe break

Two clues now, not one. A big drop at the payment step, and a smaller weakening at the top of the funnel. A good analyst doesn't discard the smaller clue — we'll explain both before we're done.

Checkpoint 5

When Did It Start, and Where?

Now we slice with TROPiCS. We can't look at everything at once, so prioritise: some dimensions tell you whether something suddenly broke or slowly changed. Time is one of them.

5
Framework step · Internal Data — TROPiCS (Time, Platform)
A cliff, not a slope

Hourly conversion, 12 AM – 6 PM

Day 1
~3.1%
Steady all day. This is our baseline.
Day 2
~2.8%
Steady all day — lower than Day 1, but no sudden change.
Day 3
~2.8% → ~2.1%
Steady until 12 PM, when the Day-3 lightning deals went live. Then a sharp drop, and it stayed there.
Clue 1 — the cliff at 12 PM on Day 3

A step drop means something changed or broke at that moment. A shift in customer behaviour wouldn't look like a cliff.

Clue 2 — the lower level from Day 2

2.8% instead of 3.1%, with no cliff, is a different and smaller effect. This is the upper-funnel clue from Checkpoint 4. We come back to it in Checkpoint 6.

One more thread: the iOS team released a new app version on Day 2, and several people in the war room already suspect it.

Which evidence would show that the new iOS release is the cause?
💡If one platform's update caused the problem, how would that platform's numbers compare with the others?
Answer: A — a platform-specific cause leaves a platform-specific mark.

So what does the data say? On Day 3, conversion fell by about the same amount on Android, iOS and web, and only at 12 PM. On Day 2, after the release, there was no step drop on any platform. The cause is something shared across all platforms — like the checkout or payment system. The iOS release is a red herring.

Why the others fail
  • BTrue, but it doesn't point to iOS at all.
  • CSpending levels don't show that the release broke anything.
  • DTiming alone is weak evidence. Many things happened on Day 2 — and the drop didn't start until 12 PM on Day 3.

Where we stand

Where

The payment step

Platforms

Not

The iOS release — crossed off

❓ Still open: why the level was already lower from Day 2.
Checkpoint 6

AI Moment — Does the AI's Conclusion Hold Up?

Time to explain the smaller clue. Marketing's social and influencer campaign ramped up from Day 2 and brought in a lot of new users. Let's look at new vs existing users — and let's ask an AI tool to analyse it, the way many analysts do today.

6
Framework step · Internal Data — TROPiCS Segmentation
New vs existing users

Conversion by user type, 12 AM – 6 PM

User typeDay 1 — shareDay 1 — conversionDay 3 — shareDay 3 — conversion
Existing users80%3.5%63%3.0%
New users20%1.5%37%1.3%
Overall100%3.1%100%2.4%
🤖 The prompt
"Here is Amazon's festival-sale conversion data by user type for Day 1 and Day 3. What is the main cause of the drop in overall conversion? Answer in 3 lines."
The AI's conclusion
"The drop is driven by the influx of new users, who convert at less than half the rate of existing users. New users rose from 20% to 37% of sessions, pulling overall conversion down. Recommendation: improve the onboarding experience for new users."
Do you agree with the AI's conclusion?
💡Look at each row on its own. Did each group's conversion change between Day 1 and Day 3?
Answer: C — partly right.

The AI found a real effect. New users do convert less, and their share of traffic jumped from 20% to 37%, so the overall average is pulled down. That's a mix effect, and it explains why conversion was already lower from Day 2.

But the AI missed the bigger story: both groups' own conversion fell — existing users 3.5% → 3.0%, new users 1.5% → 1.3%. A change in who is visiting can't explain that. Something else hit everyone, which matches the 12 PM payment drop from Checkpoints 4 and 5.

These are 12 AM – 6 PM figures. Before noon, each group converted at its Day-1 rate; the whole fall in each group's rate happened after 12 PM.

Why the others fail
  • AIgnores the drop within each group. Fix only new-user onboarding and the 12 PM problem remains.
  • BThe mix effect is real — ignoring it leaves part of the drop unexplained.
  • DWe already have enough data. Always check each segment's own rate before blaming the mix.
What the AI still can't tell us

Which step broke, and why. AI is great at spotting the obvious pattern in a table. The analyst's job is to check every segment and connect it to the rest of the evidence before agreeing.

Checkpoint 7

Find the Root Cause

Here's what we know: at 12 PM on Day 3, the payment step broke, on every platform, for new and existing users alike. Which cut comes next?

7
Framework step · Internal Data (Segmentation) → Root Cause
Slice the broken step itself
First, pick the next cut.
Answer: B — payment method. The drop is at the payment step, so slice the payment step by payment method. This is exactly how the Myntra case in RCA I was cracked: cut the broken step along its own dimensions.

Payment methods on Day 3 — before vs after 12 PM

MethodShare beforeShare afterSuccess beforeSuccess after
UPI50%32%86%86%
Partner bank cards
festive 10% instant discount		15%45%86%38%
Other cards15%9%84%84%
Net banking / EMI8%5%80%79%
Cash on delivery12%9%95%95%
All methods100%100%86%65%

Only one method broke. Every other method's success rate is unchanged. And 86% before 12 PM blending with 65% after gives the 75% we saw for the whole 12 AM – 6 PM window in Checkpoint 4 — the numbers reconcile.

Why did the share jump too?

At 12 PM, marketing extended the partner-bank offer to the Day-3 lightning deals and promoted it in a push notification — so far more shoppers chose those cards. Share went 15% → 45% at the same moment success collapsed. Then the engineering evidence:

The error

Most failed partner-card payments since 12 PM show "offer validation timeout".

The load

Requests to the offer validation service jumped about 4× at 12 PM; response time rose from about 0.3 s to 8 s.

The gap

The service had not been load-tested for the offer being applied to lightning deals.

Then: direct cause or root cause? Which of these is the most likely root cause?
💡The direct cause is what you can see failing. Keep asking "why?" until you reach the reason it fails. Fix that, and the problem doesn't come back.
Answer: D — it was never load-tested or scaled for that promotion.
Why the others fail
  • AThe direct cause — what we can see failing, not why it fails.
  • BA real but separate, smaller effect (the mix effect). It doesn't explain the payment failures.
  • CCloser, but still a direct cause. Scaling the service fixes today; load-testing before every promotion stops it happening again.

The "why?" ladder

Why did conversion drop at 12 PM?
Partner-card payments started failing.
Why did they fail?
The offer validation service timed out.
Why did it time out?
Requests jumped 4× when the offer went live on lightning deals, and the service slowed from 0.3 s to 8 s.
Why couldn't it cope?
It was never load-tested or scaled for that promotion. That's the root cause.

The full causal chain

No load test→ Offer pushed to lightning deals at 12 PM→ 4× requests→ Offer service times out→ Partner-card payments fail→ Payment success 86% → 65%→ Conversion drops
🔍 The logs make this the most likely root cause. Engineering would confirm it — but this is what a complete RCA answer sounds like.
Checkpoint 8

External Data — Close Flipkart and the Banks Properly

CRIED's last data step is External. Two outside suspects are still on the hypothesis list from Checkpoint 3: Flipkart's Big Billion Days, which went live on Day 2, and a bank or payment-gateway outage. Finding your root cause isn't permission to leave them dangling.

8
Framework step · External Data
Rule out, with evidence — not assumption
Which evidence best rules out Flipkart's sale as the cause of the 12 PM drop?
💡A competitor can lower conversion too, by pulling away shoppers who are comparing prices. What does the timing and location of our drop say?
Answer: A — the timing and location of the drop point inside Amazon.

If Flipkart were pulling shoppers away, we'd expect a change from Day 2, when its sale went live, and mostly in the upper funnel. Instead we see a cliff at 12 PM on Day 3, at the payment step, and the error logs show timeouts in our own offer service.

Why the others fail
  • BWeak on its own. A competitor can lower our conversion even while our traffic rises — by winning the shoppers who compare prices.
  • CWe can't easily verify discount levels, and they don't explain a sudden 12 PM step.
  • DAn assumption, not evidence.

And the second suspect: a bank or gateway outage

The errors are ours

Failures are "offer validation timeout" errors from Amazon's own service — they happen before the payment reaches the bank.

The banks are fine

Partner-bank cards used without the offer are still succeeding at about 85%.

Both external suspects crossed off. Flipkart's Big Billion Days · Bank / gateway outage — both sounded convincing. The evidence ruled them out.

Checkpoints 9 & 10

Size It, Split It, Then Act

Your manager asks: "Is this big enough to wake up the engineering team in the middle of the sale?" We know when it started and which payments are failing — so we can estimate the cost properly.

9
Framework step · Conclude — size the impact (Guesstimate)
How much is this costing us?

Data you have — 12 PM to 6 PM on Day 3

Checkouts started

~2.3 lakh

Partner-card share

45%

Success drop

86% → 38%

a 48-point fall

Average order value

₹2,000

Roughly how much GMV has Amazon lost between 12 PM and 6 PM because of the failing partner-card payments?
💡Lost orders = checkouts × partner-card share × the drop in success. Then multiply by the order value.
Answer: C — about ₹10 crore over six hours.
Lost orders = 2.3 lakh × 45% × 48% ≈ 50,000 orders
Lost GMV = 50,000 × ₹2,000 ≈ ₹10 crore over 6 hours
That's roughly ₹1.7 crore every hour the problem continues.

At ₹1.7 crore an hour, this clearly needs escalating now. Treat it as an upper bound: some shoppers whose card payment failed will have retried with UPI or cash on delivery, so the true loss is somewhat lower.

Why the others fail
  • A/BUnderestimate the scale of festival checkouts. ₹1–2 crore is closer to the hourly loss.
  • DWould mean losing more partner-card orders than were even attempted.
📏 Always size the problem — and always say over what time window. It tells you how urgently to act, and it gets the right people in the room.
10
Framework step · Conclude — explain the whole drop, recommend a fix
Split the 0.7-point drop between its two causes

Leadership will ask two more things: how much of the drop does each cause explain, and what do we do right now? Work the first from the Checkpoint 6 segment table.

Day 1 (12 AM – 6 PM)

80% existing users at 3.5% + 20% new users at 1.5% → 3.1%

Day 3 (12 AM – 6 PM)

63% existing users, 37% new users → actual conversion 2.4%

Work out what Day-3 conversion would have been if each group had kept its Day-1 rate. Roughly what share of the 0.7-point drop does the traffic mix explain?
💡Expected conversion = (63% × 3.5%) + (37% × 1.5%). Compare the gap from 3.1% to that number with the full gap from 3.1% to 2.4%.
Answer: A — roughly 40–50% is the mix effect.
Day-1 rates with Day-3 mix = (63% × 3.5%) + (37% × 1.5%) ≈ 2.76%
Mix effect: 3.1% → 2.76% ≈ 0.34 points (about half)
Rate drop (the payment bug): 2.76% → 2.4% ≈ 0.36 points
Total: 0.7 points. Both matter — but only one of them is a bug.

The exact split depends on the order you calculate it in. Starting from Day-3 rates instead gives about 0.29 points for mix and 0.41 for the rate drop — so say "roughly 40–50% is mix" rather than a precise number.

Why the others fail
  • BIgnores the clear shift towards new users, who convert less.
  • CThis is the AI's original conclusion — which ignored the drop within each group.
  • DSimple guesstimate maths with the segment rates gives a good estimate.
The next hour

What is the first thing to do in the next hour?

The test for any action: does it fix the root cause, or only the symptom? The plan below does both — in the right order.

The recommended action plan

🔧 Fix now

Ask engineering to scale up the offer validation service — or temporarily apply the bank discount as post-payment cashback, so payments don't depend on the live check.

↩️ Recover

Send a retry nudge (app notification) to users whose partner-card payment failed since 12 PM.

📡 Monitor

Track payment success by method, every hour for the rest of the sale, and alert when any method drops sharply against the same hour on previous days.

🧊 Don't panic about the mix

Report conversion separately for new and existing users, so a traffic-mix shift doesn't trigger a false alarm. Improving new-user conversion is a separate, longer-term project.

🛡️ Prevent recurrence

Load-test the offer service before any offer is pushed to lightning deals in future sales. This is the one that addresses the root cause.

Checkpoints 11 & 12

Catch It Faster, Then Say It in Thirty Seconds

We found the problem at 6 PM, from the day-to-date dashboard. But it started at 12 PM. At about ₹1.7 crore an hour, those six hours cost roughly ₹10 crore. So the last analyst question is: how do we catch this faster next time?

11
Framework step · Conclude — prevent recurrence (Supporting metrics)
Which alert would have caught this?
Which alert would have caught this problem within about 15 minutes?
💡Think about three things: how often the metric updates, whether it points to what broke, and whether its normal level differs by method or by hour.
Answer: C — hourly payment success by method, against the same-hour baseline.

This is a Supporting (L2) metric: granular enough to show exactly which lever broke. Partner-card success fell from about 86% to 38% right after 12 PM — a huge drop against its own normal level.

Comparing each method with its own baseline for that hour matters, because normal levels differ: net banking and EMI usually succeed only about 80% of the time, so a single fixed threshold like "alert below 80%" would fire constantly for them.

Why the others fail
  • ATempting, and it would probably notice something. But overall conversion swings every hour with traffic mix, campaigns and lightning deals, so the threshold has to be loose. It reacts later and doesn't tell you what broke.
  • BThe right metric, but daily is far too slow during a sale.
  • DDaily is too slow, and the overall rate mixes the payment problem with the new-user mix effect.
⭐ North Star metrics tell you if you're winning. Supporting metrics, watched closely against their own baseline, tell you the moment something breaks.
12
Framework step · Conclude — summarise and recommend
Update leadership

You've solved it. Now the VP of the festival sale messages you: "What's going on? Give me a quick update." How you communicate the finding matters as much as finding it — and the VP has thirty seconds.

The task

A 2–3 line update to the VP

💡Cover five things, in this order: the impact, what happened, why, what we're doing, and when the next update is.

The model update

"We're losing about ₹1.7 crore of GMV an hour. Since 12 PM, about 6 in 10 partner-card payments fail because our offer service is overloaded. Engineering is scaling it, the offer is moving to post-payment cashback, and affected users get a retry nudge. Next update in 1 hour."

The structure to remember

Impact→ What happened→ Why→ Action→ Next update
What was left out on purpose

The new-user mix effect. It's real, but it's expected and not urgent — so it goes in the follow-up report, not the 30-second update. Knowing what to leave out is part of the skill.

Wrap-up

The Method Won

Look back at the first instincts: blame Flipkart, blame the new users, or check the data first. No single instinct got the full answer — and that's the lesson.

Checking the data

showed the drop was real

Funnel + timeline

found the 12 PM payment failure

Segmenting

found the new-user mix effect

External checks

ruled out Flipkart and the banks

Two causes, one drop. A payment bug (roughly half the fall, and a genuine emergency) and a traffic-mix shift (roughly the other half, real but expected). The method won by putting all the pieces together.

Key Takeaways

1
Clarify and rule out first

Make sure the number is real and the comparison is fair — including the time window. A 6 PM figure against a full previous day is not a drop, it's an artefact.

2
List hypotheses, then localize

One funnel check tells you which whole group of hypotheses to test first — far faster than working through them one at a time.

3
Timing is a clue

A sudden step means something changed or broke. A lower level from day to day means behaviour or mix shifted. The shape of the line tells you which kind of problem you have.

4
Rule out red herrings with data

The iOS release, Flipkart and the banks all sounded convincing. The evidence didn't support any of them. Conviction isn't evidence.

5
Check each segment's own rate

An overall drop can be a mix effect, a real rate drop, or both. Here it was both — and only looking inside each segment revealed it.

6
Keep asking "why?"

Separate the symptom from the direct cause from the root cause — and fix the root cause. "Payments are failing" is where you start, not where you stop.

7
Put a number on the problem, with a time window

"₹1.7 crore an hour" gets engineering out of bed; "conversion is down" does not. And say whether it's an upper bound.

8
Monitor supporting metrics against their own baseline

Granular, frequent alerts catch failures in minutes, not hours. Fixed thresholds across methods with different normal levels just create noise.

9
Communicate in thirty seconds

Impact → what happened → why → action → next update. And deliberately leave out what isn't urgent.

10
AI spots patterns; the analyst validates them

The AI's answer was real and incomplete. Never accept an AI conclusion without checking it against every segment and the rest of your evidence.
