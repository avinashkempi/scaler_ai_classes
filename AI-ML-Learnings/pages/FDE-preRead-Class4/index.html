The answer that sounds right

In Class 3, you moved beyond the Meridian prototype and examined the environment around it.

You traced where important facts came from. You kept missing information visible. You separated what the customer asked for from what the official record said. You also kept the final decision with a human.

Now imagine that all of this work has been compressed into one instruction:

Review this return case and tell us what to do.

An AI system may still produce an answer. It may be clear, polite, and confident. It may even sound exactly like the answer an experienced support agent would give.

But what did it have to guess?

Did review mean summarise the case, draft a reply, recommend a next step, or approve the return? Which policy was it expected to use? Was the delivery date available? Which record should be trusted if two pieces of information differed? What was the system forbidden from deciding?

A fluent answer can hide every one of those gaps.

This creates a difficult risk. The better the answer sounds, the easier it becomes to forget that the model may be working with incomplete evidence, unclear definitions, or authority it was never supposed to exercise.

The specialist with the wrong envelope

Think of an AI model as a capable specialist sitting in a room with a sealed envelope.

The specialist can work only with what is inside. If the envelope contains the task, the relevant facts, the meaning of important fields, the rules, the boundaries, and the required form of the answer, the specialist has a fair chance of producing useful work.

If the envelope is missing the delivery date, the specialist cannot discover it by sounding more certain. If the latest policy is absent, a beautifully written instruction cannot recreate it. If nobody has stated that refund approval belongs to a human, the specialist may cross a boundary without knowing that it exists.

The question is therefore not only, “How intelligent is the model?”

It is also, “What exactly did we place inside the envelope?”

The smallest complete envelope

When people first work with AI, they often assume that more context is always better. They attach every document they can find, write a very long instruction, and hope the model will locate what matters.

That can create a different problem. Relevant facts become harder to find. Old definitions sit beside current ones. Background information looks like a rule. A model may spend attention on material that has nothing to do with the decision in front of it.

The goal is not the largest possible information pack. It is the smallest complete one for the task.

That means making five things explicit:

  • Task: What should the model actually do?
  • Evidence: Which supplied facts may it use?
  • Definitions: What do the important fields and states mean?
  • Boundaries: What must it not decide, claim, or invent?
  • Output: What structure should the result follow so another person can inspect it?

A controlled information pack does not guarantee a correct answer. It does something more basic: it makes the task, evidence, and limits visible enough that the result can be checked.

Two kinds of AI in the same project

There is another distinction that is easy to miss.

AI can help an engineer inspect and change an application. AI can also operate inside that application to prepare an output for a user. These may rely on similar underlying technology, but they are not doing the same job.

The first sees selected project files, instructions, and permitted commands. Its work is judged through proposed changes, tests, and human code review.

The second sees selected case evidence, policy, definitions, and an output requirement. Its work is judged against the supplied facts and the behaviour expected for that case.

Both require supervision, but at different points.

An engineer must approve what may change in the software. A reviewer must still own the customer-facing decision. Calling both activities simply “AI” hides the permissions, evidence, and human responsibilities that make each one safe.

Not every step needs intelligence

Suppose a delivery date is required and the field is empty.

Recognising that the field is empty is an exact check. Ordinary code can perform it consistently and testably. Asking a model to reason about something that can be expressed as a fixed condition adds uncertainty without adding value.

Not every step needs intelligence

Now suppose the customer describes the same problem in dozens of different ways. Understanding that varied language may be a useful task for a model.

Finally, suppose the next step is to approve a refund. That action carries business authority. It should remain with an authorised person or an agreed business process.

A dependable workflow may therefore use all three:

  • Normal code for fixed checks and predictable rules.
  • An AI model for interpreting or drafting across variable language.
  • A human for decisions that require authority, accountability, or policy judgment.

The most advanced choice is not always the choice that uses the most AI. It is the choice that places each responsibility where it can be handled most dependably.

Fluency is not evidence

Even when the right task is given to a model, the output still needs to be inspected.

A reviewer should be able to ask: Which source supports this fact? Was required information missing? Did two records disagree? Which policy rule was used? Did the output stay inside the agreed boundary?

This is why a structured answer is useful. Important facts can appear in named fields. Missing information can remain visible. Claims can point back to supplied sources. The next human action can be stated explicitly.

But structure alone is not proof. A wrong answer placed neatly inside valid fields is still a wrong answer.

The result must be compared with something outside the model's own opinion: the supplied evidence, the agreed rules, and the expected behaviour already established by people.

The question at the centre of Class 4

Class 4 moves one step forward from the manual trace created in Class 3.

We will ask how AI can contribute without being asked to rediscover missing facts, replace fixed controls, or assume human authority.

That brings us to the central question:

How do we make AI useful without allowing a convincing answer to replace evidence, dependable controls, or human judgment?

What sits around an AI answer

In Class 4, we will examine five connected responsibilities:

  • Control the context: Give the model the smallest complete information pack for its task.
  • Route the work: Decide what belongs to normal code, a model, or a human.
  • Structure the result: Make facts, gaps, differences, and next actions visible.
  • Link claims to evidence: Let a reviewer locate the supplied source behind an important statement.
  • Evaluate externally: Compare the output with agreed behaviour and evidence rather than trusting its confidence.

What will Class 4 do?

Class 4 is a working session. By the end of it, you should be able to:

  • explain the main parts surrounding an AI-assisted application in simple language;
  • distinguish between AI used to help build software and AI used inside the software;
  • prepare a controlled information pack for one clearly bounded AI task;
  • choose between normal code, an AI model, and a human decision - and explain why;
  • design an output that keeps important facts connected to supplied evidence;
  • supervise a small, bounded improvement without silently changing existing behaviour; and
  • identify missing information, preserved conflicts, and unsupported claims by comparing the result with an external reference.

You will not be expected to memorise AI terminology or accept an output because it sounds impressive. The useful skill is learning to control what the system receives, preserve the boundaries around what it may do, and demand evidence for what it claims.

What to bring to class

Nothing to install. Nothing to submit. Bring three things in your head.

One. Think of a time when an AI answer sounded convincing but was difficult to verify. What information, source, or definition would have made the answer easier to trust?

Two. Think of one task from your own work that follows an exact rule, one that requires interpreting varied language, and one that requires authority. Would you place all three with the same kind of system?

Three. Return to Meridian's missing delivery date. If an AI system produced a polished return recommendation anyway, what evidence would reveal that the answer had crossed the boundary?

Class 3 gave us a manual reference for what the evidence supports and what must remain unknown.

Class 4 uses that reference to decide what AI should receive, what role it should play, and how its work should be checked.

See you there.
