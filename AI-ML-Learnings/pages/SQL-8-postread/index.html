Opening Hook — Why Is Our Data Split Into So Many Tables? 🤔

MANAGER
I want a report showing every staff member's name, their department's location, and their salary grade's role level — all in one view.
ANALYST
Department location lives in the departments table. Role level lives in salary_grades. Staff details live in staff. None of it is in one place.
MANAGER
Then bring it together.

Real-world databases are deliberately split into many small tables instead of one giant table — this is called normalization. It avoids repeating a department's location on every single staff row, and avoids repeating a salary band's range on every single staff row. Each fact lives in exactly one place. The cost of this design is that answering a real business question almost always means reading from more than one table at once. The tool that makes this possible is the JOIN.

⭐ Data is split for cleanliness. JOINs bring it back together for analysis.

Relationships — How Tables Connect 🧩

Every relationship between two tables can be described by how many rows on one side correspond to rows on the other side. Three possibilities exist — and each one you will encounter in real hotel data.

1:1, 1:N, and M:N — the three relationship blueprints, each with a leela_palace example

1️⃣ One-to-One (1:1)

One record on each side. No repeating. The Foreign Key is always unique on the side that holds it. Hotel example: one staff member ↔ one current salary record (if we only ever track the latest salary). Rare in practice — most systems favor 1:N so history can be tracked.

2️⃣ One-to-Many (1:N) — the most common relationship

One row on the "one" side can relate to many rows on the "many" side. The Foreign Key always sits on the many side. Hotel examples:

  • One department ↔ many staff members (department_id is the FK, sitting on staff)
  • One salary_grade ↔ many staff members who share that grade
  • One staff member ↔ many staff_salaries rows (salary history over time)

3️⃣ Many-to-Many (M:N)

Many rows on one side can relate to many rows on the other side — and this cannot be stored directly in a two-table structure. It requires a junction (bridge) table that breaks the M:N relationship into two clean 1:N relationships. Hotel example: many guests ↔ many spa_services, connected through the spa_bookings junction table. A guest can book many services; a service can be booked by many guests.

RelationshipRuleWhere the FK lives
1:1One record on each side, never repeatsEither side (unique)
1:NOne side has many matching rows on the otherAlways on the "many" side
M:NBoth sides can repeat against each otherCannot be direct — needs a junction table
⭐ Get the relationship right first. The relationship tells you which column is the Foreign Key — and that decides how you write the JOIN.

What Is a JOIN — Concept Before Syntax 🔧

A JOIN does not add new data anywhere. It does not change a single row in your actual tables. It matches and aligns rows from two tables, side by side, on the fly — only for the duration of that one query.

Think of it like this visually — before the JOIN:

SQL looks at every row in the anchor table, hunts for a match in the target table, and fuses them into a temporary "virtual table"

Notice in the diagram: staff_id 3 (Mike Brown) is missing from the joined result even though it exists in the staff table — because there was no row 3 present with a match under the join logic shown. This is the first hint of a theme we return to later — JOINs can silently drop rows if the matching condition isn't satisfied.

🧲
The Magnet Analogy: Imagine laying two decks of cards side by side — one deck is staff, the other is departments. A magnet scans every card in the staff deck, reads its department_id, and pulls over the matching department card from the other deck, gluing them together temporarily. Once the query finishes, the glue disappears — the original two decks are untouched.

JOIN Types Overview — The Map 🗺️

Five types exist. Today we cover INNER JOIN only. This is the map — so you know where we are headed.

INNER JOIN handles roughly 80% of day-to-day analytical queries — LEFT, RIGHT, FULL OUTER, CROSS, and SELF JOIN build on the same foundation
JOIN typeWhat it returnsCovered
INNER JOINOnly rows with a match in BOTH tables✅ Today
LEFT JOINAll rows from the left table + matches from the rightLecture 9
RIGHT JOINAll rows from the right table + matches from the leftLecture 9
FULL OUTER JOINAll rows where there's a match in either tableLecture 9
CROSS JOINEvery row of A paired with every row of B (Cartesian product)Later

💡 Rule of thumb

Get the strictest JOIN right first — INNER JOIN — and the rest follow naturally once you understand how matching works.

Table Aliases — Mandatory Once Two Tables Are Involved 🏷️

Both staff and departments could have overlapping column names, or the query simply gets long and repetitive writing leela_palace.staff.department_id every time. The fix: give each table a short alias right after naming it in FROM or JOIN.

📄 Aliasing two tables
FROM        leela_palace.staff       AS s
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.departments AS d
  ON        s.department_id = d.department_id
🗣️ Reads as: staff becomes "s", departments becomes "d". Every column reference downstream — in SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY — can now use the short prefix instead of the full table name.
RuleWhy it matters
Alias right after the table name (AS s, or just s)AS is optional but improves readability
Use the alias everywhere after — SELECT, ON, WHEREConsistency avoids "which staff?" ambiguity errors
Keep aliases short and meaningful (s for staff, d for departments)Long queries stay readable
If both tables have a column with the same name (e.g. both have status)SQL throws an "ambiguous column" error unless prefixed with the alias
⚠️ Forgetting the alias prefix on an ambiguous column

If both staff and departments had a name column and you wrote SELECT name without a prefix, BigQuery would throw: "Column name is ambiguous." Always write s.first_name or d.department_name once two tables are in play.

6

The ON Clause — The Load-Bearing Pillar 🏛️

ON is the most important line in any JOIN. It defines the matching condition. Getting it wrong silently produces wrong results — no error message.

ON connects the Foreign Key in one table to the Primary Key in the other — think of it as the WHERE clause for your JOIN
✅ The Golden Rule
  • Think of ON as the WHERE clause for your JOIN.
  • Always connect the Foreign Key (FK) in one table to the Primary Key (PK) of the other.
🚨 The Silent Error
  • Connecting the wrong columns (e.g. s.department_id = s.staff_id) will not trigger a system error.
  • It will run perfectly and return entirely meaningless data. You own this logic — SQL will not warn you.
⭐ Get the ON clause right, and your results will be reliable and accurate. Get it wrong, and nothing will tell you.

INNER JOIN Syntax — Your First Live Query 🚀

MANAGER
Give me every staff member, their employment type, and their exact department location.
📄 Query 7.1 — First INNER JOIN: staff + departments
SELECT      s.first_name,
            s.last_name,
            s.employment_type,
            d.department_name,
            d.location
FROM        leela_palace.staff       AS s
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.departments AS d
  ON        s.department_id = d.department_id;
🗣️ Reads as: For each staff member, find the matching department (where department_id matches), then return columns from both tables together.
Anchor table (s) + Target table (d), matched on the Connection Condition (ON), producing names + locations

How it reads logically: start with staff (s), bring in departments (d), match them where department_id is identical, and pull specific columns from the newly fused virtual table.

KeywordRole
SELECTChoose columns — can pull from either table using the alias prefix
FROM ... AS sThe anchor (left/first) table
INNER JOIN ... AS dThe target table to connect
ON s.col = d.colThe matching condition — FK to PK

Four Business Scenarios — JOINs in Action 📋

Scenario 2 — Staff + Salary Grade

MANAGER
Show every staff member alongside their salary grade name and role level.
📄 Query 8.1 — staff + salary_grades
SELECT      s.staff_id,
            s.first_name,
            s.role,
            g.grade_name,
            g.role_level
FROM        leela_palace.staff         AS s
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.salary_grades AS g
  ON        s.grade_id = g.grade_id;
🗣️ Reads as: For every staff row, look up the matching salary grade using grade_id, and attach the grade name and role level.

Scenario 3 — Staff + Department + Salary (a 3-table JOIN)

MANAGER
I need staff name, department name, and their current monthly salary — all in one row.
📄 Query 8.2 — three tables joined in a chain
SELECT      s.first_name,
            s.last_name,
            d.department_name,
            ss.monthly_salary_inr
FROM        leela_palace.staff          AS s
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.departments    AS d
  ON        s.department_id = d.department_id
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.staff_salaries AS ss
  ON        s.staff_id = ss.staff_id;
🗣️ Reads as: Start from staff. Chain in departments using department_id. Chain in staff_salaries using staff_id. Every JOIN adds one more ON clause, connecting a new table to something already in the chain.
Note: A 3-table JOIN is just two INNER JOINs stacked back to back. Each new INNER JOIN ... ON ... block can reference any table already joined so far — not just the very first one.

Scenario 4 — Aggregation Across Joined Tables (Headcount per Department)

MANAGER
How many staff members work in each department, by department name (not just the ID)?
📄 Query 8.3 — JOIN + GROUP BY for headcount
SELECT      d.department_name,
            COUNT(*) AS headcount
FROM        leela_palace.staff       AS s
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.departments AS d
  ON        s.department_id = d.department_id
GROUP BY    d.department_name
ORDER BY    headcount DESC;
🗣️ Reads as: Join staff to departments to get readable names, then count how many staff rows land in each department name group, sorted busiest first.

Scenario 5 — JOIN + WHERE + Aggregation (Average Salary of Active Staff, per Department)

MANAGER
What is the average monthly salary of active staff, calculated per department?
📄 Query 8.4 — the complete analytical pipeline
SELECT      d.department_name,
            ROUND(AVG(ss.monthly_salary_inr), 0) AS avg_salary
FROM        leela_palace.staff          AS s
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.departments    AS d
  ON        s.department_id = d.department_id
INNER JOIN  leela_palace.staff_salaries AS ss
  ON        s.staff_id = ss.staff_id
WHERE       s.status = 'active'
GROUP BY    d.department_name
ORDER BY    avg_salary DESC;
🗣️ Reads as: Fuse all three tables. Keep only active staff. Group the survivors by department. Calculate the average salary per group.

The Universal JOIN Query Pattern

Every JOIN query — no matter how many tables — follows the same six building blocks in the same order:

  1. FROM → anchor table
  2. INNER JOIN → target table(s)
  3. ON → matching condition (FK = PK)
  4. WHERE → filter rows (before grouping)
  5. GROUP BY → group similar rows together
  6. ORDER BY → sort the result
Execution order: connect the tables first, filter the rows second, aggregate the surviving dataset last
⭐ Connect the tables first. Filter the rows second. Aggregate the surviving dataset last. That order never changes.

Non-Matching Rows — INNER JOIN's Silent Trap ⚠️

Recall from the schema: staff.department_id is NULL for new joiners who haven't been assigned to a department yet. What happens to those staff members in an INNER JOIN against departments?

MANAGER
What's our average salary across the whole hotel?
ANALYST
I ran the staff + departments + staff_salaries JOIN and got the average... but wait, this doesn't include the staff still waiting on department assignment. They just vanished from the query.

INNER JOIN requires a match on both sides. If a staff member hasn't been assigned a department yet (their department_id is NULL), there is nothing on the departments side to match against — they silently disappear from the result. No error. No warning. The row is simply gone.

500 total staff → INNER JOIN on department_id → only 487 emerge matched, 13 staff (missing department IDs) drop entirely
RiskConsequence
INNER JOIN drops staff with no department yetHeadcount reports undercount reality
Averages computed only on matched rowsReporting an undercounted average salary without knowing it can trigger wrong business decisions
🎮 SIMULATOR — Watch INNER JOIN Drop Unmatched Rows!
Some staff below have a NULL department_id (new joiners). Run the INNER JOIN and see exactly who survives — and who silently disappears.
🎯 The Fix

Rule: always check COUNT(*) from the JOIN vs COUNT(*) from the source table before trusting any aggregate.

SELECT COUNT(*) FROM leela_palace.staff vs SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (your joined query) — if the numbers differ, rows were dropped. (LEFT JOIN, covered in Lecture 9, is the fix when you need to keep unmatched rows too.)

⭐ INNER JOIN = keeps matches only. Unmatched rows disappear silently. Always validate row counts!

Interview-Style Questions — Self-Check 🎤

Q1. What is the difference between a 1:N and an M:N relationship? Give a hotel example of each.
In a 1:N relationship, one row on the "one" side can relate to many rows on the "many" side, and the Foreign Key sits on the many side — e.g., one department has many staff members. In an M:N relationship, rows on both sides can relate to many rows on the other, and it cannot be stored directly — it needs a junction table. Example: many guests can book many spa services, connected through the spa_bookings junction table.
Q2. Why is getting the ON clause wrong more dangerous than a syntax error?
A syntax error stops the query from running at all — it's obvious and gets fixed immediately. An incorrect ON clause (e.g., joining on the wrong columns) runs perfectly fine and returns a full result set — just one that is logically meaningless. Because there's no error message, this kind of mistake can go unnoticed and feed wrong numbers directly into a business report.
Q3. Why might an INNER JOIN report show fewer staff than actually exist in the staff table? How would you catch this?
INNER JOIN only returns rows that have a match in both tables. If a staff member's department_id is NULL (e.g., a new joiner not yet assigned to a department), there is no matching department row, so that staff member is silently dropped from the result. To catch this, always compare COUNT(*) from the joined query against COUNT(*) from the original staff table — a mismatch reveals dropped rows.
