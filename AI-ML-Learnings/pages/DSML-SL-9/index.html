Mission 1

Meet the problem

Which of these two messages is spam?

Mail 1"I, Nigerian prince, need your help. Send money."
Mail 2"Meeting scheduled for 5pm. Kindly revert."

Obviously Mail 1. How did you know? A few words gave it away instantly — "Nigerian prince," words like "lottery" or "million dollars" that show up disproportionately in spam. That instinct, judging a message by which words appear in it, is exactly the idea behind Naive Bayes.

What's in the data

Each row in MailNest's dataset has a message and a label: spam (class 1) or ham (class 0, meaning "not spam"). A message is just a sequence of words — message $i$ can be written as $w_1^{(i)}, w_2^{(i)}, \ldots, w_k^{(i)}$, where $k$ is that message's own length. Different messages have different lengths.

A notation heads-up: $k$ always refers to one specific message's own word count. Later we'll need a separate letter, $V$, for the total number of unique words across the entire dataset — a fixed, much larger number. Don't let the two blur together.

Far more ham than spam — a class imbalance. Keep it in mind: Naive Bayes has a specific vulnerability tied to how little data some words appear in, which shows up later in Mission 6.

Mission 2

Cleaning the text

Raw text is messy. Before any model can use it, it needs cleaning, in four steps. Click through them below, following one real message as it transforms.

Tokenization splits each message into individual words.

"Hi my name is Anthony."→["hi","my","name","is","anthony","."]

Lower-casing — "GO" and "go" should count as the same word, so every word becomes lowercase. (This example was already lowercase, so nothing visibly changes, but the step still runs on every token.)

["hi","my","name","is","anthony","."]→["hi","my","name","is","anthony","."]

Removing punctuation — since we only care which keywords appear, punctuation carries no useful signal and is stripped out.

["hi","my","name","is","anthony","."]→["hi","my","name","is","anthony"]

Removing stopwords — words like "a," "an," "the," "of," "is" are extremely common but carry almost no information about spam versus ham. Removing them also makes everything downstream cheaper to compute.

["hi","my","name","is","anthony"]→["hi","name","anthony"]
A caveat worth remembering Removing stopwords introduces grammatical errors into the text. That's fine here, since only keyword signal matters — not fluent language. Tasks like translation or question-answering, where grammar and word order genuinely matter, cannot afford to remove stopwords this way.

Frequent isn't the same as distinguishing

They overlap more than you'd think. Words like "call," "u," "2," "4" and "get" are frequent in both — they're just common in text messages generally, spam or not. What matters isn't whether a word is frequent, it's whether its frequency differs between the two classes.

"Call" is the single most common word in spam messages, but it's also common in ham. What Naive Bayes actually uses is the rate at which each word shows up in one class versus the other — not just whether it appears at all. A handful of words, like "free," "claim," and "prize," are frequent in spam and genuinely rare in ham; those are the ones doing the real work.

Mission 3

The math — Bayes' theorem

To classify a new message, we compare two probabilities and predict whichever is higher:

$$P(y=1 \mid \text{text}) \quad \text{vs} \quad P(y=0 \mid \text{text})$$

Since a text is just a sequence of words $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_k$, this is really $P(y=1 \mid w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_k)$.

Bayes' theorem

For any two events $A$ and $B$:

Bayes' theorem$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}$$

Let $X$ be the event describing this exact message: which words are present, and which are absent. Applying Bayes' theorem, with $Z = P(X)$:

$$P(y=1 \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid y=1) \cdot P(y=1)}{Z} \qquad P(y=0 \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid y=0) \cdot P(y=0)}{Z}$$

Since we only care which of the two is larger, and $Z$ is identical in both, we can drop it entirely and just compare:

What we actually compare$$P(X \mid y=1) \cdot P(y=1) \quad \text{vs} \quad P(X \mid y=0) \cdot P(y=0)$$

$P(y=1)$ and $P(y=0)$ are simple to compute: just the fraction of training messages that are spam, and the fraction that are ham. These are the class priors.

Try it: fire and smoke

Dangerous fires are rare, smoke is fairly common (mostly from barbecues), and most dangerous fires do produce smoke. Hover each value, then reveal how they combine.

P(fire) = 0.01The prior: how rare dangerous fires are overall. P(smoke) = 0.1How common smoke is overall, mostly from barbecues. P(smoke | fire) = 0.9Of all dangerous fires, 90% produce smoke.

Question: given that you see smoke, what's the probability there's a dangerous fire — $P(\text{fire}\mid\text{smoke})$?

$$P(\text{fire}\mid\text{smoke}) = \frac{P(\text{smoke}\mid\text{fire}) \cdot P(\text{fire})}{P(\text{smoke})} = \frac{0.9 \times 0.01}{0.1} = 0.09$$

Even though smoke is a strong signal of fire, fire is so rare to begin with that seeing smoke only raises confidence to 9%, not certainty. This same logic is what lets Naive Bayes combine rare-but-telling words with how common spam is overall.

Mission 4

The naive assumption

$P(X \mid y=1)$ means: "given that a message is spam, what's the probability it looks exactly like this — this specific pattern of which words are present and which are absent?"

Why this is a problem

To estimate this directly from data, we'd need training messages that are spam and match this exact pattern across every word in the vocabulary. For any message longer than a few words, almost no training example will match exactly — the probability collapses toward zero, and it's expensive to even search for.

The fix: assume independence, given the class

Naive Bayes makes a deliberately simplifying assumption: whether any one word appears is independent of whether any other word appears, conditioned on the class. If "Nigerian" and "prince" always appear together in spam messages, we still assume, for the sake of the math, that their presence is independent once we already know the message is spam.

Independence assumption: off

Being precise about what gets multiplied

The vocabulary has $V$ unique words in total. For every one of those $V$ words, treat it as a yes/no event: does this specific word appear in the message, or not? With the independence assumption, the probability of the entire message's pattern becomes a product over all $V$ vocabulary words, not just the ones that happen to appear:

Likelihood of the whole message, under independence $$P(X \mid y=1) = \prod_{j=1}^{V} P(w_j \mid y=1)^{[w_j \text{ present}]} \cdot \big(1 - P(w_j \mid y=1)\big)^{[w_j \text{ absent}]}$$

In words: for each vocabulary word that's present, multiply in the probability that word appears in spam; for each word that's absent, multiply in the probability it's absent. Every one of the $V$ words contributes a factor, whether it showed up in this particular message or not.

Why "naive"?

Because this assumption plainly isn't true. "Nigerian" and "prince" genuinely aren't independent — they tend to show up together. The assumption is naive precisely because it ignores that, and it works anyway, well enough to be useful.

A common mistake The independence assumption is about the conditional probability — independent given the class — not the raw, unconditional probability of the words. The words can absolutely be correlated overall; we only assume independence once we already know whether the message is spam or ham.

Computing each likelihood term

$P(w_j \mid y=1)$ is simply: out of all spam messages, what fraction contain the word $w_j$?

$$P(w_j \mid y=1) = \frac{\text{number of spam messages containing } w_j}{\text{total number of spam messages}}$$

Putting it all together

The posterior for each class $$P(y=1 \mid X) \propto P(y=1) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{V} P(w_j \mid y=1)^{[w_j \text{ present}]} \big(1-P(w_j \mid y=1)\big)^{[w_j \text{ absent}]}$$ $$P(y=0 \mid X) \propto P(y=0) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{V} P(w_j \mid y=0)^{[w_j \text{ present}]} \big(1-P(w_j \mid y=0)\big)^{[w_j \text{ absent}]}$$

In practice, absent words with likelihoods close to typical background rates contribute similar factors to both classes and roughly cancel out; most of the discriminating signal comes from the words genuinely present in the message. In words: posterior probability is proportional to (class prior) × (product of likelihoods). This combination of Bayes' theorem with the naive independence assumption is exactly where the name "Naive Bayes" comes from.

Mission 5

What happens at training and test time

Every unique word across the dataset becomes a feature. This full set of unique words is the corpus, or vocabulary, with size $V$. Every message, regardless of its own length $k$, gets represented in terms of the same, shared $V$ vocabulary words.

"Training," for Naive Bayes

Unlike Logistic Regression or a Neural Network, there's no gradient descent, no iterative weight updates. "Training" simply means computing and storing:

  • 2 class priors: $P(y=1)$ and $P(y=0)$
  • 2V likelihoods: $P(w_j \mid y=1)$ and $P(w_j \mid y=0)$, for every one of the $V$ vocabulary words

These stored numbers are Naive Bayes' equivalent of a model's learned weights.

Training time — O(n · V)O(2000)
Prediction time — O(V)O(50)
Space — O(2 + 2V) = O(V)O(50)

Prediction time depends only on $V$, not on the new message's own length $k$ — every vocabulary word is checked, present or absent.

Mission 6

The zero-probability problem

The vocabulary $V$ is built entirely from the training data. A genuinely new word at test time, never seen in training at all, simply isn't one of the $V$ modeled features — it contributes no factor and is effectively ignored. That case, on its own, doesn't break anything.

The real danger is subtler: suppose a word $w'$ is part of the vocabulary, since it showed up in some training messages, but it happened to appear in zero spam messages, while appearing in several ham messages.

Think it through Given that $w'$ never occurred in any spam message during training, what do you expect $P(w' \mid y=1)$ to come out to?
$$P(w' \mid y=1) = \frac{\text{spam messages containing } w'}{\text{total spam messages}} = \frac{0}{n_1} = 0$$ Exactly zero — and that's where the trouble starts.

Now a new test message arrives and it contains $w'$. The formula multiplies in $P(w' \mid y=1)$ for present words — toggle the demo below to see what one zero factor does to the whole product.

Word w′ is a brand-new word (never seen in training)
P(y=1)×P(w₁|y=1)×P(w₂|y=1)×… (w′ skipped, not in V)
Result: normal comparison — ham vs. spam still competes fairly

When $w'$ is in the vocabulary but has zero spam count: $P(y=1 \mid X) \propto P(y=1) \cdot 0 \cdot (\text{everything else}) = 0$. Meanwhile $w'$ did appear in some ham messages, so the ham-side product stays nonzero. Spam scores exactly 0 no matter what any other word in the message suggests — one rare word has silently overridden everything else.

Why not just add every English word to the vocabulary in advance, so this never happens? That would make $V$ enormous, and both the $O(n \cdot V)$ training time and $O(V)$ space from Mission 5 would balloon along with it. Not a sensible fix.
The fix

Laplace (additive) smoothing

Add a small constant to both the numerator and denominator of every likelihood calculation, so it can never land on exactly zero:

Smoothed likelihood $$P(w_j \mid y=1) = \frac{(\text{count of } w_j \text{ in spam}) + \alpha}{n_1 + C\alpha}$$

where $\alpha$ is a smoothing constant (commonly 1), and $C$ is the number of distinct values the word-feature can take. Since a word either appears in a message or it doesn't, $w_j$ is a Bernoulli variable with exactly 2 possible values, so $C = 2$.

Important This smoothing isn't applied only to brand-new words. Every single likelihood, for every word, in both training and prediction, uses this same smoothed formula.

A worked example — try different values of alpha

A spam class has $n_1 = 100$ messages. For a brand-new word $w'$ that appeared in 0 of them, drag the slider to see how the smoothing constant $\alpha$ changes the resulting probability.

α = 0α = 1α = 10
P(w′ | y=1) = (0 + 1) / (100 + 2×1) = 0.0098
Edge case: what if there were zero spam messages at all (n₁ = 0)?

With no spam data whatsoever, P(w′|y=1) = (0+1)/(0+2) = 0.5 — exactly the right amount of uncertainty, since there's no reason to believe a word is more likely present than absent.

Notice the direction: as $\alpha$ grows large, the added constant dominates the real counts, and every word's smoothed probability drifts toward 0.5 — the model trusts the data less and the smoothing more.

Wrap-up

What we now know

  • Raw text needs cleaning before it's usable: tokenizing into words, lowercasing, stripping punctuation, and removing stopwords, all to isolate the keyword signal that actually distinguishes spam from ham.
  • Bayes' theorem lets us flip P(words | class), which we can estimate from data, into P(class | words), which is what we actually want to predict.
  • The naive assumption treats words as independent of each other, given the class. It's rarely true in reality — which is exactly why it's called naive — but it makes the math tractable and works well in practice.
  • The final rule: posterior probability is proportional to (class prior) × (product of per-word likelihoods); predict whichever class scores higher.
  • Naive Bayes "trains" by computing and storing class priors and per-word likelihoods — no gradient descent. Training is O(n·V); prediction is O(V); space is O(V).
  • A single unseen word can collapse an entire prediction to zero, since all likelihoods multiply together. Laplace Smoothing fixes this by adding small constants to every likelihood calculation, not just new words.
