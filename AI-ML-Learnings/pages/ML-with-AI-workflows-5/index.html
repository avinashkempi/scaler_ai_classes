01

The Used-Car Marketplace

Regression vs. classification · encoding · scaling · data leakage

Your manager looks at the Wheelio pricing feature and says: "This is obviously a regression problem — just throw everything into a Linear Regression model and ship it."

  1. aWhy is predicting final_selling_price a regression task rather than a classification task? Give one scenario where the same target column could instead be framed as classification.
  2. bfuel_type and transmission are categorical. How should each be encoded for a Linear Regression model, and why does one-hot encoding a variable with k categories need only k−1 columns when an intercept term is present?
  3. ckm_driven ranges from 0 to ~200,000 while age_years ranges from 0 to 15. Does an ordinary Linear Regression fit via the normal equation strictly require feature scaling to produce correct coefficients? Does it matter if the same model is trained with gradient descent instead?
  4. dYour manager wants to add days_to_sell as a feature to help predict final_selling_price. What's wrong with this, in the correct terminology?

a — Regression vs. classification

final_selling_price is a continuous numeric quantity, and what matters is how much it is, not just which bucket it falls into — so this is regression. The same underlying data becomes a classification problem the moment the target is bucketed: for example, predicting whether a car lands in a "Premium" (>₹8L) or "Budget" (≤₹8L) price band.

b — Encoding the categoricals

fuel_type (4 categories) and transmission (2 categories) are both nominal, with no natural order, so one-hot encoding is the right approach. Label encoding would be wrong here — assigning Petrol=0, Diesel=1, CNG=2, Electric=3 would tell the model "Electric" is three times "more" of something than Petrol, which is meaningless.

If a variable has k categories and you create k one-hot columns while also keeping the intercept, those k columns always sum to exactly 1 for every row — one column becomes an exact linear combination of the others plus the intercept. This is the dummy variable trap: a case of perfect multicollinearity. The design matrix becomes singular, so \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) doesn't exist and the normal equation has no unique solution. Dropping one category — which becomes the reference category, absorbed into the intercept — removes the redundancy. So transmission needs only one column (e.g. is_automatic), and fuel_type needs three columns for its four categories.

Why k columns break the model, and k−1 fixes it

Four fuel-type columns always sum to 1 for every row — an exact linear combination of the intercept. Drop one category and the redundancy disappears.

c — Does scaling matter?

Normal equation: no — scaling isn't required for correctness. \(\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) finds the exact minimizer of the squared-error cost regardless of each feature's scale; the coefficients simply come out in different units to compensate (a tiny coefficient for km_driven, a large one for age_years). Predictions and R² are identical either way. In practice, extreme scale differences can cause numerical instability while computing the matrix inverse — but that's a floating-point precision issue, not a correctness issue.

Gradient descent: yes — scaling matters a great deal, though it still doesn't change what the correct answer is, only how easily you get there. Features on very different scales stretch the cost surface into a long, narrow elliptical bowl rather than a circular one, which makes gradient descent zig-zag and converge slowly. This geometry is explored in full under Scaling & Gradient Descent. Scaling is about optimization efficiency, not about changing the theoretically correct coefficients.

d — The leakage problem

days_to_sell is only known after a car has already sold — it's generated by the same process being predicted. In real deployment, at the moment a listing is created, this feature simply doesn't exist yet. Using it during training is a textbook case of data leakage: the model learns a relationship that depends on future information relative to when the prediction actually needs to be made. It would look unrealistically strong during training and testing, then fail in production, where days_to_sell is unknown at prediction time.

The Hospital Readmission Model

Train/test splits · time-based leakage · group leakage

A hospital chain wants to predict length_of_stay (in days) for incoming patients, to help with bed allocation. A junior analyst builds a dataset from three years of patient records and runs a standard 80/20 random split. The model scores an impressive R² = 0.91 on the test set. Deployed on new patients at admission time, however, its errors are almost twice as large as expected — no better than a much simpler model using only age and admitting_diagnosis.

The full feature list: age, admitting_diagnosis, number_of_lab_tests_ordered, discharge_summary_word_count, total_medication_doses_administered, attending_physician_id.

  1. aWhich feature(s) are almost certainly causing data leakage? Explain the mechanism — why does the model perform "too well" in testing but fail at deployment?
  2. bThe dataset spans 3 years, and patient volume/case-mix has shifted noticeably. Why might a random split still give an overly optimistic estimate of real-world performance here, and what kind of split would be more appropriate?
  3. cSuppose the same patient appears multiple times in the dataset across separate visits. What new problem does this introduce for a random split, and how would you fix it?

a — The leaking features

discharge_summary_word_count and total_medication_doses_administered are both generated during or after the hospital stay — you can't know either until the stay is essentially over, which is exactly the quantity being predicted. They're highly correlated with length_of_stay because they're downstream consequences of it, not signals available at admission time.

Mechanism: during training and testing these columns exist for every historical patient, so the model learns a strong but non-deployable relationship — longer stays produce more doses administered, so the model "predicts" length of stay by essentially reading a proxy for the answer. This inflates R² because the leakage is baked into every row, train and test alike. At deployment, made at admission, none of this future information exists yet, so the model's apparent skill evaporates.

b — Random splits over a shifting population

A random 80/20 split scatters all three years of data into both train and test. The model is often evaluated on patients from periods it has effectively already seen similar patients from — a form of temporal leakage. It inflates the test score because the "unseen" test set is statistically similar to the training distribution in a way that genuinely future patients won't be.

Better approach: a time-based (out-of-time) split — train on years 1–2, test on year 3 — mimics real deployment: trained on the past, evaluated on a genuinely future period, including any distribution shifts that occurred later.

c — Patient-level (group) leakage

If a patient's three visits are scattered across train and test by a purely random row-level split, the model can partially memorize patient-specific traits from that patient's other visits, then get an unfair advantage predicting their test-set visit. The "unseen" test row isn't really independent of the training data.

Fix: a group-based split (e.g. scikit-learn's GroupShuffleSplit or GroupKFold, grouped by patient_id) so all of a given patient's visits land entirely in train or entirely in test. Combined with part b, the most rigorous approach is a grouped, time-based split.

Two Models, One Winner?

MSE · MAE · R² · Adjusted R² · outlier sensitivity

At Zipline Express, the ops team has trained two models to predict delivery time from a real dataset of 200 deliveries. Model A uses 5 features (distance, packages, traffic index, time-of-day, weather). Model B uses 12 — Model A's five plus seven more, including driver experience and restaurant prep time.

MetricModel A (5 features)Model B (12 features)
R²0.8200.840
MSE (min²)45.040.0
MAE (min)5.24.9

The ops lead's read: "Model B wins on every metric, ship Model B." The data scientist on the team is hesitant.

  1. aCompute the Adjusted R² for both models by hand (n = 200 for both; k = 5 for Model A, k = 12 for Model B).
  2. bUsing your answer to (a), why is the data scientist hesitant even though Model B's raw R² is higher? What general property of R² does this illustrate?
  3. cIn plain English, what do an MSE of 40 min² and an MAE of 4.9 min actually mean about real-world prediction error? Why can't 45 min² be compared directly to 5.2 min?
  4. dThree deliveries in the test set turn out to be extreme outliers. Which metric would be more affected, and why? What does that imply about choosing MSE vs. MAE as a primary evaluation metric?

a — Adjusted R² by hand

\[ R^2_{adj} = 1 - (1-R^2)\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-k-1} \]

Model A (n=200, k=5): \(1 - (1-0.82)\cdot\frac{199}{194} = 1 - 0.18\times1.0258 = \boxed{0.8154}\)

Model B (n=200, k=12): \(1 - (1-0.84)\cdot\frac{199}{187} = 1 - 0.16\times1.0642 = \boxed{0.8297}\)

Raw R² vs. Adjusted R²

Raw R² Adjusted R²

Model B still leads on Adjusted R², but its lead over Model A shrinks once each model is penalized for its feature count — the more features, the harder the penalty.

b — Why the hesitation

Plain R² always increases, or at worst stays the same, when features are added — even pure-noise ones — because OLS can always use extra degrees of freedom to explain a little more in-sample variance. The 0.82 → 0.84 jump doesn't by itself prove the seven extra features are genuinely useful.

Adjusted R² penalizes for the number of predictors. Model A's R² drops only slightly (0.820 → 0.815), while Model B's drops more (0.840 → 0.830) — more than twice as hard, matching its larger feature count. Model B's adjusted R² is still higher, so the added complexity appears to earn its keep here — but by a smaller margin than the raw numbers suggested. This is why a data scientist reaches for adjusted R² (and ideally per-feature significance or cross-validation) rather than treating "more features, higher raw R²" as proof of a better model.

c — Explaining the numbers plainly

MAE = 4.9 minutes is the most directly interpretable figure: on average, a prediction is off by about 4.9 minutes, in either direction. MSE = 40 min² isn't directly interpretable in minutes, because each error is squared before averaging — its units are "minutes squared." Taking the square root gives RMSE = √40 ≈ 6.3 minutes, notably higher than the MAE of 4.9 — a gap that is itself informative (see part d).

d — MSE vs. MAE under outliers

MSE is far more sensitive. Squaring the error before averaging means a delivery off by 120 minutes contributes \(120^2=14{,}400\) to the sum, dwarfing dozens of "normal" deliveries off by a few minutes each. MAE counts that same error as simply 120, proportional to how much it actually happened — which is exactly why RMSE (6.3 min) sat noticeably above MAE (4.9 min).

Business callIf typical accuracy matters most and rare extreme delays are acceptable noise, MAE is the more appropriate primary metric. If occasional very-late deliveries are especially costly — refunds, churn — MSE/RMSE's outlier sensitivity becomes a feature, not a bug, pushing the model to avoid large misses. There's no universally correct choice; it depends on which kind of error the business cares about more.

Reading the Coefficients

Interpretability · standardized coefficients · multicollinearity

The CMO at BrightCart, a D2C skincare brand, has a monthly dataset of ad spend (₹ thousands) across TV, Instagram, and Google Search, and the resulting sales (thousand units). A Linear Regression on the raw, unscaled data gives:

\[ \widehat{sales} = 4.2 + 0.045\cdot TV + 0.19\cdot Instagram + 0.003\cdot GoogleSearch \]

The CMO's read: "Instagram's coefficient (0.19) is way bigger than TV's (0.045) — it's clearly our most powerful channel. Let's shift the whole budget there." A colleague re-trains the same model after standardizing every feature (z-score) first:

\[ \widehat{sales} = \overline{sales} + 28.4\cdot TV_{scaled} + 11.7\cdot Instagram_{scaled} + 0.6\cdot GoogleSearch_{scaled} \]

  1. aWhat does the coefficient 0.19 for Instagram mean in the first (unscaled) model — precise about units and the "holding other variables constant" interpretation?
  2. bIs "bigger raw coefficient = more powerful channel" valid? What's wrong with comparing 0.045 and 0.19 directly, given what you know about TV and Instagram's spend ranges?
  3. cIn the standardized model, TV's coefficient (28.4) is now much larger than Instagram's (11.7) — the ranking flips. Why does standardizing do this, and what does a standardized coefficient like 28.4 actually mean?
  4. dEven standardized coefficients can mislead if the channels are correlated with each other. Name the phenomenon, and one way to check for it.

a — Interpreting the raw coefficient

Holding TV and Google Search spend fixed, a ₹1,000 increase in Instagram spend is associated with an average increase of 0.19 thousand units — 190 units — in sales. This is a correlational, model-internal reading: it doesn't by itself prove that spending ₹1,000 more on Instagram causes 190 more units to sell, but it's what the number represents within this linear model.

b — Why raw coefficients aren't comparable

Unscaled coefficients are entangled with each feature's typical scale. If TV spend typically runs in the ₹100,000s while Instagram runs in the ₹10,000s, a "1 unit" (₹1,000) change represents a much smaller relative shift for TV's budget than for Instagram's. A small coefficient on a large-scale variable can correspond to a bigger overall effect than a large coefficient on a small-scale one — you can't tell from the raw numbers alone. The CMO is comparing a ₹-per-thousand effect on a huge budget line to the same effect on a small one.

c — Why standardizing flips the ranking

Z-scoring converts each feature into "standard deviations from its own mean," putting every channel on the same scale — one standardized unit now means "one typical amount of variation," regardless of ₹ units. A standardized coefficient of 28.4 for TV means a one-standard-deviation increase in TV spend, holding the other channels fixed, is associated with a 28.4-thousand-unit increase in sales.

The flip happens because TV likely varies less month to month (smaller standard deviation — steady campaigns) than Instagram, whose spend swings more. A ₹1,000 change in TV "does less" in raw terms, but a typical change in TV (one full standard deviation, potentially a large ₹ amount on a big steady budget) moves sales far more than a typical Instagram change does. Standardized coefficients better reflect how much a channel matters given how it actually varies in practice.

d — The multicollinearity caveat

If TV and Instagram spend move together — campaigns often launch on both at once — the model struggles to cleanly attribute the sales lift to one channel over the other. Standardized coefficients can become unstable, inflated, or even flip sign with small data changes, because credit is being split between two correlated predictors. This is multicollinearity, explored in depth under The Suspiciously Confident Model. A simple check: the pairwise correlation matrix between channels; more rigorously, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each feature. A VIF well above ~5–10 is a red flag that a coefficient shouldn't be trusted as a clean importance measure.

One Step at a Time

Weight-update rule · learning rate · convergence vs. divergence

A student, Ananya, tests her from-scratch gradient descent on a toy dataset she knows the exact answer to ahead of time — every point lies perfectly on \(y = 2x+1\):

xy
13
25
37

She initializes \(w=0,\ b=0\), with learning rate \(\alpha = 0.1\).

  1. aCompute \(\partial J/\partial w\) and \(\partial J/\partial b\) at the very first step (\(w=0, b=0\)).
  2. bUsing those gradients, compute the updated \(w_1\) and \(b_1\) after one iteration.
  3. cAfter many iterations, \(w\) and \(b\) do converge toward 2 and 1. What would happen instead with \(\alpha = 10\)? With \(\alpha = 0.0000001\)? Reason through the mechanism of both failure modes.

a — The gradients at the first step

With \(w=0,b=0\), every prediction is \(\hat y_i = 0\), so the errors are \(e_i = \hat y_i - y_i = -y_i\): \(e_1=-3,\ e_2=-5,\ e_3=-7\).

\[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{m}\sum e_i x_i = \frac{1}{3}\big[(-3)(1)+(-5)(2)+(-7)(3)\big] = \frac{-34}{3} \approx -11.33 \]

\[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{m}\sum e_i = \frac{1}{3}\big[-3-5-7\big] = -5 \]

b — The first update

\[ w_1 = 0 - 0.1\times(-11.33) = \boxed{1.133} \qquad b_1 = 0 - 0.1\times(-5) = \boxed{0.5} \]

After a single step, \((w,b)\) has already moved from \((0,0)\) toward the true answer \((2,1)\) — in the right direction, though many more iterations are needed to close the gap.

c — Learning rate too large vs. too small

Three learning rates, three outcomes

\(\alpha=10\), too large: \(J(w,b)\) is a convex bowl, and gradient descent is meant to step down its slope. Step size is proportional to \(\alpha\), so a gradient of roughly \(-11\) produces a step of about \(10\times11=110\) — wildly overshooting the minimum and landing on the opposite side of the bowl, at a higher cost than before, with an even steeper gradient. Each step overshoots by more than the last: \(w\) and \(b\) oscillate with ever-increasing magnitude. This is divergence — in practice, the cost increases every iteration, often blowing up to inf/NaN within a handful of steps.

\(\alpha=0.0000001\), too small: the opposite problem — each step is minuscule (roughly \(0.0000001\times11.33\approx0.0000011\) per iteration for \(w\)). The algorithm moves in the correct direction every time and is mathematically guaranteed to converge eventually, but would need an impractically large number of iterations. With a fixed iteration budget, this looks like the cost barely decreasing at all — easy to mistake for a stalled or broken model when the learning rate is simply too conservative.

The learning rate trades off the risk of overshoot (too large) against unacceptably slow convergence (too small) — there's no universally correct value; it typically needs tuning for a given problem and its feature scales, which is exactly why feature scaling (see Scaling & Gradient Descent) matters so much for making one learning rate work well across all features at once.

Why Scaling Matters for Gradient Descent

Contour geometry · zig-zagging · standardization

A student is training a from-scratch Linear Regression to predict house prices from area_sqft (roughly 500–5000) and age_years (roughly 0–50), using gradient descent with a single learning rate applied to both weights. Whatever single \(\alpha\) he tries, something goes wrong: large enough for age_years to converge reasonably, and area_sqft explodes and diverges. Small enough to keep area_sqft stable, and age_years barely moves after thousands of iterations.

  1. aIn terms of the cost function's contour plot, why does pairing a large-scale feature with a small-scale one in unscaled gradient descent create this exact problem?
  2. bConcretely, why does a single learning rate, applied identically to both weight updates, end up simultaneously too big for one weight and too small for the other?
  3. cWrite the standardization formula the student should apply, and explain precisely why it fixes the problem. State one rule about which mean and standard deviation to use when scaling test data, and why.

The cost surface, before and after scaling

a — The elongated bowl

\(J(w_{area}, w_{age})\) is a convex quadratic bowl in 2D weight-space, whose equal-cost contour lines are ellipses. Because area_sqft values are numerically much larger than age_years values, a small change in \(w_{area}\) moves the prediction — and the cost — much more than the same-sized change in \(w_{age}\). The cost surface is steep in the \(w_{area}\) direction and comparatively flat in the \(w_{age}\) direction, producing elongated, cigar-shaped contours instead of circular ones.

Gradient descent's update direction is perpendicular to the local contour line, not necessarily pointed at the center of an elongated ellipse — so instead of a straight line to the minimum, the path zig-zags across the ellipse's narrow axis, making slow net progress along its long axis. That's exactly what the student is seeing.

b — One learning rate, two conflicting requirements

The two weights have gradients of very different natural magnitude because of the scale mismatch: steep for \(w_{area}\), shallow for \(w_{age}\). Since both updates use the same \(\alpha\), no single value is simultaneously small enough to avoid overshooting the steep direction and large enough to make progress along the shallow one — a mechanical consequence of the elongated bowl in part a.

c — The fix: standardization

\[ x_{scaled} = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \]

with \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) computed on the training set. After this, both features sit on the same scale (mean 0, standard deviation 1), so a one-unit change in either represents a "typical" amount of variation for that feature. The contours become far more circular, the gradient direction points much more directly at the minimum, and a single well-chosen learning rate works reasonably well for both weights — convergence becomes dramatically faster and more stable.

Train-only statisticsThe \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) used to scale must be computed only from the training set, then that same \(\mu,\sigma\) — not freshly recomputed values — applied to the test set and any new incoming data. Recomputing on the test set (or train+test combined) leaks information about the test distribution into the modeling process, and risks an inconsistent transformation: a model trained on one "scale" of numbers must always see inputs on that same scale to produce meaningful predictions.

The Suspiciously Confident Model

F-statistic vs. t-statistics · multicollinearity · VIF

An HR analytics team predicts monthly_salary from several features, including — by an unfortunate oversight — both experience_years and experience_months (≈ years × 12, computed from the same join date). The statsmodels OLS summary:

coefstd errtP>|t|
const18500.0850.021.760.000
experience_years5200.03100.01.680.095

R² 0.812 · Adj. R² 0.806 · F-statistic 138.4 · Prob(F) 2.1e-42

A teammate: "The overall model looks great — high R², tiny F p-value — but experience_years and experience_months both come back insignificant, and months even has a negative coefficient. Something's broken."

  1. aWhat do the F-statistic and its p-value tell you, versus what the individual t-statistics tell you? Why can the overall model be highly significant while individual coefficients aren't?
  2. bDiagnose the specific problem. What's it called, and why does it produce exactly these symptoms?
  3. cThe VIF for experience_years comes back as 45. Using \(VIF_j = 1/(1-R_j^2)\), what must \(R_j^2\) be, and what does that number mean?
  4. dWhat's the standard rule-of-thumb VIF threshold, and what should the team actually do to fix the model?

a — F-statistic vs. individual t-statistics

The F-statistic tests a joint hypothesis: are all coefficients simultaneously zero — does the model, as a whole, explain significantly more variance than an intercept-only model? A tiny F p-value (2.1e-42) says yes, overwhelmingly, something in this set of predictors is genuinely related to salary.

Each t-statistic tests a narrower question: holding all other predictors fixed, is this one coefficient different from zero? A model can jointly explain a lot of variance while no single predictor's isolated contribution can be pinned down with confidence — exactly what happens when predictors are too tangled up with each other for OLS to cleanly assign credit to any one of them.

b — Diagnosis: multicollinearity

experience_years and experience_months are near-perfectly linearly related — a textbook case of multicollinearity, two predictors carrying almost the same information. When predictors are highly correlated, OLS struggles to determine how much of the outcome to attribute to one versus the other; the \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) matrix entries blow up for these two columns, inflating their standard errors (visible here — experience_years's std err of 3100 is more than half its own coefficient). Large standard errors mechanically produce small t-statistics (\(t=\text{coef}/\text{std err}\)) and therefore large, insignificant p-values, even though the combined effect of "years of experience" is real and drives the model's high R².

The nonsensical negative sign on experience_months is a classic multicollinearity artifact: with two near-duplicate columns, OLS can arbitrarily assign an inflated positive coefficient to one and a compensating negative to the other, as long as their combined net effect on predictions stays reasonable. Individually the coefficients are unstable; only their combined contribution is meaningful.

c — Computing R²ⱼ from VIF = 45

\[ VIF_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}=45 \ \Rightarrow\ 1-R_j^2=\frac{1}{45}\approx0.0222 \ \Rightarrow\ R_j^2\approx \boxed{0.978} \]

97.8% of the variation in experience_years can itself be explained just from the other predictors — overwhelmingly, from experience_months — so it carries almost no independent information once experience_months is already known, which is exactly why the model can't give either a stable, individually significant coefficient.

d — Threshold and fix

A common rule of thumb: VIF > 10 signals problematic multicollinearity (some practitioners use a stricter cutoff of 5); VIF = 45 is far beyond it — severe multicollinearity.

Recommendation: the cleanest fix is dropping one of the two redundant columns — keep experience_years, remove experience_months, since they encode essentially the same information. More generally, options for correlated features include dropping the redundant one, combining them into a single feature, or using a regularized technique like Ridge regression, which handles correlated predictors more gracefully than plain OLS — though for a case this clear-cut, removing the duplicate is the right first step.

The Widening Cone

Heteroskedasticity · Durbin-Watson · autocorrelation

Case A. A utility company predicts monthly_electricity_bill from household size, home area, and AC usage. Plotting residuals against fitted values: for small predicted bills (₹500–1000) residuals cluster tightly near zero (±100); for large predicted bills (₹8000–10,000) they scatter as far as ±3000 — a cone opening left to right.

Case B. A separate team predicts daily stock trading volume from lagged price features. A standard diagnostic check returns a Durbin-Watson statistic of 0.65.

  1. aWhich assumption is violated in Case A? Why is the cone/megaphone shape the tell-tale sign, as opposed to some other violation?
  2. bOLS coefficients stay unbiased even under this violation. So what, precisely, becomes unreliable? Name at least two concrete downstream consequences.
  3. cSuggest two practical remedies for Case A.
  4. dDW ranges 0–4; near 2 means no autocorrelation, notably below 2 means positive autocorrelation. Interpret DW = 0.65 for Case B. Why is this unsurprising for time-ordered financial data? Name one remedy.

Residual spread: constant vs. widening

a — Case A: heteroskedasticity

This violates the homoskedasticity assumption — that residuals have constant variance across all fitted values. Here the spread is clearly not constant: small for low predicted bills, large for high ones, growing systematically with the fitted value. This "cone opening outward" shape is the classic diagnostic signature of heteroskedasticity — likely because households with larger predicted bills (bigger homes, heavier AC use) also carry more variable unmodeled factors (appliance count, occupancy, tariff slabs) that scale up with bill size. It's distinct from a curved, U-shaped residual pattern, which signals a linearity violation instead, or residuals correlated across sequential rows, which signals autocorrelation, as in Case B.

b — What actually breaks

OLS coefficient point estimates stay unbiased on average — they aren't systematically wrong. What breaks is everything depending on the coefficients' standard errors, since the standard OLS formula assumes constant error variance.

  1. Standard errors become incorrect (typically underestimated), so reported confidence intervals are too narrow — a false sense of precision.
  2. Hypothesis tests become unreliable — with wrong standard errors, t-statistics and p-values are miscalculated, so a predictor might look significant when it isn't, or vice versa.
  3. Prediction intervals become poorly calibrated — true confidence is understated for low-bill households and overstated for high-bill ones, since the model assumes uniform uncertainty everywhere.

c — Remedies for Case A

Transform the target — predict \(\log(\text{monthly\_electricity\_bill})\) instead of the raw bill. Log transforms often stabilize variance because they compress large values proportionally more than small ones, directly counteracting a "spread grows with the value" pattern.

Use heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (HC-robust, available in statsmodels), which recompute valid standard errors and p-values without assuming constant variance — this doesn't fix the underlying non-constant spread, but it does fix the unreliable inference from part b. Weighted Least Squares, down-weighting high-variance observations, is another acceptable option.

d — Case B: interpreting DW = 0.65

0.65 sits far below the "no autocorrelation" benchmark of 2, indicating strong positive autocorrelation: an unusually high residual on one day tends to be followed by another unusually high one the next day, rather than errors being independent across time steps.

This is unsurprising for time-ordered financial data, because trading volume has momentum and clustering effects — a day of unusually high volume, driven by news or volatility, tends to be followed by more high-volume days, not because the model's lagged-price features capture this, but because the un-modeled dynamics behind volume (sentiment, ongoing news, volatility clustering) persist across consecutive days. A plain Linear Regression using only lagged price levels isn't built to capture this temporal dependency, so it leaks into the residuals as autocorrelation.

Remedy: incorporate the time-dependent structure directly — add lagged values of the target itself (an autoregressive term) — or move to models built for time series (ARIMA), or use Newey-West/HAC standard errors, which correct the inference for autocorrelation the same way robust standard errors do for heteroskedasticity, without necessarily fixing the model's underlying specification.

09

The Curve Nobody Modeled

Linearity assumption · residual diagnostics

A physiotherapy chain builds a Linear Regression to predict recovery_time_days for knee-surgery patients from a single feature, patient_age. The model fits reasonably well overall (R² = 0.61), but plotting residuals against patient_age reveals a pattern: positive residuals (under-prediction) for very young patients, negative (over-prediction) for middle-aged patients, and positive again for elderly patients — a smooth U-shaped curve.

  1. aWhich of the five Linear Regression assumptions is violated? How does a U-shaped residual-vs-feature plot reveal this, as opposed to a random scatter?
  2. bPropose two different concrete modeling fixes, and briefly explain each mechanism.
  3. cSuppose a different model's residual-vs-fitted plot instead showed a perfectly random, structureless cloud, evenly spread above and below zero with roughly constant spread. What would you conclude, and which two assumptions does this reassure you about?

Residual pattern: structured vs. structureless

a — The violated assumption: linearity

This violates the linearity assumption — that the true relationship between predictor(s) and target is approximately a straight line. If the true relationship between age and recovery time is genuinely curved — both very young patients (more active or riskier rehab behavior) and elderly patients (typically slower overall healing) taking longer than middle-aged patients, forming a U- or J-shape — forcing a single straight line through that curve will systematically overshoot in the middle (predicting recovery times too long for middle-aged patients, hence negative residuals there) and undershoot at both ends (too-short predictions for the very young and very old, hence positive residuals at both extremes).

That's precisely why a U-shaped, systematic residual pattern signals a linearity violation: the model's errors still contain a predictable, structured relationship with the feature that a straight line failed to capture. A linearity-satisfying model would instead show no systematic pattern — just random scatter around zero, because a straight line was the right shape and any remaining error is unpredictable noise.

b — Two concrete fixes

  1. Add a polynomial (quadratic) term: include patient_age² as an extra feature, fitting \(\hat y = \beta_0+\beta_1\cdot age+\beta_2\cdot age^2\). This is still technically linear regression — linear in the parameters \(\beta\), even though the relationship with the raw feature is now curved — and can directly capture a U-shaped or inverted-U relationship, since a quadratic curve is exactly the right shape for one "bend."
  2. Transform the feature or move to a non-linear model: bucket patient_age into bands (child / young-adult / middle-aged / senior) and one-hot encode them, letting the model learn a separate effect per group rather than one continuous slope; or use a model class that naturally captures non-linearity — decision trees, or a spline/GAM-based regression — if the U-shape turns out more complex than a clean quadratic.

c — Interpreting a clean, structureless plot

A random, evenly scattered residual cloud with no visible pattern and roughly constant spread is exactly what you want to see, and it reassures you about two of the five assumptions at once:

  1. Linearity — no systematic curve or trend in the residuals means the straight-line form was appropriate; there's no leftover structure the model failed to capture.
  2. Homoskedasticity — spread staying roughly the same width across the full range of fitted values, rather than fanning into a cone as in The Widening Cone, means the constant-variance assumption is reasonably satisfied.

This particular plot doesn't, by itself, say anything about the other three assumptions — no multicollinearity, normally-distributed errors, or no autocorrelation — since those need different diagnostics: VIF values, a Q-Q plot or residual histogram, and a time-ordered residual plot or Durbin-Watson statistic, respectively.

The throughline

Every diagnostic covered here — Adjusted R², VIF, residual plots, Durbin-Watson — exists for the same reason. A model can look deceptively good on a single headline metric like R² while quietly violating an assumption that makes its coefficients or its inference untrustworthy. A high R² is a starting point for investigation, never the end of it.

  • Before your next model, look for one plausible leakage risk in your own dataset, in the spirit of the Wheelio and hospital-readmission cases.
  • And one plausible multicollinearity risk, in the spirit of the HR salary model — two near-duplicate columns are easy to miss until VIF surfaces them.
