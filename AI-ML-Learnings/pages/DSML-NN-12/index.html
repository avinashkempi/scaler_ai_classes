Model Interpretability: LIME and SHAP

A neural network can reach 97% accuracy and still be a model nobody is allowed to use, if nobody can say why it made a particular decision.

THE PROBLEM

Why Trust a Model You Cannot Explain?

Most people, asked whether they'd let a model deny someone a loan or diagnose a disease without being able to explain why, say no. Regulators agree: in healthcare, finance, and hiring, an unexplainable decision is often an unusable one. Two tools have become the standard answer to this problem — LIME and SHAP — and this read builds both from the ground up.

The Business Case

Imagine working as a data scientist at a hospital. You've built a neural network that predicts whether a patient has diabetes. The model is accurate — but it is also a black box with no obvious way to interrogate a single decision.

The model says "Patient X has diabetes" with 92% confidence. The doctor asks "why?" Saying "the model is 92% confident," or describing its architecture, doesn't answer the question. Neither tells the doctor which of this patient's values pushed the prediction toward diabetes.

That gap — between what a model can report about itself and what a human actually needs to know — is exactly what LIME and SHAP exist to close.

PART ONE

LIME: Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations

LIME is a technique for explaining individual predictions of black-box models. Rather than trying to describe everything a complex model does, it approximates the model's behavior in a small neighborhood around one prediction — close enough that a simple, interpretable model can stand in for it there.

Its name is also a map of the three ideas that define it:

Local

It works on one prediction at a time, not the model's behavior everywhere.

Interpretable

It can be trusted to explain that one prediction faithfully, using a model simple enough for a human to read.

Model-agnostic

It doesn't care what kind of model it is explaining — neural net, random forest, or anything else.

Local vs. Global Analysis

A global explanation describes how a model behaves across its entire input space — nearly impossible to state simply for a network with millions of parameters. A local explanation, scoped to one patient or one image, is a smaller and more honest promise. That's the promise LIME makes.

Scope: One instance — a single patient, a single image.
Who wants it: A doctor treating one patient, asking why the model flagged this case.
Why it's tractable: Even a wildly complex decision boundary can look simple when you zoom into one small region of it.

Scope: The model's behavior across every instance it might see.
Who wants it: A hospital administrator deciding whether to deploy the model at all.
Why it's hard: Summarizing millions of parameters into one simple global story is, in practice, nearly impossible — which is the question LIME deliberately leaves unanswered.

Locality of Interest

If a model's decision boundary were a straight line everywhere, the region around any one point would look just like the rest of the boundary — zoomed in or zoomed out. LIME's idea only pays off when the boundary is complex overall but can be approximated by something simple, like a line, in the small neighborhood around the point being explained. That's exactly the kind of boundary a neural network tends to have.

The same boundary, two scales: globally intricate, locally almost straight.

Model Agnosticism

LIME never looks inside a model's weights — only at its inputs and outputs. That trade-off cuts both ways:

Advantage

The same LIME code explains a neural network, a random forest, or an XGBoost model with no change to the explainer itself. One tool, any model type.

Disadvantage

Because it only watches inputs and outputs, LIME has to call the model many times per explanation — and it can only be as faithful as its perturbation strategy, since it never sees the model's actual reasoning.

Reading the Explanation

Plotting the result highlights which image regions push the prediction toward the correct class (positive regions) and which push it away (negative regions). Red regions mark the model's vulnerable points — add noise there, and the model is likely to misclassify the image.

OBSERVATION

Running the same test image through every one of the ten class panels shows something useful: for some classes — like the image's actual label — there's almost no red region, meaning the model predicts that class correctly and confidently. For others, a large red region shows the prediction is one small perturbation away from flipping.

Is LIME Stable?

LIME perturbs an image randomly and fits a local model on those random samples — which raises an uncomfortable question: run the exact same explanation on the exact same image twice, and will it highlight exactly the same region both times? Testing this directly, by re-running the explanation several times with a small sample count and watching how much the top region's importance score moves, shows a real answer: it swings noticeably.

50
Conceptual illustration — more perturbation samples, narrower run-to-run swing in the top region's weight. High variance

This instability isn't a bug — it's an honest property of an estimate built from random samples. More samples make the estimate more stable. The practical lesson: never trust a LIME explanation generated with a tiny sample count, and for any decision that matters, run the explanation more than once and look for agreement before trusting a single run.

Advantages and Disadvantages of LIME

Advantages

Model-agnostic — one explainer works across architectures. Produces intuitive, locally faithful explanations that non-experts can read. Supports tabular, text, and image data.

Disadvantages

Needs hundreds or thousands of model calls per explanation, so it's slow — a poor fit for real-time, high-volume use. Explanations can be unstable at low sample counts, since they come from random perturbation.

A NATURAL QUESTION

Why Another Interpretability Tool?

LIME approximates a local decision boundary with a simple model and random sampling — fast, but, as just shown, sometimes unstable. SHAP comes from a different direction entirely: it treats every feature as a player in a cooperative game and distributes the prediction "fairly" among them, using a formula with mathematical guarantees LIME doesn't have.

THE FOUNDATION

Shapley Values: Fair Credit in a Cooperative Game

Shapley values come from cooperative game theory, introduced by Lloyd Shapley in 1953, as a way to measure each player's fair contribution to a group's total payoff. Applied to a model, each feature becomes a player, the model's prediction function is the game, and the prediction itself is the profit being divided.

To find one feature's Shapley value, you compute its marginal contribution — the change in profit when that feature joins a given subset of the other features — for every possible subset it could join, then average all of those marginal contributions.

Try It: Marginal Contribution

Four friends — A, B, C, D — work together toward a shared profit. To split it fairly, each friend's contribution is measured by comparing the group's profit with and without them. Below, friend A considers joining the coalition of B, C, and D. Toggle B, C, and D to change which coalition A joins, and watch the profit figures from the source example play out.

With 3 other features, A has 2³ = 8 possible coalitions to join. Its Shapley value is the plain average of all eight marginal contributions.

WHY THIS DOESN'T SCALE

With n features, every feature needs 2n−1 marginal contributions — exact Shapley values become computationally impossible for anything beyond a few dozen features. This problem is known to be NP-hard. SHAP exists to approximate these values efficiently, without paying that exponential cost.

PART TWO

SHAP: SHapley Additive exPlanations

SHAP applies Shapley values to machine learning predictions, offering a unified way to attribute each feature's contribution — one that accounts for interactions and dependencies between features, which simple coefficients or tree-based feature importances cannot capture.

Unlike linear-model coefficients, which are sensitive to variable scale and miss local importance, SHAP values measure each feature's impact when combined with every possible subset of the other features — the same fairness guarantee Shapley brought to the friends-splitting-profit example.

The Additivity Guarantee

SHAP values for one prediction are guaranteed to add up exactly to the gap between the model's output for that instance and the average output over a baseline. This property — sometimes called local accuracy — means SHAP values aren't just a relative ranking of features; they're an actual, complete decomposition of the prediction. LIME's weights carry no equivalent guarantee.

SHAP in Practice: Explaining a CNN

To see SHAP applied to images, a small convolutional network is trained on the same Fashion MNIST task, then explained pixel-by-pixel.

▸ Generate SHAP values for an image› 
masker = shap.maskers.Image("blur(4,4)", X_train[0].shape)
explainer = shap.Explainer(predict, masker, output_names=class_names)

shap_values = explainer(
    X_test[1:3], max_evals=200, batch_size=50,
    outputs=shap.Explanation.argsort.flip[:4])

shap.image_plot(shap_values.values, shap_values.data, shap_values.output_names)
READING THE OUTPUTEach row is one test image; each column is one of the model's top four predicted classes. Red pixels push the prediction toward that class, blue pixels push it away. Unlike LIME's colored segments, SHAP assigns a signed number to every single pixel — every input, down to the pixel, gets its exact fair share of credit or blame.

The SHAP Algorithm, Step by Step

  1. Choose a reference point as a baseline for feature contribution.
  2. Explore all feature permutations for a specific instance.
  3. Evaluate each feature's contribution by comparing predictions with and without it.
  4. Apply the Shapley value formula to calculate feature importance.
  5. Interpret positive and negative values as positive and negative contributions to the prediction.
PUTTING THEM SIDE BY SIDE

LIME vs. SHAP

Both tools answer the same underlying question — why did the model say this? — but they get there differently, with different costs.

LIMESHAP
ApproachPerturb inputs, fit a simple local modelDistribute the prediction via Shapley values
SpeedFaster, fewer guaranteesSlower — approximating exact Shapley values is expensive
GuaranteesNone — purely an approximationAdditivity: attributions sum exactly to the prediction gap
StabilityCan swing run-to-run at low sample countsGrounded in a fixed mathematical formula
Best fitQuick, one-off, exploratory explanationsExplanations that must survive an audit
A regulator wants explanations that are consistent and mathematically justified — pointing toward SHAP's additivity and its grounding in Shapley values. The trade-off is compute cost: exact or near-exact SHAP values are more expensive than a LIME run, which matters if a model needs to be explained at high volume in real time. Naming the additivity guarantee specifically, connecting it to what an auditor actually wants, and acknowledging the compute trade-off is what separates a strong answer from "SHAP, because it's newer."
CHECK YOURSELF

Quick Checks

A hospital wants to know which single input most often drives the model's diabetes predictions across all ten thousand patients in its database. Which kind of explanation answers this?
CORRECTThe hospital's question concerns behavior across the whole population, not any one patient — that's a global question. Local explanations and locality-of-interest plots are, by definition, scoped to one instance; model-agnostic describes how an explainer works, not its scope.
A team needs explanations for a fraud-detection model within 50 milliseconds per transaction, in production, at high volume. Is LIME a good fit?
CORRECTEvery LIME explanation needs hundreds or thousands of perturbed samples passed through the model — far longer than 50ms even for a fast model. Being model-agnostic is true but irrelevant to latency, and LIME also supports tabular and text data, not just images.
For a 3-player game with players A, B and C: the coalition {B, C} alone generates a profit of 40. Adding A raises it to 55. What is A's marginal contribution for this coalition?
CORRECTMarginal contribution = profit with the player − profit without: 55 − 40 = 15. A's full Shapley value would average this together with its marginal contribution across every other coalition it could join.
SHAP values for one prediction always sum exactly to (model output) − (average baseline output). Why does this matter in practice?
CORRECTThis additivity (local accuracy) means SHAP values aren't just a relative ranking — summing them exactly reconstructs the gap between the prediction and the baseline. LIME carries no such guarantee, and additivity says nothing about compute time or the need for the original model.
BRINGING IT TOGETHER

Key Takeaways

  • ◆LIME answers "why this prediction?" quickly, for one instance, by perturbing the input and fitting a simple local model — but its answer can wobble unless enough samples are used.
  • ◆SHAP answers the same question more slowly, but with a mathematical guarantee that every feature's credit adds up exactly to the gap between the prediction and the baseline — the stronger choice when an explanation has to survive an audit.
  • ◆Neither tool opens the model up and reveals its "true" reasoning. Both are approximations, built differently, with different guarantees and different costs. Choosing between them is itself a modeling decision.

Back to the hospital: a doctor asks why the model says a patient has diabetes. LIME can answer quickly, for that one patient, with a caveat about sample count. SHAP can answer more slowly, with a guarantee that the credit it assigns adds up exactly — which is what makes it the stronger choice when the explanation itself has to hold up under scrutiny.
