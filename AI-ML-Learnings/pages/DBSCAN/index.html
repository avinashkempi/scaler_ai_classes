The story so far

Where GMM left off

Back at Veloura, the previous session fit a Gaussian Mixture Model on customer behavior data — spend, order frequency, recency, and related features — and came away with soft, probabilistic segments: a real upgrade over hierarchical clustering's rigid, hard boundaries. Expectation–Maximization got there by alternating between two steps:

  • Expectation — computing each customer's likelihood under every Gaussian component.
  • Maximization — updating each Gaussian's mean and variance as a likelihood-weighted average of customers.

But there was a crack in the story — two of them, in fact, both real and both still unsolved.

Problem 1

The forced-fit problem

A handful of Veloura customers — near-zero spend but a huge order count, classic reseller or bulk-buyer behavior — got confidently assigned to some segment anyway. A GMM always picks the Gaussian with the highest likelihood, even when that likelihood is low for every single one. It has no concept of "doesn't belong anywhere."

Problem 2

The shape problem

A GMM assumes every segment is elliptical. Some of Veloura's real behavior clusters — think clickstream or browsing-pattern segments — are elongated or crescent-shaped, and a GMM misfits them for the same reason KMeans did back in the first session.

What just happened?

Both examples expose the same underlying weakness in two different ways. In the Veloura customer data, the unusual reseller-shaped customers are clearly far away from every normal segment — yet the GMM still assigns each one to a cluster, because it has no "none of the above" option. Every point must belong somewhere, even if the fit is terrible.

In the crescent-shaped browsing data, the problem isn't unusual customers — it's the shape of the clusters themselves. The two moon-shaped groups are visually obvious to a person, but a GMM can only draw smooth, elliptical regions, so it slices through a group that is naturally connected.

How can we naturally identify customers that don't belong to any cluster, instead of forcing an assignment?

How can we discover clusters that aren't circular or elliptical at all?

Both questions point to the same realization: maybe we shouldn't start by assuming what a cluster looks like.

Instead of asking — "Which Gaussian generated this point?"
What if we asked: "Is this point surrounded by lots of nearby neighbors, or is it sitting alone?"

That simple change in perspective leads to an entirely different way of thinking about clustering — one that looks only at how crowded each point's neighborhood is, instead of modeling probability distributions at all.

Section 1

Thinking in density

A GMM's whole question was: which distribution best explains this point? DBSCAN throws that question out and asks something much simpler:

Is this point sitting in a crowded neighborhood, or an empty one?

That's it. No Gaussians, no distribution-shape assumption at all — just: look around this point, and count who's nearby.

Name the algorithm in full: DBSCAN = Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise.

That last word — Noise — is the whole point. DBSCAN is the one algorithm in the toolkit explicitly built to output "noise" as a real label, instead of forcing every point into a cluster, however bad the fit.

Section 2

eps, MinPts, and three kinds of points

The outlier customers a GMM couldn't explain are the running example for this whole section — building up, piece by piece, exactly the vocabulary DBSCAN needs to catch them.

ε

eps (ε) — the neighborhood radius

For any customer, eps is basically: how far should I look around this customer to see who's nearby? Think of it as an invisible circle drawn around every point. A small eps only considers very close customers; a large eps considers customers farther away.

"eps is the size of the area you look around in — 1 meter in a crowded room might show you 10 people, 5 meters might show you 50."

A reasonable eps is tied to the distance to a point's k-th nearest neighbor — in other words, asking: how far do I need to look before I find a normal-sized local neighborhood? (Formalized in Section 4.)

n

MinPts — the density threshold

MinPts answers a different question: how many points need to be inside my neighborhood for this area to count as crowded?

"eps is how far you look. MinPts is how many people you expect to find."

For example: eps = 2 km means look within 2 km around the customer; MinPts = 5 means at least 5 points must be in that neighborhood for it to be considered dense. Together, the two knobs define what DBSCAN considers a dense region.

Core, border, and noise points

With those two knobs, every point in the dataset falls into exactly one of three buckets. This is exactly what makes DBSCAN useful for the outlier customers: instead of forcing an assignment, it can say "this customer isn't part of any dense region — mark it as noise." That label is what a GMM couldn't naturally give Veloura's forced-fit customers.

Core point

Its eps-neighborhood contains ≥ MinPts points.

Read more ⌄
A point sitting comfortably inside a crowded region — dense enough, by itself, to start or extend a cluster.

Border point

Not dense enough alone, but inside a core point's neighborhood.

Read more ⌄
Relevant by association, not by its own density — it rides along with whichever cluster its core-point neighbor belongs to.

Noise point

Neither core nor border.

Read more ⌄
Genuinely isolated from every dense region — the label a GMM simply doesn't have.

Walking the expansion process — exactly what the full algorithm does in the next section:

  1. Pick an unvisited customer.
  2. Count neighbors within eps. Is it a core point (≥ MinPts)?
  3. If yes — absorb every point in its neighborhood into the cluster (they become border points, or core points themselves if they also clear MinPts), and keep expanding outward through connected core points.
  4. If no, and it isn't inside any core point's neighborhood either — label it noise.
  5. Move to the next unvisited point. Repeat until everyone is classified.
Think it through — a point has only 3 neighbors within eps, and MinPts = 5. Core, border, or noise?
What do you need to know before deciding? It can't be core on its own — 3 is fewer than MinPts = 5. Whether it's border or noise depends entirely on one more fact: is it inside the eps-neighborhood of some other point that is core? If yes, it's a border point. If not, it's noise.
Knowledge check · 2 of 5

A point has only 2 neighbors within its eps radius (MinPts = 5), but one of those neighbors is a core point. What is this point classified as?

WhyIt fails the core-point density test on its own, but because a core point's neighborhood includes it, it's "reachable" from a cluster and classified as a border point rather than noise.
AIWhy does DBSCAN need two separate parameters instead of just one density threshold?

eps controls spatial proximity — which points count as neighbors at all — while MinPts controls density — how many of those neighbors are required before a region counts as crowded. A single threshold can't disentangle the two: a sparse region with points close together and a dense region with points spread slightly farther apart could look identical to one combined number. Keeping them separate lets DBSCAN recognize clusters of different densities and shapes without confusing "close" with "crowded."

Section 3

The algorithm, step by step

Putting the density intuition and the eps / MinPts vocabulary together into one clean loop:

  1. Pick an unvisited point.
  2. Test it. If it's a core point (≥ MinPts neighbors within eps), start a new cluster and expand: pull in every point density-reachable from it, directly or through a chain of other core points.
  3. Classify absorbed points. Any point pulled in this way that isn't itself core becomes a border point of that cluster.
  4. Otherwise, label it noise — if the starting point isn't core, and isn't reachable from any core point.
  5. Repeat until every point has a label: a cluster ID, or noise.

Two points are density-connected when they're linked through a chain of core points — not necessarily direct neighbors of each other. That chaining is exactly what lets DBSCAN trace out long, curved, irregular shapes, instead of only ever drawing neat blobs.

from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
from matplotlib.patches import Circle

np.random.seed(42)
X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=180, centers=[[0, 0], [5, 5], [0, 5]],
                   cluster_std=0.6, random_state=42)
noise = np.random.uniform(low=-3, high=8, size=(20, 2))
X = np.vstack([X, noise])

eps = 0.8
min_pts = 5

nbrs = NearestNeighbors(radius=eps).fit(X)
neighbor_counts = np.array([len(idx) for idx in nbrs.radius_neighbors(X, return_distance=False)])
is_core = neighbor_counts >= min_pts

db = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=min_pts).fit(X)
labels = db.labels_

Section 4

Tuning eps and MinPts

Neither knob is guesswork — both follow principled rules.

MinPts rule of thumb

≥ d + 1, and in practice often 2 × d, where d is the number of features. Why scale it with dimensionality? In higher dimensions, points naturally spread out more — the curse of dimensionality — so a fixed, small MinPts would call almost any region "dense enough." Scaling MinPts with d keeps the density test meaningful as Veloura adds more behavioral features.

eps tuning — the k-distance elbow method

  1. Compute k-distance. For every point, compute the distance to its k-th nearest neighbor (set k = MinPts).
  2. Sort and plot. Sort those distances and plot them.
  3. Find the elbow. Where distances suddenly start climbing is a natural eps choice.

Why does this work? It identifies exactly the point where customers transition from densely packed to sparse — which is precisely what eps is supposed to capture.

▸ View the code — the k-distance elbow plot 
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

k = 5  # will match MinPts once it's set on the real dataset
nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=k).fit(warmup)
distances, _ = nn.kneighbors(warmup)
k_distances = np.sort(distances[:, -1])

plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 4.5))
plt.plot(k_distances, marker="o", markersize=3)
plt.xlabel("Points, sorted by k-distance")
plt.ylabel(f"Distance to {k}th nearest neighbor")
plt.title("k-distance elbow plot -- the 'knee' is a natural eps")
plt.show()

Each point on this graph is the distance from one customer to its 5th nearest neighbor (k = 5), sorted from smallest to largest. For most customers, that distance grows slowly and smoothly — they lie in naturally dense regions. Near the end of the curve, the distances jump sharply: those points are much farther from their 5th nearest neighbor, meaning they sit in sparse regions or are potential outliers. Points before the knee are likely to become part of clusters; points after it are more likely to be treated as noise. That gives a data-driven starting point for choosing eps instead of picking it arbitrarily.

Did DBSCAN find the anomalies?

Yes — and without ever being told where they were. DBSCAN correctly identified all 12 planted reseller-shaped customers as noise, demonstrating one of its biggest advantages over algorithms like K-Means and GMM: points that don't belong to any dense region don't have to be forced into a cluster.

It also flagged 6 additional customers as noise. These weren't planted anomalies — they're simply customers whose behavior is unusually isolated compared to the rest of the dataset. Whether they represent genuinely unusual customers, data errors, or interesting edge cases would require further business investigation. This points to an important distinction: DBSCAN detects points in low-density regions — not "fraud" directly. Every flagged point is a candidate anomaly; interpreting why it's unusual still requires domain knowledge.

Section 6

Strengths, limits, and choosing the right algorithm

✓ Advantages

  • Noise-resistant — explicitly labels outliers instead of forcing them in
  • Handles arbitrary cluster shapes
  • No need to pre-specify k

✕ Limitations

  • Sensitive to the eps / MinPts choice
  • Struggles with genuinely varying density — a dense VIP segment and a sparse casual-browser segment can trip up a single global eps

That varying-density blind spot is DBSCAN's own honest caveat — the same way a GMM was upfront about its elliptical-shape assumption.

Veloura wants to flag potentially fraudulent transactions in real time, where fraud patterns are rare, irregular in shape, and shouldn't be forced into a "normal" segment. Which algorithm from this two-part series fits best?

WhyThis is precisely DBSCAN's design goal: no forced assignment, native noise / outlier labeling, and no assumption of elliptical or globular cluster shape.
AIIf Veloura's VIP segment is much denser than its casual-browser segment, why is a single global eps risky, and what would you try instead?

A single global eps assumes one neighborhood size describes "dense" everywhere. When the VIP segment is naturally tight and the casual-browser segment is naturally spread out, an eps tuned for one will misclassify the other — either merging the sparse segment into noise, or swallowing both segments into one cluster. A few directions worth exploring instead:

  • Density-aware algorithms such as OPTICS or HDBSCAN, built specifically to handle varying density within one dataset.
  • A two-stage approach — cluster the dense and sparse populations separately, then combine the results.
  • Feature transforms (log-scaling, normalization) that reduce the gap in density between segments before running DBSCAN.

Wrap-up

The complete toolkit

Recap

  • Density, not distribution shape or distance-to-centroid, is DBSCAN's core idea.
  • eps + MinPts define core, border, and noise points.
  • The algorithm expands clusters from core points and explicitly labels true outliers as noise.
  • Tuning follows principled rules — the k-distance elbow for eps, the d-based rule for MinPts — not guesswork.
  • DBSCAN closes both gaps GMM left open: it can say "this doesn't belong anywhere," and it isn't limited to elliptical clusters.

With DBSCAN in place, Veloura now has segment-level insight for Marketing from the GMM, and an outlier / fraud-flagging layer for Ops from DBSCAN — plus the complete four-algorithm toolkit (K-Means, Hierarchical, GMM, DBSCAN) to choose from depending on which business question comes in next.
