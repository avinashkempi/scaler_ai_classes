Decoding the story hidden in sales numbers

Aisha Verma just joined Vastra Loop's demand planning team. Her manager hands her eighteen years of monthly sales and one question: how much will we sell, and when? Before she can forecast anything, she has to learn to read the signal underneath the noise — this is that process.

01 — The Brief

Meet Vastra Loop, and the forecasting problem

It's Aisha's first Monday at Vastra Loop, an Indian fashion e-commerce startup headquartered in Bengaluru. The company recently launched QuickStyle, a rapid-delivery promise: orders tagged QuickStyle reach customers within 24–48 hours, much like the express-delivery bets real fashion e-commerce players have made in recent years.

Aisha has been hired as a Demand Planning Analyst. On day one, her manager Rohan walks up with a laptop.

"Before we plan next quarter's inventory, I need one thing from you: how much will we sell, and when? Here's our monthly sales history — go figure it out."

Rohan, to Aisha, day one

This is Aisha's problem. It's ours too, for the rest of this read.

Why does this forecast matter?

Aisha asks Rohan why the number matters so much. Knowing what future sales will look like helps Vastra Loop plan production and inventory better — there's no upside to over- or under-producing. During peak seasons the company may want to run deals and promotions, and hiring, purchasing, discounting, marketing, and maintenance decisions all depend on a realistic sense of upcoming demand.

Rohan sums it up simply: "A good forecast isn't about being cautious. It's about being close to the truth."

Over-forecast

Predicting too much demand

  • Clothing piles up unsold in the warehouse.
  • Inventory holding cost increases.
  • Roughly 10% of unsold stock eventually gets scrapped — fashion trends move fast, and old stock goes out of style.
Under-forecast

Predicting too little demand

  • A lot of demand goes unmet.
  • Revenue takes a direct hit.
  • Customers who don't find what they want may not come back — a loss of trust, not just a lost sale.
02 — Foundations

What counts as time series data?

Rohan's file contains historical monthly sales figures for Vastra Loop. Before Aisha can forecast anything, she needs to understand what kind of data this is — and it turns out to be a classic example of time series data.

Definition

A signal, indexed by an ordered timestamp, is a time series. To qualify, you need at minimum two things: a date/timestamp (t), and one measurable quantity at each timestamp (y).

Analogy

Think of a time series like a diary. Each page (timestamp) has exactly one entry (the value) written on it, and the pages are always read in order — you'd never skip ahead and read March before February. That ordering is what makes it a time series rather than just a list of numbers.

For Vastra Loop, t is measured in months. A proper datetime type for the date column matters more than it seems — pandas' date-aware tools (slicing by month, resampling by year, and so on) only work smoothly once a column is genuinely typed as datetime, rather than stored as plain text. A CSV would need an explicit conversion; Aisha's file, loaded from Excel, already had it right.

Eighteen years of monthly data should give 18 × 12 + 1 = 217 rows (the extra one is the single January 2019 entry) — and that's exactly what the DATE column shows. No rows silently vanished. Had the count been off, it would've meant entire months were missing outright — for example, if 2010 jumped straight from February to May, nothing in the raw numbers would warn her about that gap on its own.

One more small but useful move: swap the default 0,1,2,3… row index for the dates themselves. Since every date is unique, and a datetime column is numeric under the hood, it's a perfectly valid index — and it's what unlocks pandas' date-aware slicing and makes plotting far more readable.

Quiz 1

If a time series has only 3% missing values, is it fine to simply drop those rows?

Explanation: Time series data is sequential — each row is tied to a specific point in time, and most techniques expect an unbroken chain of timestamps. Dropping rows creates a gap in the timeline itself, not just a missing value, which most time series methods can't handle gracefully. The gap needs to be filled intelligently, not removed.

Aisha's plan is systematic: find where values are missing, check whether there's a pattern to the missingness, then fill the gaps with the most appropriate value. Nothing obvious jumps out in the pattern — the missing months are scattered, not clustered around one season or year. Had there been a pattern, she'd have needed to investigate further, since a data pipeline that consistently fails every December, say, calls for a different fix than random gaps do.

Look at any stretch where sales were genuinely running low or high: the imputed value snaps to the 18-year mean regardless, creating an unnatural jump right where data is missing.

✗ Not realistic here

Vastra Loop's monthly sales wouldn't randomly crash to zero for a whole month, so the sharp drops this creates aren't realistic. Zero can be the right fill elsewhere — daily sales for a physical store might legitimately be zero on days it's shut — but that always depends on what the business actually does on that day, and needs confirming with the business team rather than assumed.

Original (with gaps)Interpolated
✓ Blends in naturally

Instead of averaging the whole 18-year history, interpolation averages just the point right before and right after each gap — a local average. If it was 20°C at 2pm and 24°C at 4pm, your best guess for 3pm isn't the year's average temperature, it's simply the midpoint: 22°C. Under the hood, this joins the point before and after each gap with a straight line, and the result blends in far more naturally than either fill above.

Quiz 2

Why is filling missing values with the overall mean not a great idea for time series?

Explanation: Time series values usually depend on where in time you are — early-year sales look different from late-year sales. A single global mean ignores this local context, causing unnatural jumps right around the imputed points.

04 — Cleaning, Part II

Challenge 2: anomalies

With the gaps filled, Aisha turns to the second issue she spotted: unusual spikes and dips.

Definition

An anomaly (or outlier) is an abnormal or unusual data point that stands out from the rest of the series. It could be a genuine data-entry error, or a real but one-off event — a flash sale, a system glitch — that isn't expected to repeat, and so shouldn't influence future forecasts.

Analogy

Anomalies are like a single very loud word shouted in an otherwise quiet conversation — it doesn't fit the flow, and it draws attention precisely because it's out of place.

Replacing these points with NaN, the way missing values were handled, isn't ideal either — once a value becomes NaN, the model has no information at all for that date, and most forecasting methods rely on having a value at every time step. An empty slot is still a problem, just a different one.

Spotting outliers with a histogram

One of the best ways to spot anomalies is a histogram — it shows how often each value range occurs, so outliers stand out as isolated bars far from the main cluster.

Distribution of monthly sales
Most months cluster in the middle; a handful sit far outside it.
Reconstructed series, calibrated to Aisha's reported statistics

Eyeballing a cutoff here works — say, replace everything below 5,000 with 5,000 and everything above 20,000 with 20,000 — but only because the gap between the main cluster and the outliers happens to be visible. If the histogram had looked smoothly continuous instead, eyeballing wouldn't work, and Aisha would need a more principled, repeatable rule.

A systematic rule: quantiles

A common rule of thumb treats anything above the 95th percentile or below the 5th percentile as a candidate anomaly.

Analogy

This is like a school deciding that the top 5% and bottom 5% of exam scores in a batch get manually re-checked by a teacher — not necessarily because they're wrong, but because they're unusual enough to deserve a second look.

₹15,164.60
95th percentile
₹7,307.80
5th percentile

Pandas' clip(upper=…, lower=…) caps values at these two thresholds: anything higher gets pulled down to the upper bound, anything lower gets pushed up to the lower one. At 95th/5th, the outliers vanish — but looking closely at the very start and end of the series, some genuinely useful high and low values get clipped away too. Loosening the thresholds to the 98th/2nd percentile fixes the over-aggressive clipping. One rule of thumb: keep the two percentiles symmetric unless there's a specific business reason not to — clipping at 98/10, say, would be lopsided without justification.

05 — Finding The Signal

Moving averages & weighted moving averages

With the data clean, Aisha reports back to Rohan. He nods: "Good. Now let's actually understand what's driving these numbers — is the business growing? Are there patterns we can rely on?" Looking at the cleaned plot, two things are visible at a glance: sales appear to be growing year over year, and certain peaks and dips seem to recur. The next question is whether Aisha can separate the "steady growth" part from the "repeating pattern" part — and for that she needs a new tool.

The idea

What if Aisha simply averaged the last two data points to guess the next one? It's a reasonable starting idea — it avoids a flat, boring forecast, and reacts, a little, to recent data. Expanding on this: what if she averaged the last m observations instead of just two?

ŷt = ( yt−1 + yt−2 + … + yt−m ) / m
Analogy

Think of a cricket commentator predicting how a batter will perform today. Instead of looking at just their very last innings — too noisy, one bad day doesn't define a player — they look at the batter's average score over the last 5 innings. That average becomes a far more stable prediction than any single match. A moving average does exactly this: it smooths out one-off noise by averaging over a recent window.

Weighted Moving Average (WMA)

So far every past observation gets equal weight. What if more recent months should matter more than months from years ago? That's the idea behind a Weighted Moving Average: recent data gets a bigger say, older data a smaller one, by multiplying each observation by its own weighting factor.

Analogy

Think about how a teacher calculates your "current form" grade — your most recent test should probably count for more than one from three months ago, since you've likely learned and changed since then. Because of this weighting, a WMA tends to follow the actual series more closely and react faster to real changes than a plain, equally-weighted moving average.

In code, this is pandas' rolling() method: pick a window size k, and pandas slides that window across the data, letting you run an operation — typically .mean() — on each window as it moves.

Try it yourself — window size and centering

Play with the window slider and watch the smoothed line change live. A small window barely smooths anything; a very large window flattens the series into something close to a pure trend line.

A few things to notice as you drag the slider. The smoothed series lags the original when backward-looking — peaks and dips shift along the x-axis by roughly the window size, since a sudden change only shows up a few months later, adjusting gradually rather than over-reacting. The smoothed series is also less spiky — short-term noise disappears. And there are missing values wherever the window runs out of history: with a backward-looking window of size 12, the first 12 points have nothing to average over.

Centered moving average

Instead of only looking backward, a centered average looks n points before and n points after the current point. This can't be used for actual forecasting — future values don't exist yet — but if the goal is simply smoothing for analysis or preprocessing, like Aisha is doing right now, it works fine, since all the data already exists. With center=True and a window of 12, six values go missing at the very start and six at the very end, rather than twelve piling up only at the start.

Quiz 3

True or false: linear interpolation can be thought of as a special case of a moving average.

Explanation: Linear interpolation fills a gap using the average of the single point before and single point after it — a centered moving average with an effective window of just one point on each side. Wider centered moving averages simply generalize the same idea.
06 — Components

Trend

Definition

Trend is the linear increasing or decreasing behaviour of a series over a long stretch of time. When a series is described as "growing" or "shrinking" overall, that's its trend. A trend can be an uptrend, a downtrend, or a mix of both over different periods — it doesn't have to be a single straight line — and it isn't permanent: a trend usually holds for a while and then fades or reverses, unlike seasonality, which repeats on a fixed cycle.

Method 1 — centered moving average

Apply .mean() on .rolling() with a window wide enough to smooth out both noise and seasonality. For monthly data, 12 is a natural first choice, though a 1- or 2-year window both work — it just needs to be wide enough to average away noise and seasonality. This is essentially smoothing the original series, and while it's a common choice to represent trend, it is a somewhat subjective one.

Method 2 — linear regression

The other approach: fit a straight line through the points.

Analogy

Imagine plotting a child's height every year on a wall chart. The exact growth from year to year isn't perfectly uniform, but a single straight line through those points captures the overall "growing taller" story — exactly what a regression-based trend line does for sales.

ŷt+1 = m·t + c

With time t as the independent variable, evenly spaced from 0 to m, that's the whole trend line.

07 — Components

Seasonality

Definition

Seasonality is a pattern that repeats at a regular, known interval — essentially a repeating wave. Stack enough copies of that wave together, and you start to reconstruct the series. Seasonality always has a fixed, known period, and our whole ability to forecast a series at all relies on the fact that some of its behaviour repeats predictably.

Analogy

Think of a beach town's ice-cream sales — every summer, sales spike, and every winter, they dip, regardless of which year it is. That predictable, calendar-tied wave is seasonality.

Quiz 4

Which of these is not a seasonal signal?

Explanation: Births are roughly uniformly distributed across the year with no strong repeating calendar pattern, so this isn't seasonal. Car sales in India tend to spike around festivals like Diwali, temperature clearly follows a yearly cycle, and movie ticket sales often rise around holidays — all genuinely seasonal.

Calculating seasonality

  1. Calculate the trend and subtract it from the original series. What's left over is mostly seasonality plus noise.
  2. From that leftover signal, average across each matching period — in four years of monthly data, average all four Januaries together, all four Februaries together, and so on.

Aisha could compute this by hand — but there's an easier way, which brings us to decomposition.

08 — Putting It Together

Time series decomposition & residuals

Rohan asks Aisha for one clean summary: "Show me trend, seasonality, and whatever's left over — separately." A complex signal can generally be broken into simpler components, and we've already met two of them: trend and seasonality. Breaking a series into these pieces explicitly is called decomposition.

Analogy

Think of decomposition like separating a music track into its vocals, instruments, and background hiss. Each layer, added back together, reconstructs the original song — and each layer is easier to reason about on its own than the mixed-down track.

In an ideal world, trend plus seasonality would reconstruct the original series exactly. Real business data rarely is ideal, though — there's always some leftover noise or error that neither component can explain, called the residual.

y(t) = b(t) + s(t) + e(t)
where b = trend, s = seasonality, e = residual
e(t) = y(t) − b(t) − s(t)

The residual isn't estimated the way trend and seasonality are — it's simply whatever is left over once the estimated trend and seasonality are subtracted from the real data. If both were estimated well, the residual should look small and scattered roughly evenly around zero, like static noise with no obvious pattern of its own.

In Python, this is statsmodels' seasonal_decompose, which needs the target series and a choice of model — additive, what we've discussed, or multiplicative.

Quiz 5

Aisha finds that her residual's mean is very close to zero and the values look roughly symmetric around zero. What does this suggest about her trend + seasonality estimate?

Explanation: A residual centered near zero with no obvious remaining pattern is exactly what we'd expect once trend and seasonality have absorbed the explainable structure in the series. It doesn't tell us anything about anomalies — that was a separate, earlier cleaning step — or suggest the seasonal period is wrong; in fact, a well-behaved residual is a good sign the seasonal period was chosen sensibly.
