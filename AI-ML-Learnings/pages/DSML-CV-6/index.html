    A complete medical-imaging pipeline: classify chest X-rays as normal or pneumonia with a model small enough for a phone. We handle imbalanced data and patient-level splits, learn how MobileNet saves computation, fine-tune MobileNetV2, choose a safe decision threshold, look inside the model with Grad-CAM, and meet EfficientNet and Vision Transformers.

    01

    The problem and its constraints

    Pneumonia sends well over a million people to hospital every year in the US alone, and it is a leading cause of serious illness worldwide. The chest X-ray is the most common imaging test used to diagnose it. Suppose you are a data scientist at Qure.ai, a medical AI company. Your task is to classify whether a chest X-ray shows pneumonia or not, and the model must also run on a mobile device so that predictions are fast and work in real time.

    Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the air sacs in one or both lungs. The air sacs may fill with fluid or pus, causing cough with phlegm, fever, chills and difficulty breathing. Bacteria, viruses and fungi can all cause it. It ranges from mild to life-threatening, and is most serious for infants and young children, people over 65, and people with other health problems or weakened immune systems.

    Teaching exercise only. The model built here is not a medical device and must not be used to make clinical decisions. Real diagnostic tools need clinical validation, regulatory approval, and a clinician in the loop.

    Four constraints that shape every choice

    Speed

    Low latency

    Predictions should arrive quickly, on the device.

    Errors

    Mistakes have different costs

    False negative: the patient has pneumonia and the model says normal; they may go untreated, usually the more dangerous error. False positive: a normal patient is flagged; this causes extra tests and worry.

    Trust

    Trustworthy confidence

    A score of 0.9 should really mean high confidence.

    Insight

    Explainability

    A clinician should be able to see why the model decided what it did.

    How we will solve it

    Earlier lectures covered large networks such as VGG16 and ResNet: accurate but heavy, which makes them awkward on a phone. Here we introduce MobileNet, which has far fewer parameters (MobileNetV2 has roughly 7 times fewer than ResNet-50 and about 40 times fewer than VGG16) while keeping good ImageNet accuracy. It is widely used in mobile apps for image classification and object detection, where low latency matters.

    02

    The data and its pitfalls

    We use the chest X-ray dataset from Kermany et al., Cell, 2018. It contains chest X-rays of children aged one to five from a single hospital in Guangzhou, China, with labels from expert physicians. It is split into a training set (5,232 images) and a test set (624 images). Keep the source in mind: a model trained on it may not work on adults or on scans from other hospitals.

    Loading and caching

    The data is stored in .tfrec files, a plain binary container: each record is an 8-byte length, a 4-byte checksum, the payload bytes, and another 4-byte checksum. The payloads are raw JPEG images and their file paths, so a few lines of Python can read them with no special library. Every image is decoded once to a small grayscale array and cached; the class label is the name of the parent folder in the path. One grayscale channel is stored, and expanded to three only when it is fed to the network.

    Can we use augmentation?
    
  
    Be careful. Some augmentations create images that never occur in real scans: a horizontal flip puts the heart on the wrong side of the chest, because X-rays are always taken in a standard orientation. Mild changes such as small rotations, slight zoom, and brightness or contrast shifts are common in medical imaging and can help. The lecture skips augmentation to keep the pipeline simple.
    
  
    

  
    Split by patient, not by image
    
  
    The same patient can have several X-rays, and filenames carry a patient ID (for example person12_bacteria_34.jpeg for pneumonia scans and IM-0115-0001.jpeg for normal ones). If images of one patient land in both training and validation, the validation score looks better than it really is. GroupShuffleSplit keeps all images that share a group label on the same side.

  03
  
    MobileNet and depthwise separable convolutions
    
  
    State-of-the-art models such as ResNet-50, Inception, VGG16 and AlexNet reach strong accuracy on ImageNet but are expensive to train and to run. A phone needs something lighter that is still reliable. MobileNet, introduced by Google in 2017 (paper), is built for that.
    
  
    
  
    Figures are for the pretrained torchvision weights on the ImageNet-1k validation set. The first MobileNet (V1) has about 4.2M parameters, as reported in its paper. Models like this are meant for on-device inference: after training in PyTorch they can be exported to a runtime such as ExecuTorch or ONNX Runtime Mobile.
    

  
    The core idea
    
  
    MobileNetV1 has about 28 layers (counting each depthwise and pointwise layer separately), yet only about 4.2M parameters, around 6 times fewer than ResNet-50. The saving comes from how it does convolutions. Recall that in a standard convolution each filter spans all input channels, so one filter produces one output channel by mixing every input channel together.
    
  
    
  
    MobileNet uses a standard convolution only once, in the first layer. Every later layer is a depthwise separable convolution: a depthwise convolution followed by a pointwise convolution.
    
  
    
    
    Depthwise: filter
    Apply one filter to each input channel, separately: split the input into channels, convolve each with its own filter, stack the results. A 3-channel input gives a 3-channel output.
    
    
    Pointwise: combine
    A 1 × 1 convolution. At each position it takes a weighted sum across all channels; many such kernels give any number of output channels.
    
  
    
  
    
  
    More figures: depthwise detail and the combination
    
  
    A standard convolution filters and combines in one step. The separable version splits this into two cheaper steps: one layer for filtering, one for combining.
    

  
    How much does it save?
    
  
    For a kernel of size k × k, M input channels and N output channels, a standard convolution needs k·k·M·N weights; the separable version needs k·k·M (depthwise) plus M·N (pointwise). The ratio of the two is 1/N + 1/k², which for 3 × 3 kernels is a saving of roughly 8 to 9 times.
    
  
    
  
    
  
    In PyTorch the depthwise layer is an ordinary Conv2d with groups equal to the number of input channels, so each filter sees only its own channel.

    MobileNetV1 architecture and its successors
    
  
    
  
      
    
    1. A standard 3 × 3 convolution with stride 2 (32 filters).
      2. 
    
    2. 13 depthwise separable blocks. Their output channels go 64, 128, 128, 256, 256, 512, then five blocks of 512, then 1024, 1024. Blocks that halve the spatial size use stride 2 in the depthwise layer.
      3. 
    
    3. Global average pooling.
      4. 
    
    4. A fully connected layer with 1,000 outputs (one per ImageNet class).
      5. 
  
    
  
    Every convolution is followed by batch normalisation and a ReLU6 activation. ReLU6 caps activations at 6, which keeps values in a small range and works well with the low-precision arithmetic used on mobile hardware.

    MobileNet versions
    
  
    
    
    2017
    MobileNetV1
    Introduced by Google with depthwise separable convolutions, to run vision models efficiently on low-resource devices.
    
    
    2018
    MobileNetV2
    Adds inverted residuals and linear bottlenecks. A block expands the channels with a 1 × 1 convolution, applies a depthwise 3 × 3 convolution, then projects back down with a 1 × 1 convolution that has no activation (the linear bottleneck). A shortcut joins the narrow ends of the block. Higher accuracy at lower cost.
    
    
    2019
    MobileNetV3
    Found with neural architecture search; adds squeeze-and-excitation blocks and the hard-swish activation.
    
  
    
  
    

    

    


    

    
  05
  
    Fine-tuning MobileNetV2
    
  
    Basic transfer learning freezes the pretrained layers and trains only a few new fully connected layers on top. But ImageNet photos are very different from chest X-rays, so the pretrained features may not fit perfectly. With enough data it helps to let some pretrained layers learn too. This is fine-tuning: unfreeze the layers near the top, which hold the most task-specific features, and train them on the new data. (In everyday speech the two terms are often interchangeable; transfer learning is the general idea, fine-tuning one way of doing it.)
    
  
    
  
    How many layers to unfreeze? More trainable parameters raise the risk of overfitting on about four thousand training images. As a middle path we freeze most of the network and train only the last two blocks plus a new classifier.
    
  
    
  
    features holds 19 convolutional blocks and classifier the final layers (Dropout(0.2) then Linear(1280, 1000)). The old head is replaced by 1280 → 128 → 1 with a ReLU between. The result has 2,387,969 parameters in total, 1,050,177 of them trainable.
    
  
    Code: load, build_model and set_train_mode
    
  
    Batch norm needs care. Batch norm layers keep running averages of the activations they have seen. If we simply call net.train(), the frozen layers keep updating those averages, which quietly changes “frozen” features. set_train_mode keeps the batch norm layers of frozen blocks in inference mode.
    

  
    Training setup
    
  
    
    
    Loss
    Binary cross-entropy, each example weighted by its class weight.
    
    
    Early stopping
    Stop when validation loss has not improved for 3 epochs and restore the best weights. We monitor the same class-weighted loss that we optimise.
    
    
    Checkpoint
    Save the best weights to disk so we need not retrain.
    
    
    Learning rate
    Adam: 1e-4 for the unfrozen pretrained blocks, 1e-3 for the new head, both decayed exponentially (10× smaller every 20 epochs).
    
  
    
  
    Metrics that matter in medicine
    
  
    
    
    Precision
    Share of predicted pneumonia cases that are real.
    
    
    Recall (sensitivity)
    Share of real pneumonia cases we catch.
    
    
    Specificity
    Share of normal cases we correctly call normal.

    Choosing the threshold
    
  
    The model outputs a probability, and 0.5 is a choice, not a rule. In screening, missing a case is worse than a false alarm, so a common approach is to require high recall (say 95%) and then pick the threshold with the best specificity under that requirement. We choose it on the validation set and then apply it to the test set. Choosing it on the test set would be cheating, because the test set would no longer be unseen. (The validation set also picked our best epoch, so it is slightly optimistic, and 95% recall on validation does not guarantee 95% on test.)
    
  
      
    
    1. roc_curve on validation data; keep thresholds with recall ≥ 95% (30 candidates).
      2. 
    
    2. Among those, pick the one with the fewest false alarms: 0.901.

  07
  
    Grad-CAM: where does the model look?
    
  
    When a doctor reads an X-ray, they look at specific regions of the lungs. For a medical model it matters that it bases its decision on the lungs, not on something incidental like a text marker, a scan border, or a medical device. Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) highlights the regions of the image that most influenced a prediction. It generalises the earlier CAM method: it works with any CNN architecture, with no need to change the network.
    
  
    
  
      
    
    1. Run the image through the CNN and keep the feature maps of a chosen convolutional layer, normally the last one.
      2. 
    
    2. Take the score of the class of interest (here, the pneumonia output) and compute its gradient with respect to those feature maps.
      3. 
    
    3. Average each channel’s gradient over all positions: one weight per channel, saying how much that channel matters for the class.
      4. 
    
    4. Compute the weighted sum of the feature maps and apply a ReLU, keeping only features that push the score up.
      5. 
    
    5. Upsample to the image size, rescale to 0 to 1, and overlay on the image as a heat map.
      6. 
  
    
  
    
  
    Which layer? The last convolutional layer is the best choice: it combines high-level meaning with some remaining spatial layout. Earlier layers keep fine spatial detail but respond to simple patterns such as edges, so their maps say little about the class. In MobileNetV2 the last convolutional block is features[-1] (1280 channels at 7 × 7). In code, a forward hook saves the feature maps and asks PyTorch to keep the gradient: both have shape (1, 1280, 7, 7); the channel weights have shape (1, 1280, 1, 1) and the heat map (1, 1, 7, 7).
    
  
    
  
    Code: hooks, channel weights, weighted sum and overlay
    

  
    A reusable function, and four kinds of result
    
  
    Wrapping the steps in grad_cam(net, x, target_layer, sign) lets us reuse them on any image and any layer. The sign argument lets us explain evidence for pneumonia (+1) or for normal (−1), because the model has a single output. The function reproduces the manual result. The figure shows a true positive, a false positive, a false negative and a true negative; the middle column uses the last convolutional layer and the right column an early layer.
    
  
    
  
    The last-layer maps are smooth and coarse (7 × 7 stretched to the image size). The early-layer maps are sharper but follow low-level structure, which is usually not what we want to explain.
    
  
    What a heat map does and does not show. It shows where the model looked, not that the model is right. A model can reach good scores by using shortcuts, such as the hospital’s text marker on the image, and a heat map is one way to catch that.

  08
  
    EfficientNet and Vision Transformers
    
  
    EfficientNet: compound scaling
    
  
    When designing an architecture we can scale in three directions: width (more channels per layer), depth (more layers) and resolution (a larger input). Earlier families mostly scaled one at a time (ResNet mainly scales depth). A brute-force search over all combinations would be far too expensive. EfficientNet (paper) is a family B0 to B7 built around careful scaling.
    
  
    
  
    The three directions are not independent: if the input image is bigger, the network needs more layers to grow its receptive field and more channels to capture finer patterns. Compound scaling uses a single coefficient φ to scale depth, width and resolution together, in fixed proportions found by a small grid search on the smallest model (B0): depth = 1.2^φ, width = 1.1^φ, resolution = 1.15^φ. B0 itself was found by neural architecture search and is built from MBConv blocks (the inverted-residual blocks of MobileNetV2, plus squeeze-and-excitation).
    
  
    
  
    
  
    EfficientNets reach the same accuracy as other ConvNets with many times fewer parameters and FLOPs. For example, EfficientNet-B7 reached 84.3% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet while being 8.4 times smaller and 6.1 times faster at inference than the best ConvNet of the time, and EfficientNet-B3 beat ResNeXt-101 with 18 times fewer FLOPs.
    
  
    More figures: accuracy versus size, and the PyTorch model
    
  
    Each size has a native input resolution (B0: 224, B3: 300, B6: 528). Working at 224 × 224 on a phone-sized budget, we use B0 with the classifier replaced by a single-output head. It has the same features / classifier structure as our MobileNet, so the same training function works. Parameters with a one-output head: EfficientNet-B0 4,008,829 versus MobileNetV2 2,387,969.

    Vision Transformers (optional)
    
  
    Every architecture so far relies on convolutional filters, which need far fewer weights than dense networks to extract information from images. As datasets grow very large, that built-in assumption becomes less necessary. Vaswani et al. proposed the Transformer for language in “Attention Is All You Need” (2017). Its key idea is attention: when producing each output, the model learns which parts of the input to focus on. To translate “ma chemise rouge” into “my red shirt”, it learns to attend to “rouge” when producing “red”. Attention alone does not know word order, so positional information is added to each input: (ma, 1), (chemise, 2), (rouge, 3).
    
  
    The Vision Transformer (ViT) of Dosovitskiy et al. (“An Image is Worth 16x16 Words”, 2020) applies this to images. The equivalent of words are square patches, so the first step is cutting the image into patches. Each patch is flattened and linearly projected into a vector, and a learnable position embedding is added so the model can learn which patches are close to each other.
    
  
    
  
    
  
    The sequence then passes through several Transformer blocks. Each has (1) multi-head self-attention, letting every patch look at all the others to decide which matter, added back through a skip connection, and (2) a small dense network (MLP) applied to each patch, followed by a second skip connection. Layer normalisation is applied before each part. The block output keeps the shape (1, 196, 64).
    
  
    Code: patch embedding and a Transformer block
    
  
    A full ViT also prepends a learnable class token, stacks many blocks and reads the prediction from that token; the lecture’s code leaves this out. ViT needs much more data than a convolutional network, so the authors recommend pretraining on a very large dataset and then fine-tuning. With a small dataset like ours, a ViT trained from scratch is not promising, but applying Transformers to images is an exciting source of new ideas.

    Choosing a model and a transfer-learning strategy
    
  
    
  
    Points to remember before applying transfer learning
    
  
    Two factors matter: the size of your new dataset, and how similar it is to the original (ImageNet-like content and classes, or very different).
    
  
    
  
      
    
    • Class imbalance, patient-level splitting and class weights come before the model: a baseline of “always pneumonia” already scores 62.5%.
      • 
    
    • MobileNet’s depthwise separable convolutions save roughly 8 to 9× the weights of a standard 3 × 3 convolution.
      • 
    
    • Fine-tuning the top blocks of a pretrained MobileNetV2 (keeping frozen batch norm in inference mode) gave a 9.8 MB model with about 21 ms CPU latency.
      • 
    
    • In screening, choose the threshold on validation data to keep recall high; 0.5 is not a rule.
      • 
    
    • Grad-CAM shows where the model looked, not whether it is right.
      • 
    
    • This model is a teaching exercise, not a medical device.
      • 
  
    
  
    Other models to follow: DenseNet, SqueezeNet, NASNet.
