The prototype that begins too late

In Class 2, you looked at the capabilities an FDE may need when a customer problem refuses to stay inside one technical box.

You also worked with a small Meridian Retail prototype. It could take a prepared support case, check a supplied set of rules, and produce a structured draft for a human to review.

That sounds like a sensible starting point.

But look closely at the word prepared.

Before the prototype could do anything, someone had already selected the case. Someone had already found the order. Someone had already supplied the delivery date and issue status. Someone had already decided which policy applied. And someone had already decided that the output would stop with a human reviewer.

The prototype did not make those decisions. It inherited them.

This is true of many demonstrations. The software appears to begin at step one, but the real work began much earlier - with people locating information, resolving ambiguity, choosing boundaries, and deciding what the system was allowed to do.

The solution that worked only in the demo

Imagine a team builds an assistant for handling customer-support cases.

In the demo, every case arrives with the right order ID, the correct delivery date, the latest issue status, and the approved policy. The assistant produces a useful draft in seconds. Leadership is impressed.

Then the team tries to use it in everyday work.

The order information lives in one place. The issue status lives somewhere else. The current policy is difficult to identify. Some cases are missing a required fact. Sometimes the customer asks for something that the official record does not support. Nobody is certain where the final review decision should be recorded.

The assistant has not suddenly become less capable. The environment around it has become visible.

The solution that worked only in the demo

This is where a technically successful prototype can fail. It was designed for a clean input that the real workflow does not reliably produce.

Improving the model may not solve that problem. Adding more features may make it worse. Before connecting anything, an FDE must understand how work actually moves, which information matters, where that information comes from, and who is allowed to make each decision.

The code is only one part of entering a customer environment.

The workflow on paper and the workflow in practice

Most organisations can describe how a process is supposed to work.

A support case arrives. An agent checks the order. The policy is applied. The customer receives a response.

That description may be correct and still leave out the steps that decide whether the process succeeds.

An agent may open several tools to assemble one case. They may search for the latest policy, ask a colleague what to do when a field is blank, re-read the original customer message because its wording matters, or shorten a note because the system will not accept the full explanation.

These actions can look like minor workarounds. They are often where the real requirements are hiding.

If an FDE designs only from the documented process, they may build a solution for a workflow that nobody actually performs. If they observe the work without separating fact from interpretation, they may mistake one unusual case for the whole organisation.

The challenge is to do both: respect the written process, and investigate the lived one carefully enough to see what it leaves out.

One case, several kinds of truth

A customer says an item arrived yesterday. An order record contains a specific delivery date. An issue record says the case is still open. A policy says which facts are required before the case can move forward.

All of these pieces matter, but they do not answer the same question.

The customer's message is the best evidence of what the customer asked. It may not be the approved source for the delivery date or issue status. The policy may describe what should happen, but it does not prove that the required information is present. A generated draft may suggest the next response, but it does not mean a human decision has been made.

One case, several kinds of truth

Strong FDE work keeps these distinctions visible.

When a fact is missing, the gap should remain visible rather than being filled with a plausible guess. When a customer request conflicts with an official record, the disagreement should be surfaced rather than silently resolved. When a system produces a draft, the draft should remain separate from the decision and action that follow.

This is how a prototype begins to earn trust: not by pretending uncertainty has disappeared, but by showing exactly where it remains.

The question at the centre of Class 3

Class 3 moves one layer outward from the prototype.

Instead of asking only whether the output looks useful, we will ask what had to be true before that output could be produced - and what must remain true after it is produced.

That brings us to the central question:

What must an FDE understand before a working prototype can responsibly enter a customer's real workflow?

The answer is not a longer feature list. It begins with a clearer picture of the people, workflow, evidence, sources, permissions, and human decisions surrounding the software.

What sits around the prototype

In Class 3, we will examine several connected parts of the customer environment:

  • People: Who owns the outcome, who performs the work, who controls access, and who would maintain what gets built?
  • Boundary: What single piece of work are we following, and where does it begin and end?
  • Reality: How does the work actually happen, compared with how it is described?
  • Evidence: Which facts are required, and what decision depends on each one?
  • Source: Where should each fact come from, and what happens when it is absent or disputed?
  • Control: What may software read or draft, and what must remain a human decision?
  • Claim: What does the available evidence genuinely allow us to say?

Do not treat these as seven separate phases. In real customer work, they influence one another. A missing owner can block access. An unclear source can make a policy impossible to apply. An undocumented step can explain why a technically sound tool is rejected.

What will Class 3 do?

Class 3 is a working session. By the end of it, you should be able to:

  • identify the people involved in a customer engagement and the decisions each person can make;
  • protect one useful question when several reasonable requests compete for attention;
  • follow one case from the original request through evidence, a proposed response, and human review;
  • distinguish supplied facts from missing information, interpretation, and future decisions;
  • recognise where the documented workflow differs from the work people actually perform;
  • identify the source and owner needed for each decision-relevant fact; and
  • explain honestly what has been tested, what remains unknown, and what should happen next.

You will not be expected to know Meridian's systems or invent a production design. The useful skill is learning to keep facts, assumptions, proposals, and decisions separate while you build a trustworthy picture of the environment.

What to bring to class

Nothing to install. Nothing to submit. Bring three things in your head.

One. Think of a process you know that looks simple when written down but requires several extra steps in practice. Which of those steps are invisible to someone seeing the process for the first time?

Two. Return to the Meridian prototype. It receives a clean case file and an approved policy. If one required fact were missing, what would you want the system to do - and what would you never want it to assume?

Three. Think about a decision from your own work. Which facts does it depend on? Where do those facts come from, and who would you ask if two sources disagreed?

Class 3 takes that map into the customer environment, where the inputs are incomplete, the workflow is partly unwritten, and the most important decisions rarely belong to one person.
