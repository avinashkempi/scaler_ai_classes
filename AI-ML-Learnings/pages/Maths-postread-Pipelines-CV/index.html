Lecture 05

Regularisation, and the plumbing that keeps it honest

Sklearn Pipelines · Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net, ColumnTransformer, Pipeline

The previous lecture ended by observing that models with many large weights tend to overfit. This one acts on that observation. The remedy is one extra term in the cost function — and that single term turns out to solve the multicollinearity problem, the overfitting problem, and the feature-selection problem, depending on which norm you pick.

Step 1 · Making the tug-of-war precise

The notebook's roof-leakage analogy is a good one: you want to minimise leakage and minimise cost, and the two pull against each other. Mathematically there are two ways to write that, and they are equivalent.

Constrained form — minimise error, but you are only allowed a weight budget:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{w}} \;\|\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \le t \]

Penalised form — minimise error plus a price per unit of weight:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{w}} \;\|\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \]

These describe the same family of solutions. For every budget \(t\) there is a price \(\lambda\) that produces the identical answer — \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint. The relationship runs backwards, which is worth saying out loud to learners: a large \(\lambda\) corresponds to a small budget \(t\). Expensive weights mean you buy fewer of them.

The constrained form is the one to draw. The penalised form is the one to code. Same object.

The notebook's two limiting cases now have one-line proofs. At \(\lambda = 0\) the penalty term vanishes and we are back to ordinary least squares, free to overfit. As \(\lambda \to \infty\) any non-zero weight costs infinitely more than it saves, so \(\hat{\mathbf{w}} \to \mathbf{0}\) and the model predicts a constant — maximum underfitting. Everything useful lives between.

One caveat the lecture text glosses

The notebook motivates regularisation by saying the model "tries to make \(\hat{y}\) values larger" by inflating the weights. That is not quite the mechanism — least squares pushes \(\hat{y}\) towards \(y\), in whichever direction that is, and shrinking a coefficient is just as likely as growing it.

The real reason weights blow up is the one from Lecture 4: when features are collinear, \(X^\top X\) is near-singular, and its inverse has enormous entries. The fit then relies on huge coefficients that nearly cancel — recall your year = +4.6e11 and const = −4.6e11. Large weights are a symptom of an ill-conditioned matrix, not of greed. Framing it that way makes the next section land, because the penalty is going to fix the conditioning directly.

Step 2 · Ridge regression, derived

Deriving the Ridge closed form
1

Write the penalised objective in matrix form:

\[ J(\mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w})^{\!\top}(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}) + \lambda\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{w} \]
2

Differentiate. The first part is exactly the gradient from the previous companion; the new part uses \(\partial(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w})/\partial\mathbf{w} = 2\mathbf{w}\):

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J = -2X^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} + 2X^{\!\top}X\mathbf{w} + 2\lambda\mathbf{w} \]
3

Set to zero and factor out \(\mathbf{w}\):

\[ (X^{\!\top}X + \lambda I)\,\mathbf{w} = X^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^{\!\top}X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}\;} \]

One identity matrix, scaled. That is the entire difference from OLS — and it is the difference between a model that falls over on collinear data and one that does not.

4

Why that inverse always exists. Let \(\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_p \ge 0\) be the eigenvalues of \(X^\top X\) (all non-negative, since we proved it is positive semi-definite). Adding \(\lambda I\) shifts every eigenvalue up:

\[ \text{eigenvalues of } (X^{\!\top}X + \lambda I) \;=\; \mu_1 + \lambda,\; \ldots,\; \mu_p + \lambda \]

For any \(\lambda > 0\), every eigenvalue is now strictly positive, so the determinant — their product — is non-zero. Ridge is invertible even when OLS is not. Your year/age pair, which made \(X^\top X\) singular and produced coefficients of size \(10^{11}\), would have been handled by \(\lambda = 0.01\) without dropping a single column.

5

The condition number improves too:

\[ \kappa_{\text{ridge}} = \frac{\mu_{\max} + \lambda}{\mu_{\min} + \lambda} \;<\; \frac{\mu_{\max}}{\mu_{\min}} = \kappa_{\text{OLS}} \]

So gradient descent also converges faster on the regularised problem. Regularisation is a numerical stabiliser as much as a statistical one.

▸ What Ridge does to each direction in feature space (the SVD view)

Write the singular value decomposition \(X = UDV^\top\), where \(D\) holds the singular values \(d_1 \ge d_2 \ge \cdots \ge d_p\). Substituting into both formulas, the fitted values become

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{OLS}} = \sum_{j} \mathbf{u}_j\,\mathbf{u}_j^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_{j} \mathbf{u}_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\,\mathbf{u}_j^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} \]

Every direction is multiplied by a shrinkage factor \(d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)\), which sits between 0 and 1. Directions where the data varies a lot (large \(d_j\)) are barely touched; directions where the data barely varies (small \(d_j\)) are crushed towards zero.

That is exactly the right behaviour. Low-variance directions are where the estimate is least reliable — recall from post-read 1 that \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{w}_j)\) has the spread of \(x_j\) in its denominator. Ridge shrinks hardest precisely where the data has least to say.

Step 3 · Lasso, and the real reason it produces zeros

Swap the squared penalty for an absolute one and you have Lasso:

\[ J_{\text{lasso}}(\mathbf{w}) = \|\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|w_j| \]

There is no closed form — \(|w|\) is not differentiable at zero, so you cannot set a gradient to zero — and sklearn solves it with coordinate descent. But the sparsity property has a clean proof in the simplest case, and that proof is far more convincing than the usual diamond picture.

Why Lasso hits exactly zero and Ridge does not
1

Take one coefficient at a time, with the features uncorrelated and standardised (\(X^\top X = I\)). Let \(z\) be the OLS estimate for that coefficient. The one-dimensional problem is

\[ \min_{w}\; (w - z)^2 + \lambda|w| \]
2

Ridge version first. With \(\lambda w^2\) instead, differentiate and solve: \(2(w-z) + 2\lambda w = 0\), giving

\[ \hat{w}_{\text{ridge}} = \frac{z}{1+\lambda} \]

Multiplicative shrinkage. Dividing a non-zero number by \(1+\lambda\) never produces zero, however large \(\lambda\) gets. Ridge coefficients approach zero asymptotically and never arrive.

3

Lasso version. Away from \(w = 0\) the derivative is \(2(w-z) + \lambda\,\mathrm{sign}(w) = 0\). At \(w = 0\) the absolute value has no derivative but has a subgradient — the whole interval \([-\lambda, \lambda]\). Zero is optimal exactly when \(2z\) lies inside that interval. Solving the three cases:

\[ \hat{w}_{\text{lasso}} = \begin{cases} z - \lambda/2 & z > \lambda/2\\[2pt] 0 & |z| \le \lambda/2\\[2pt] z + \lambda/2 & z < -\lambda/2 \end{cases} \;=\; \mathrm{sign}(z)\bigl(|z| - \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigr)_+ \]

This is the soft-thresholding operator. Subtractive shrinkage, with a floor at zero. Any coefficient whose OLS estimate is smaller than the threshold is set to exactly zero, not approximately.

So the difference is one word: Ridge divides, Lasso subtracts. Division never reaches zero; subtraction with a floor does. That is the whole story, and MCQ 1 in your notebook is asking exactly this.

Step 4 · Elastic Net, and why it exists

Elastic Net charges both prices at once:

\[ J_{\text{EN}}(\mathbf{w}) = \|\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\|^2 + \lambda\left(\alpha\sum_j |w_j| + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum_j w_j^2\right) \]

with \(\alpha = 1\) recovering Lasso and \(\alpha = 0\) recovering Ridge. It is not merely a hedge. Lasso has a specific failure mode: when a group of features is highly correlated, Lasso tends to pick one at random and zero the rest — and which one it picks can flip if you re-sample the data. The L2 term, being strictly convex, spreads weight across correlated features instead. Elastic Net therefore keeps whole correlated groups together while still zeroing genuinely useless features. This is called the grouping effect.

Your CarPrice data is a textbook case: carlength, carwidth, wheelbase and curbweight have correlations of 0.79 to 0.88 with each other, exactly as your heatmap shows. Lasso will keep one of them; Elastic Net will keep the family.

▸ The Bayesian reading — where the two penalties come from

Post-read 1 showed that minimising squared error is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. Regularisation is what you get when you additionally place a prior on the weights and maximise the posterior instead:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{w}} \; \underbrace{\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{w})}_{\text{likelihood} \to \text{squared error}} + \underbrace{\log p(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{prior} \to \text{penalty}} \]
  • A Gaussian prior \(w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\) has log-density \(\propto -w_j^2\) → the L2 penalty → Ridge.
  • A Laplace prior \(p(w_j) \propto e^{-|w_j|/b}\) has log-density \(\propto -|w_j|\) → the L1 penalty → Lasso.

And \(\lambda\) is the ratio of noise variance to prior variance: \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\). A large \(\lambda\) says "I believe the weights are small more strongly than I believe the data." The Laplace distribution has a sharp spike at zero, which is the probabilistic version of the diamond's corner — it genuinely expects many coefficients to be zero.

Step 5 · Two rules the penalty imposes on your preprocessing

Rule one: you must scale, and it is no longer optional

In post-read 1 we established that for plain OLS, scaling changes coefficients but not predictions. Under regularisation that is no longer true. Here is why, in one line.

Suppose you measure a feature in metres and then switch to centimetres: \(x_j' = 100\,x_j\). To keep the same predictions the coefficient must become \(w_j' = w_j/100\). But the penalty charges \(w_j^2\), and

\[ (w_j/100)^2 = w_j^2/10{,}000 \]

The same feature is now penalised ten thousand times less, purely because of the unit you chose. Features measured in small units get large coefficients and are punished hard; features in large units get small coefficients and slip past the penalty free. The model you fit depends on your choice of measurement system, which is indefensible. Standardising puts every feature on a common footing so that \(\lambda\) means the same thing everywhere.

Rule of thumb: any model with a penalty on \(\mathbf{w}\), or any distance-based model, must be scaled. OLS, decision trees, and random forests need not be.

Rule two: never penalise the intercept

If \(w_0\) were penalised, adding a constant to every target — switching from rupees to rupees-above-a-baseline — would change the fitted model. Predictions should not depend on where you put the origin of your \(y\) axis. sklearn handles this for you: Ridge and Lasso centre the data internally and leave intercept_ out of the penalty. Worth stating in class, because the formula on the slide does not show it.

The three scalers in the notebook

That matters enormously for sparse matrices — subtracting a mean from a sparse matrix fills in every zero and can turn a 100 MB matrix into 40 GB. It is the scaler you reach for after one-hot encoding thousands of categories.

Step 6 · Pipelines, formally

A Pipeline is function composition and a ColumnTransformer is a direct sum. Writing it that way makes the difference obvious:

\[ \text{Pipeline:}\quad T(\mathbf{x}) = f_k \circ f_{k-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1(\mathbf{x}) \qquad \text{(sequential — output feeds the next)} \] \[ \text{ColumnTransformer:}\quad T(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl[\,g_1(\mathbf{x}_{C_1}) \;\|\; g_2(\mathbf{x}_{C_2}) \;\|\; \cdots \;\|\; g_m(\mathbf{x}_{C_m})\,\bigr] \qquad \text{(parallel — outputs concatenate)} \]

where the \(C_i\) are disjoint sets of columns and \(\|\) is horizontal concatenation. FeatureUnion is the same concatenation but with each branch seeing all the columns rather than a slice — which is why the notebook's two-people-splitting-the-work story fits it.

The leakage argument, stated properly

This is the part that matters, and it is a real theorem rather than a style preference. A transformer has parameters that it learns from data: StandardScaler learns \((\mu, \sigma)\), MinMaxScaler learns \((x_{\min}, x_{\max})\), an imputer learns a median, OneHotEncoder learns the category list. Call them \(\theta\).

\[ \theta = \text{fit}(X_{\text{train}}), \qquad X'_{\text{train}} = \text{transform}(X_{\text{train}}; \theta), \qquad X'_{\text{test}} = \text{transform}(X_{\text{test}}; \theta) \]

If instead you compute \(\theta\) from the full dataset, then \(\theta\) is a function of the test rows, so \(X'_{\text{train}}\) depends on the test set, so your model has seen information it will not have at prediction time. The test score is then an estimate of nothing in particular. A Pipeline enforces the correct pattern structurally: calling fit on the pipeline fits every step on the training fold only, and predict calls transform — never fit_transform — on new data. That is the substance behind MCQ 5.

Reading your own output: four findings in this notebook

Finding 1 — the ordinal encoder's category list scrambles the order it was meant to preserve

The whole point of ordinal encoding is that the integers carry rank. The notebook supplies:

OE_categories_list = [
    [-2, -1, 3, 2, 1, 0],                                    # symboling
    ['two','three','four','five','six','eight','twelve']   # fine
]

OrdinalEncoder maps each category to its index in the list you give it. So this produces:

symboling−2−10123
encoded as015432

The safest risk rating (0) becomes the largest number and the riskiest (3) becomes a middling one. You can confirm it in the notebook's own output: row 0 has symboling = 3 and the encoder prints 2.0; row 2 has symboling = 1 and prints 4.0. The cylinder list is correctly ordered, which makes the contrast a good teaching moment. The fix is one line:

[-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3]   # monotone — now the integers mean something
Finding 2 — symboling and cylindernumber are encoded twice, creating exact linear dependence

Both columns appear in the OrdinalEncoder list and in the OneHotEncoder list. The output therefore contains both representations of the same variable, and one is an exact linear combination of the other:

\[ \text{ord}_{\text{symboling}} \;=\; \sum_{k} \mathrm{idx}(k)\cdot \text{onehot}_k \]

There is a second dependency as well: OneHotEncoder is called without drop='first', so the six symboling dummies sum to 1 in every row, matching the intercept column. \(X^\top X\) is singular twice over.

To be precise about what that means — and this is worth getting exactly right in class — the redundancy lets you shift the intercept against all the dummies of that variable together. Subtract \(c\) from the intercept and add \(c\) to every one of the six symboling dummies, and predictions are unchanged, because each row contributes exactly one dummy. Shifting the intercept against only one dummy does not work: it changes the prediction for every other category. The coefficients are individually meaningless, but only along that specific direction.

Two consequences for the lesson: pick one encoding per column, and pass drop='first' to the one-hot encoder. Note that Ridge would survive all of this — which is a nice way to demonstrate step 2 of the derivation on live data.

Finding 3 — the target variable is being scaled as if it were a feature

num_cols is built with cars.select_dtypes(include='number'), which returns price — the thing you are predicting — along with car_ID, a row number with no meaning. Both then flow into the MaxAbsScaler branch and out into preprocessed_data.

Two separate problems. Scaling the target is not fatal but it silently changes the units of every coefficient and of MSE, and it must be inverted before anyone reads a predicted price. Keeping car_ID is worse: it is a pure index, and in this dataset it correlates with price at −0.11 because the file happens to be sorted by manufacturer. The model can learn from it, and it will generalise to nothing.

num_cols = cars.select_dtypes(include='number').columns.drop(['price', 'car_ID'])
Finding 4 — the Ridge model is fitted on data scaled before the split

The final modelling section runs X = pd.DataFrame(scale(X)) across all 205 rows, and only then calls train_test_split. So the \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) used on the training rows were computed partly from the test rows — the exact pattern the ColumnTransformer section spent twenty cells teaching learners to avoid.

It is a small leak here (a mean over 205 rows barely moves), but it undermines the lecture's own argument, and the fix is the punchline the notebook is already building towards:

    ('prep', preprocessor),          # the ColumnTransformer from earlier

Two smaller fixes

The instructor note before the encoder cell says "drop='first' attribute of the Standard Scaler" — drop is a OneHotEncoder parameter; StandardScaler has no such argument. And sns.distplot in the EDA cells has been removed from current seaborn; sns.histplot(..., kde=True) is the replacement.

Why this matters outside the classroom

The reason pipelines are non-negotiable in production is not tidiness — it is that the training code and the serving code must apply identical transformations, and the serving code usually runs in a different process, written months later, on one row at a time. A pickled pipeline carries its learned \(\theta\) with it, so the scaler that serves a request is the same object that was fitted during training. Hand-rolled preprocessing is where training/serving skew comes from, and it is one of the most common causes of a model that scored well in a notebook and failed in production.

Lecture 06

Cross-validation: getting a number you can trust

Cross Validation in ML · fold estimators, the LOOCV shortcut, grid search, and the winner's curse

Lecture 5 gave you a knob to turn. This one is about the measuring instrument you use while turning it — and about the fact that if you calibrate the instrument on your test set, you no longer have a test set.

Step 1 · Why one split is not enough, quantified

Your notebook makes the argument by intuition: a 20% slice might happen to be easy or hard. Here is the same argument with numbers attached.

Hold-out R² is computed from \(n_{\text{test}}\) observations, so it is an average of \(n_{\text{test}}\) noisy quantities. Its standard error therefore scales as

\[ \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\text{score}}) \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\text{test}}}} \]

The housing data gives 164 test rows; the car data gives 62. Small test sets produce loud estimates. And you can see exactly this in your own output — the five fold scores from cross_val_score on the housing model:

Fold12345meanspread
R² (cv=5)0.6830.6930.6760.6180.5930.6530.101
R² (shuffled)0.5990.7130.6130.6270.6210.6350.114

Those are the same model on the same data. Depending on which slice you had drawn as your single hold-out, you would have reported anywhere from 0.59 to 0.71 — a range of eleven percentage points of explained variance. Averaging over folds is what turns that into one defensible number, and the spread itself is information: your notebook is right that folds disagreeing wildly is a red flag.

Report the mean and the standard deviation. A model at 0.65 ± 0.04 and one at 0.65 ± 0.20 are not the same model.

Step 2 · The k-fold estimator

Partition the training data into \(k\) disjoint folds \(F_1, \ldots, F_k\) of roughly equal size. For each \(i\), train on everything except \(F_i\) and score on \(F_i\). The cross-validation estimate is the average:

\[ \mathrm{CV}_k \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} L\bigl(\hat{f}^{(-F_i)},\, F_i\bigr) \]

Every observation is used for validation exactly once and for training exactly \(k-1\) times. That is the property the diagram in your notebook is showing.

Choosing k is itself a bias–variance tradeoff

Small k (e.g. 3)Large k (e.g. n = LOOCV)
Training set per fold67% of data(n−1)/n ≈ 100%
Bias of the estimateHigher — models are trained on less data than the final one, so CV is pessimisticAlmost none
Variance of the estimateLowerHigher — the n training sets overlap almost completely, so the n scores are highly correlated and averaging them removes little noise
Cost3 fitsn fits

\(k = 5\) or \(k = 10\) is the standard compromise, and it is what your notebooks use. It is worth telling learners that this is an empirical convention rather than a theorem.

Step 3 · The result that surprises people: LOOCV for free

Leave-one-out sounds prohibitively expensive — \(n\) model fits. For linear regression it costs one. This is a genuinely beautiful piece of algebra and it uses the projection matrix from post-read 1.

Recall the hat matrix, which turns actual values into fitted values:

\[ H = X(X^{\!\top}X)^{-1}X^{\!\top}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{y}} = H\mathbf{y} \]

Its diagonal entries \(h_{ii}\) are called leverages: \(h_{ii}\) measures how much observation \(i\) influences its own fitted value. Then:

The PRESS identity
1

Let \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) be the ordinary residual from the model fitted on all the data. Then the leave-one-out residual — the error the model makes on point \(i\) when point \(i\) was excluded from training — is

\[ \boxed{\;y_i - \hat{y}_i^{(-i)} = \frac{e_i}{1 - h_{ii}}\;} \]
2

So the full leave-one-out cross-validation error is

\[ \mathrm{CV}_{\text{LOO}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{e_i}{1-h_{ii}}\right)^{\!2} \]

No refitting. One fit, one diagonal, done. This quantity is called the PRESS statistic, and it is why RidgeCV can search a hundred alphas almost instantly while LassoCV cannot.

3

The identity also explains why training error is optimistic, in a way the \(p/n\) formula from post-read 1 only hinted at. Since \(\mathrm{tr}(H) = p\), the average leverage is exactly \(p/n\), so on average

\[ \frac{1}{1 - h_{ii}} \approx \frac{1}{1 - p/n} \]

Every training residual is understating the true error by roughly this factor — and the factor blows up as \(p\) approaches \(n\).

Put your own numbers in it

The CarPrice model has \(n = 143\) training rows and \(p = 68\) features. Average leverage is \(68/143 = 0.476\), so

\[ \frac{1}{1-0.476} = 1.91 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{squared errors understated by} \approx 1.91^2 \approx 3.6\times \]

The training fit is flattering itself by more than a factor of three. The housing model, at \(p/n = 13/381 = 0.034\), has \(1/(1-0.034) = 1.035\), so on the same squared basis it inflates by only \(1.035^2 \approx 1.07\times\) — barely anything. Same technique, wildly different exposure, and the ratio \(p/n\) is the number that tells you which situation you are in. This is also the strongest possible argument for why the CarPrice model needs Ridge and the housing model does not.

▸ Proof of the PRESS identity (uses the Sherman–Morrison formula)

Removing row \(i\) removes its contribution from both accumulators:

\[ X_{(-i)}^{\!\top}X_{(-i)} = X^{\!\top}X - \mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^{\!\top}, \qquad X_{(-i)}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y}_{(-i)} = X^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}_i y_i \]

Sherman–Morrison gives the inverse of a rank-one update in closed form:

\[ (A - \mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\!\top})^{-1} = A^{-1} + \frac{A^{-1}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}A^{-1}}{1 - \mathbf{x}^{\!\top}A^{-1}\mathbf{x}} \]

With \(A = X^\top X\) the denominator is \(1 - h_{ii}\), since \(h_{ii} = \mathbf{x}_i^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf{x}_i\) by definition. Combining and simplifying:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{(-i)} = \hat{\mathbf{w}} - \frac{(X^{\!\top}X)^{-1}\mathbf{x}_i\,e_i}{1-h_{ii}} \]

Predicting at \(\mathbf{x}_i\) with those weights, \(\hat{y}_i^{(-i)} = \hat{y}_i - \dfrac{h_{ii}e_i}{1-h_{ii}}\), so

\[ y_i - \hat{y}_i^{(-i)} = e_i + \frac{h_{ii}e_i}{1-h_{ii}} = \frac{e_i(1-h_{ii}) + h_{ii}e_i}{1-h_{ii}} = \frac{e_i}{1-h_{ii}} \qquad\blacksquare \]

Step 4 · Finding the best λ

Your notebook plots training and validation error against \(\lambda\) and reads the optimum off the curve. The shapes of those two curves are forced by the mathematics of the previous lecture:

  • Training error rises monotonically with λ. Provable: at \(\lambda = 0\) the OLS solution minimises training error by definition, and any larger \(\lambda\) moves the solution away from that minimiser. Adding constraint can never improve the objective it is constraining.
  • Validation error is U-shaped. At small λ, variance dominates — the model is fitting noise. At large λ, bias dominates — the model has been shrunk into a near-constant. The minimum is the bias–variance sweet spot from Lecture 4, now reachable by turning one dial.

Grid search, counted

The cost of an exhaustive grid search is a product, not a sum:

\[ \text{fits} = k \times \prod_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|\text{grid}_j\bigr| \;\;(+\,1\text{ refit on the full training set}) \]

Your notebook's verbose=1 output confirms it three times: Fitting 5 folds for each of 28 candidates, totalling 140 fits (5 × 28), then 65 fits (5 × 13), then 190 fits (5 × 38). Note the product: two hyperparameters with ten values each at \(k=5\) is 500 fits, and three is 5,000. This is why RandomizedSearchCV exists — sampling 60 random points from a grid finds a configuration in the top 5% with probability about 95%, regardless of how many dimensions the grid has.

Step 5 · The winner's curse, and why the test set stays sealed

Your notebook opens with exactly the right warning: choosing λ by comparing test scores means you are training on the test set. Here is the formal version, which is worth giving to learners because it explains how much you are fooling yourself.

Suppose you evaluate \(m\) candidate models on the same hold-out set. Each score \(S_j\) is an unbiased but noisy estimate of that model's true performance. You report the best one. But for any random variables,

\[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\max_j S_j\Bigr] \;\ge\; \max_j \mathbb{E}[S_j] \]

The maximum of noisy estimates is biased upward — you are partly selecting the model that got the luckiest draw, not the model that is best. With \(m = 28\) alphas and a 62-row test set, that optimism is not small. The whole three-way discipline follows from this one inequality:

SetUsed forHow many times
Training foldsFitting parameters \(\mathbf{w}\)Once per fold per candidate
Validation foldsChoosing hyperparameters λ, k, degreeMany — that's the job
Test setReporting one final numberExactly once, at the end

If you look at the test score and then go back and change anything — the model, a feature, λ — the test set has become a validation set and your reported number is optimistic again. In a workplace this is the difference between a model that hits its projected numbers in production and one that quietly underperforms by five points.

Your notebooks get this structurally right: GridSearchCV is fitted on X_train only, and the test set is scored once. Worth pointing out explicitly, because it is the discipline the whole lecture is arguing for.

Step 6 · Where the two lectures meet

This is the single most important idea across both notebooks, and it is the one most easily missed because each lecture only shows half of it.

Cross-validation is supposed to simulate "a model meeting data it has never seen." If you scale before the CV loop, then the scaler saw every fold, including the one currently being held out — and the simulation is broken. The validation score is optimistic, and it is optimistic in a way no amount of extra folds will reveal.

# The shape both lectures are building towards
pipe = Pipeline([
    ('prep',  preprocessor),      # ColumnTransformer from lecture 5
    ('model', Ridge())
])

grid = GridSearchCV(
    pipe,
    param_grid={'model__alpha': [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100]},   # note the step__param syntax
    cv=KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42),
    scoring='r2'
)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)             # preprocessing re-fitted inside every fold
print(grid.best_params_, grid.best_score_)
grid.score(X_test, y_test)           # the one and only look at test

The double underscore in model__alpha is how you address a parameter of a named step. It is also how you tune preprocessing choices — prep__num__imputer__strategy is a legal hyperparameter.

Step 7 · Stratified k-fold, and why RFE needs scaling

Stratification

Plain KFold slices at random, so with a rare class the fold proportions wobble. If a class has prevalence \(\pi\), the count in a fold of size \(n/k\) is roughly Binomial, with standard deviation

\[ \sqrt{\tfrac{n}{k}\,\pi(1-\pi)} \]

At \(\pi = 0.02\), \(n = 1000\), \(k = 5\): each fold expects 4 positives with a standard deviation of about 2. Some folds will contain one positive; some may contain none at all, which makes several metrics undefined. StratifiedKFold removes that variance entirely by sampling within each class, so every fold has the same class balance as the whole. It is the default for classifiers in sklearn, and it becomes essential in the next module.

Note that stratified k-fold appears in the agenda and the MCQ but never in a code cell — worth adding a two-line demo before the classification module.

RFE

Recursive Feature Elimination is a greedy backward search: fit the model, rank features by \(|w_j|\), drop the weakest, refit, repeat until the requested count remains. Its cost is \(O(p)\) fits, and its correctness rests entirely on an assumption from Lecture 5: \(|w_j|\) is only a fair ranking if all features share a scale. On unscaled data, RFE will eliminate whatever happens to be measured in large units. Your notebook does scale beforehand, so the ranking is meaningful — but it is worth saying out loud, since it is the same reason regularisation needs scaling.

Step 8 · When R² goes to minus five billion

The polynomial experiment produces the most memorable number in either notebook:

Degree12361020
Train R²0.290.340.340.360.370.38
Test R²0.250.220.220.11−108.8−5.6 × 10⁹

Two things to draw out. First, the training column is almost flat — it climbs from 0.29 to 0.38 across nineteen extra degrees. Anyone watching only that column would conclude the models are all much the same. The test column collapses. This is the cleanest demonstration of the \(2\sigma^2p/n\) gap you will get.

Second, the magnitude is not a bug. From post-read 1, \(R^2 = 1 - SS_{\text{res}}/SS_{\text{tot}}\), and \(SS_{\text{res}}\) has no upper bound. A degree-20 polynomial fitted on area scaled to [0, 1] involves \(x^{20}\), and the design matrix columns span twenty orders of magnitude — a condition number so large that the coefficients are essentially numerical noise. Just outside the training range the curve leaves the page. Squaring those errors gets you to \(10^9\) easily.

The remedy is the previous lecture: a degree-20 polynomial with Ridge regularisation is perfectly usable, because the penalty suppresses precisely the high-order coefficients that are running away. Running that comparison live is a strong five minutes of class.

Reading your own output: three code defects in this notebook

Finding 5 — the Ridge tuning plot has no x-axis left
cv_results['param_alpha'] = cv_results['param_alpha'].astype('int32')

The alpha grid starts at 0.0001 and has thirteen values below 1.0. Casting to int32 truncates every one of them to 0. Half the grid collapses onto a single x-coordinate, and matplotlib draws a vertical scribble at the origin — the plot cannot show where the optimum is, which is the only thing it was drawn to show.

The Lasso version of the same cell uses float32 and is fine, which makes the contrast easy to demonstrate. Better still, plot on a log scale, since the grid spans seven orders of magnitude:

cv_results['param_alpha'] = cv_results['param_alpha'].astype('float64')
plt.semilogx(cv_results['param_alpha'], cv_results['mean_test_score'])

Related: the chosen alpha = 15 is not a member of the grid at all (it jumps 10 → 20), and model_cv.best_params_ is never consulted. Use grid.best_estimator_ — it is already refitted on the full training set and it removes the transcription step where a wrong number can creep in.

Finding 6 — the test set gets its own scaler
df_train[numeric_vars] = scaler.fit_transform(df_train[numeric_vars])
df_test[numeric_vars]  = scaler.fit_transform(df_test[numeric_vars])   # should be .transform

The second fit_transform throws away the training \((x_{\min}, x_{\max})\) and learns new ones from the test rows. Train and test now live on different scales — the cheapest house in the test set maps to 0.0 regardless of how it compares with the cheapest training house — so the fitted coefficients are being applied to inputs they were never calibrated for.

It is doubly worth fixing because the very next markdown cell says "we haven't rescaled the test set yet," which contradicts the code directly above it. A learner following along carefully will be confused, and a learner following along carelessly will copy the pattern into their capstone.

Finding 7 — the "final model" reports the score of a different model
n_features_optimal = 10
lm = LinearRegression()
lm.fit(X_train, y_train)
rfe = RFE(lm, n_features_to_select=n_features_optimal)

The RFE object is fitted and then never used. The reported 0.5996 belongs to the full 13-feature model, not to the 10-feature model the grid search just selected. The conclusion drawn from it — that the test score closely matches the CV mean — happens to hold anyway, but it is being supported by the wrong number.

Changing lm to rfe is the whole fix. It is a good cell to leave in as a deliberate spot-the-bug exercise, because it is exactly the kind of mistake that survives code review: nothing errors, and the output looks plausible.
