PCA: Finding Structure in High‑Dimensional Data

How a logistics company's 784‑pixel scanning problem leads to the single most useful idea in dimensionality reduction: find the directions where the data varies most.

Variance & eigenvectors Build PCA from scratch MNIST case study Pitfalls & scaling

The Parcel That Never Arrived

SwiftParcel handles 2 million parcels a day across 40 sorting hubs. Most addresses are typed, but a large share of parcels from small shops and villages still carry handwritten PIN codes.

Every parcel is photographed at the hub and the scan goes to the cloud so a machine can read the PIN and route the parcel. Meera, the lead data scientist, has a problem on her desk:

  • Each scanned digit is a 28×28 image — 784 numbers per digit. Millions of digits a day means terabytes of storage and slow pipelines.
  • The routing team wants a quick visual check of whether digit images from different hubs "look alike." Nobody can plot 784 dimensions.
  • Labelled data is expensive — most scans have no label at all.
"Can we shrink these images to a handful of numbers without losing what makes a '3' look like a '3'? And can you show me what the data looks like?"

Meera has no labels, so she can't use supervised learning. She needs a technique that looks only at the structure of the data itself. That technique is PCA — our first step into unsupervised learning.

Section 1

1Supervised vs. Unsupervised: What Changed?

Until now, every problem looked the same: observations xi paired with correct answers yi. Meera's situation is different — she has only xi, with no yi. The algorithm must discover hidden structure on its own.

Data(xi, yi)only xi
Goalpredict yfind structure / patterns
Evaluationaccuracy, RMSE… (compare with truth)no ground truth — metrics are indirect
Example"Is this image a cat or a dog?""Which images look alike? Can I summarise them?"

Why is unsupervised learning harder?

  • No labels — we don't know how many groups exist, or which point belongs where.
  • No obvious scorecard — with no truth to compare against, "was the result correct?" has no simple answer.

The two unsupervised families this course covers: dimensionality reduction (today) and clustering (next).

Section 2

2Meet the Data: A Digit Is Just 784 Numbers

We'll use MNIST, a classic dataset of handwritten digits — a stand-in for SwiftParcel's PIN-code scans.

  • Each image is a 28 × 28 grid of pixels, each with a darkness value from 0 (black) to 255 (white).
  • Flatten the grid row by row and you get a vector with 28 × 28 = 784 numbers.
  • So every digit is a point in a 784-dimensional space.
Pretend-play rule

The dataset also contains labels. To act like Meera, we will not use labels for learning — only at the end, to colour plots and sanity-check whether PCA found sensible structure.

Section 3

3Why Shrink the Data? The Group‑Photo Problem

Imagine a party where the photo booth only captures a flat 2D picture, but your friends live in 3D. You squeeze 3D into 2D and lose some information — how far back someone stood — but keep the most important thing: who is at the party and roughly where.

Dimensionality reduction does the same to data: it reduces the number of features while trying to keep the information that matters.

Two big reasons to do this

  1. The curse of dimensionality — as features grow, data becomes sparse and distances become less meaningful, so many algorithms struggle to learn.
  2. Visualisation and interpretation — we can't plot 784 axes. A 2D or 3D "shadow" lets us spot patterns.

Other benefits: lossy compression (storage and speed), noise reduction, and feature extraction. All of these matter to SwiftParcel.

Section 4

4Variance as Information: The Key Idea of PCA

A toy question. We have patients' age and weight, and want to tell diabetic from non-diabetic.

Which feature carries more information? Age is almost constant — everyone is roughly the same age, so it can't separate anyone. Weight is spread out, so it can.

The pattern: the feature along which the data varies most holds the most information.

Now take weight and height. They move together — taller people tend to weigh more. The most informative direction is neither axis alone; it's a diagonal combination of height and weight.

The core idea

  • Instead of the original features, find new axes (combinations of old ones) so the first axis captures the most variance, the second captures most of what's left, and so on.
  • These new axes are principal components (PCs) — each perpendicular (orthogonal) to the others.
  • With D original features you get D principal components. If the first few already carry ~90% of the variance, keep only those: fewer features, small information loss.

This is exactly the goal of PCA: find the direction with maximum variance. An equivalent view — the minimum-error formulation — says the max-variance direction is also the line that gives the smallest reconstruction error when points are projected onto it and mapped back. We'll see this when rebuilding digits later.

Quick check

How does PCA keep the most information when reducing dimensions?

Correct: capturing maximum variance. Orthogonality is a property PCs happen to have, but it isn't what retains information — maximum variance is. Dropping high-variance components would discard the most information, not the least.
Section 5

5The Maths in Plain Words

Setup. Data matrix X with N points and D features. We want a lower dimension M < D. Start with M = 1.

The variance along an eigenvector of S equals its eigenvalue. So the max-variance direction is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. The second PC is the eigenvector with the second-largest eigenvalue, and so on.

Points to remember

  • Bigger eigenvalue → more variance → more information kept.
  • To go from 10 dimensions to 2, keep the eigenvectors of the top 2 eigenvalues.
  • Because S is symmetric, its eigenvectors are automatically orthogonal.
Section 6

6Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors: A Hands‑On Feel

Multiply a matrix A by most vectors and the vector gets rotated and stretched. But for special vectors, A only stretches or shrinks them — the direction stays the same:

A·x = λ·x

  • x (non-zero) is an eigenvector of A.
  • λ is the corresponding eigenvalue (the stretch factor).

A matrix transforming vectors interactive

A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Pick a vector and watch what A does to it. Only two directions — the eigenvectors — stay on their own line.

Direction preserved? —

How to find them

Start from Ax = λx, so (A − λI)x = 0. For a non-zero x to exist, (A − λI) must be singular: det(A − λI) = 0. Solve for λ, then plug each λ back in to get its eigenvector.

Worked example

Let A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. Solving det(A − λI) = 0 gives (2−λ)² − 1 = 0, so λ = 3 or λ = 1.

  • For λ = 3: eigenvector ∝ [1, 1]
  • For λ = 1: eigenvector ∝ [1, −1]

Notice the two eigenvectors are perpendicular — exactly as expected for a symmetric matrix.

Quick check

An eigenvector x of matrix A satisfies Ax = λx. What does multiplying by A do to it?

Correct. An eigenvector stays on its own line; it's only scaled by the eigenvalue λ. If λ is negative, it flips to point the opposite way along the same line, but it's still on the same line.
Open question

You compute the covariance matrix of a 10-feature dataset and find its eigenvalues. (a) How many eigenvectors do you get? (b) Which one preserves the most variance, and what does its eigenvalue tell you?

(a) 10 — one per feature, since the covariance matrix is 10 × 10.

(b) The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. That eigenvalue is the variance of the data along that direction — the "explained variance" of that PC.

Section 7

7Building PCA From Scratch (Then Checking sklearn)

The maths turns into five lines of NumPy, using the height/weight data from earlier.

Recipe

  1. Centre the data
  2. Compute the covariance matrix
  3. Compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors
  4. Sort by eigenvalue, largest first
  5. Project the data onto the top M eigenvectors
Show code & output 
def pca_from_scratch(X, n_components):
    Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)                       # 1. centre
    S = np.cov(Xc, rowvar=False)                  # 2. covariance matrix (D x D)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(S)          # 3. eigen-decomposition
    order = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]             # 4. sort largest -> smallest
    eigvals, eigvecs = eigvals[order], eigvecs[:, order]
    W = eigvecs[:, :n_components]                 # keep top-M eigenvectors
    Z = Xc @ W                                    # 5. project
    return Z, W, eigvals

Z_mine, W_mine, eigvals = pca_from_scratch(data2d, n_components=1)
print("Explained variance ratio:", (eigvals / eigvals.sum()).round(3))

pca_sk = PCA(n_components=1).fit(data2d)
print("My PC1     :", W_mine[:, 0].round(4))
print("sklearn PC1:", pca_sk.components_[0].round(4), "(sign may be flipped)")
Output
Eigenvalues (variance along each PC): [157.43  14.45]
Explained variance ratio: [0.916 0.084]

My PC1     : [-0.894  -0.4482]
sklearn PC1: [0.894  0.4482] (sign may be flipped, which is fine)
sklearn explained variance ratio: [0.916]

What to notice

  • Our version and sklearn agree, up to a ± sign — an eigenvector and its negative point along the same line.
  • The first PC holds most of the variance (91.6%). Dropping PC2 compresses 2 features into 1 with a small loss.
  • PCA has a closed-form solution: the components come straight from the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. There's nothing to initialise and no step-by-step search, so PCA can't get stuck in a local minimum.
  • With no random starting point, PCA gives the same answer every time on the same data (up to sign flips) — unlike iterative algorithms such as K-Means, which depend on random starting centroids.

sklearn uses the SVD internally for speed and stability, but it's mathematically equivalent to the eigen-decomposition derived above.

8PCA on Real Data: Meera's Digits

Two questions to answer: can we draw a useful 2D map of the digits, and how many components do we really need?

A note on scaling

In many datasets (income in lakhs next to age in years), features sit on different scales and must be standardised first. Here all 784 pixels share the same 0–1 scale, so we don't standardise. Standardising MNIST would blow up nearly-empty border pixels that are almost always 0. The scaling problem is demonstrated properly in Section 9.

  • PCA never saw the labels, yet similar digits sit near each other — 0s and 1s separate clearly.
  • Many digits overlap. Only ~17% of the variance is in these 2 PCs, so a lot is lost — two numbers can't capture everything about a handwritten digit.
  • This is unsupervised structure discovery: patterns emerged without any answer key.

What does a principal component actually look like?

Each PC is a vector of 784 weights, so it can be displayed as an image.

Each PC is a pattern of strokes — a template. Any digit can be approximated as the average digit + some amount of PC1 + some amount of PC2 + …. This is why PCA works as compression: we store only the amounts, not 784 pixels.

How many components to keep?

Each PC's eigenvalue tells us the variance it explains. Summing the ratios gives the cumulative explained variance — how much information survives when keeping the top k components. Keep all D components and nothing is lost (you've only rotated the axes); drop components and you lose exactly their variance.

How many components for a target variance? interactive

Click a target to see how many of the 784 components were needed to reach it on Meera's 10,000-digit sample.

43 components out of 784 (≈5% of the original size) keep 80% of the variance
  • The scree plot drops quickly: a handful of PCs carry a large share of variance, and the long tail adds little.
  • Roughly ~90 components capture 90% of the variance, and ~150 capture 95%, out of 784 — about a 5× reduction with 5–10% of variance dropped.
  • The steep rise exists because MNIST pixels are strongly correlated, so a few directions carry most of the variance. PCA gains nothing when this is missing.
  • If all eigenvalues were equal, the cumulative curve would be a straight line, and dropping any component would lose just as much as dropping any other — PCA would run but compress nothing. Equal eigenvalues also make the principal directions non-unique, because any rotation of the axes is equally valid.
Open question

Meera's cumulative explained-variance curve reaches 95% at 150 components. Her manager says, "Great, so 95% of the information about which digit it is is preserved." Is that statement correct?

Not necessarily. 95% refers to variance (spread of pixel intensities), not to label-relevant information. PCA never sees labels. The 5% dropped could contain a subtle stroke that distinguishes a 4 from a 9. Variance is a proxy for information — to know whether digit identity survives, test a downstream task (e.g. classify digits on the reduced features).

Seeing information loss: rebuilding digits from fewer numbers

If we keep k components, we can map back to 784 pixels (inverse_transform) and compare with the original. This is the minimum-error view of PCA: the max-variance directions are the ones that give the best reconstruction.

Section 9

9When PCA Misleads: Limitations and Pitfalls

PCA is powerful, but it follows its assumptions blindly.

Seeing the scale problem on the Wine dataset

Features in very different units — proline ranges into the hundreds while most chemical measurements sit near 0–5. Without standardising, PCA chases the raw numbers, not meaning.

MetricValue
PC1 explained variance99.8%
Feature dominating PC1proline (std. dev. ≈ 314)

One feature with values in the hundreds or thousands hogs PC1 and "explains" almost all the variance — purely because of its units, not because it's the most informative.

MetricValue
PC1 explained variance36.2%
Top loadingsflavanoids (0.42), total_phenols (0.39), od280/od315 (0.38)

After standardising, variance spreads more sensibly across features and PC1 becomes a real mix of several chemical measurements.

Open question

SwiftParcel's data team runs PCA on a table with "parcel weight in grams" (0–30,000) and "delivery attempts" (1–5), without preprocessing. What will PC1 mostly represent, and what's the fix?

PC1 will be almost entirely parcel weight, because its variance is millions of times larger than that of delivery attempts — an artefact of units, not importance. Fix: standardise both features (z-score) before PCA so each starts with variance 1.

Section 10

10PCA in the Real World

Visualisation

2D/3D maps of high-dimensional data, like Meera's digit map.

Compression

Storing images and signals with fewer numbers — e.g. face recognition "eigenfaces."

Preprocessing

Fewer, decorrelated features often make downstream training faster and more stable. Whether it improves accuracy depends on the data — always test it.

Finance & risk

Portfolios of hundreds of instruments summarised by a few "factors" — interest-rate level, slope, curvature.

Science

Untangling many interacting measurements, for example in neuroscience.

Choosing k is also a form of regularisation

A small k gives the model fewer features, so it has less freedom to memorise noise, which can reduce overfitting. A large k gives more freedom, so regularisation is weaker. At k = all components, nothing is dropped and there's no regularisation effect. Treat k as a setting to tune: too small and the model underfits (useful information thrown away), too large and it may overfit. Compare train and test accuracy for different values of k.

When the dataset is too big for memory

With, say, 1 million rows and 1000 features, PCA is one of several options:

  • Sample the rows — train on a random subset with fewer rows and all columns. For classification, use a stratified sample so rare classes aren't lost.
  • Drop redundant columns — highly correlated features carry almost the same information, so remove one from each pair (correlation for numerical features, chi-square for categorical). This is feature selection: it keeps a subset of original columns, unlike PCA, which creates new ones.
  • Use a model that learns in small batches — an SGD-based linear model updates on one sample or mini-batch at a time, so the full dataset never needs to sit in memory.

Wrap-Up: Back to Meera's Desk

Meera walks into her manager's office with three results:

  1. Storage — each digit scan drops from 784 numbers to ~150 with only a small loss of variance, and reconstructed digits stay readable. Roughly a 5× saving across millions of daily scans.
  2. Visibility — a 2D PCA map shows similar digits clustering together, even though no labels were used.
  3. Honest caveats — 2 components are far too few for accuracy. Variance isn't the same as label-relevant information, and scaling must be handled carefully on other data.

Key takeaways

  • Unsupervised learning finds structure without labels, and is harder to evaluate.
  • PCA finds new orthogonal axes, ranked by how much variance they capture.
  • Principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; the eigenvalues are the variances along them.
  • Recipe: centre → covariance → eigen-decomposition → sort → keep top k → project.
  • Keeping all components loses nothing. Dropping components loses exactly their variance. Use the cumulative explained variance curve to choose k.
  • Always standardise when features have different units, and watch for outliers and non-linear structure.
