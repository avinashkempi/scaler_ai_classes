      Why a straight line can't grade a battery cell

      A plant sorts forty thousand lithium-ion cells a day into ship, rework, or scrap. Every classifier in the standard toolkit fails on the data below — and the reason they fail is the entire subject of this reading.

      1,200 logged cells 2 sensor readings 3 grades: A / B / C
      x1: resistance deviation  /  x2: temperature-rise deviation
      1 THE CASE FILE

      The last shift at the Hexa Cell desk

      Hexa Cell runs a lithium-ion cell plant. About forty thousand finished cells come off the line every day, and at the end of that line sits a small desk.

      Every cell is briefly put on a test rig that records two cheap measurements, and gets sorted into one of three bins:

      GradeMeaningWhat happens to the cell
      AHealthyShips to the customer
      BBorderlineGoes back for rework
      CFaultyScrapped

      For eleven years, one supervisor has made that call by eye, with a famously good hit rate. She retires in four months. The plant has handed over a file of past readings with her decisions attached, and one instruction: build something that makes this call.

      The two measurements

      The rig reports two numbers, both centred and scaled so a perfectly nominal cell sits at zero:

      x1 — internal resistance deviation
      How far this cell's internal resistance sits from the nominal value for its batch. Negative is below nominal, positive is above.
      x2 — temperature-rise deviation
      How far the cell's temperature rise during test discharge sits from nominal. Same convention.
      y — the supervisor's grade
      Encoded as 0 for A, 1 for B, 2 for C — this is the label a model has to predict.

      Looking at it before modelling anything

      1,200 logged cells, two features, one three-valued target — small enough to plot honestly, which is the reason to start here rather than on a million-row table.

      Figure 1 · the plant's log, plotted
      click a grade to isolate it

      What the picture is telling us

      Two things matter here more than any algorithm choice that comes next.

      1. Feature engineering can change the problem completely. A model might see resistance and temperature as two independent numbers. But the real question is how far those values sit from their nominal, healthy range. By transforming the original features — into deviations, absolute values, ratios, or other combinations — patterns hidden in the original representation can surface. Sometimes the classifier isn't too simple; the features simply aren't expressing the right information.

      2. The boundary is not a line, and it could never have been one. A cell is healthy when it sits close to nominal, and faulty when it drifts far from nominal — in either direction. Resistance far above nominal is bad; resistance far below nominal is also bad, for a different physical reason. A quantity that's dangerous at both extremes and safe in the middle cannot be split by a straight line. The healthy region is enclosed. The grades sit in rings — and that's not an artefact of the plot, it's what the physics of the process looks like.

      So, before reaching for a fancier classifier

      We should ask: can we engineer features that make this ring structure easier for a model to learn? That question is worth testing directly — and the toolkit we already have is the place to test it.
      2 PULSE CHECK

      Can anything in the existing toolkit do this?

      Four classifiers, four precise diagnoses. "It doesn't work" is useless — "it fails because its hypothesis class only contains straight lines" is something we can act on.

      Handles 3 classes?Not directlyscaffolding needed — built for two classes. One-vs-rest or one-vs-one bolts on three separate models.
      Handles a curved boundary?Nono — the boundary is always of the form a·x1 + b·x2 = c, a straight line, full stop.
      Can it be rescued?Yes, by handmanual — feed it engineered features (squares, products) and the boundary curves in the original space.
      The actual problem: a human has to invent those features. With two sensors and a 2D plot, tedious. With four hundred sensor channels, impossible — there's no plot to stare at.
      Handles 3 classes?Yes, nativelyyes — it just takes a vote among the neighbours.
      Handles a curved boundary?Yesyes — it makes no assumption about shape at all.
      The actual problem: it's non-parametric — the training set is the model. At forty thousand cells a day, the stored table reaches millions of rows within a year, and inference cost grows with it. It gets slower exactly as it gets better. A second issue specific to this data: near the boundary between two rings, the nearest points genuinely belong to both grades — geometry alone can't separate them there.
      Handles 3 classes?Yesyes
      Handles a curved boundary?In a manner of speakingstaircase — every split is x1 < threshold or x2 < threshold, so every boundary piece is axis-parallel. A tree approximates the circle with a staircase of rectangles, needing real depth to make that staircase fine.
      The actual problem: trees degrade badly on high-dimensional, sparse data. The roadmap has a camera coming to that desk — once the input is a photograph, a tree is looking at tens of thousands of pixel columns and splitting on one pixel at a time. That doesn't work.

      Handles a curved boundary?Yes, with a kernelyes — an RBF kernel handles rings well.
      The actual problem: cost at scale. Kernel methods need a kernel matrix with one entry per pair of training points — an m × m matrix. At forty thousand cells a day, m reaches a million within a month, and a million-by-million matrix isn't something to hold in memory.

      Running all four, and reading the numbers honestly

      Test-set accuracy on the 1,200-row log (25% held out), against three-way random guessing at 0.333:

      Figure 2 · test accuracy by classifier

      Some of these do score well — KNN and the RBF SVM both land around 0.97. So why isn't this finished? Because accuracy on 1,200 toy rows isn't the constraint. The constraints are the ones diagnosed above: KNN's prediction cost grows with the training set, kernel methods can demand unaffordable memory as data grows, and none of these families naturally handles raw high-dimensional input such as images.

      But there's a deeper limitation running through the entire exercise: a human being still has to decide what features the model should see. No classical model here takes arbitrary raw input and learns the entire representation end to end.

      Q1. The grading desk will soon also receive a 256×256 grayscale photograph of each cell — 65,538 features instead of 2. A team member proposes sticking with a decision tree "since it already handles three classes and a curved boundary." What's the strongest technical objection?

      B is correct. A tree's hypothesis is a conjunction of single-feature threshold tests. Any one pixel carries almost no information alone — what matters is the pattern across neighbourhoods of pixels. Expressing that would take an enormous number of splits, and the tree would latch onto noise long before getting there.

      A is wrong — trees handle multi-class natively; a leaf just holds the majority class. That objection applies to logistic regression and SVM, not trees. C is wrong — trees are invariant to monotonic rescaling, since a split is just a threshold comparison. D is wrong — that describes KNN; a trained tree is a fixed structure, and training data is discarded after fitting.

      3 WHERE THE REAL GAP IS

      A classifier does two separate jobs

      Before reaching for a new model family, it's worth being precise about what's actually missing.

      Fig. 3 · every algorithm studied so far does Job 2 well. Not one of them does Job 1.

      Here's how sharp that distinction is on the plant's own data. Logistic regression scored 0.34 — pure chance. Watch what one hand-built feature does to it: the squared distance from nominal, r² = x1² + x2² — physically, "how far has this cell drifted, regardless of direction."

      Figure 4 · one engineered feature, before and after
      raw x1, x2
      0.343
      + r²
      0.973

      The model did not change. Same logistic regression, same straight-line hypothesis class, trained the same way. What changed is the space it works in. In (x1, x2, x1²+x2²), the three rings become three flat slabs stacked on top of each other — and a flat boundary separates slabs perfectly well.

      So the model was never the bottleneck. The representation was. Which leaves an uncomfortable question: who invents x1²+x2²? Today, we do — there are two features and we can see the plot. With a camera and 65,538 pixel columns, nobody can. There's no plot, and no physical intuition that says "square pixel 4,412 and add it to pixel 9,003."

      What we actually need

      Requirement 1

      Self-discovered features

      A model that finds its own features instead of waiting for a human to hand them over.

      Requirement 2

      Native multi-class

      Handles more than two classes without external one-vs-rest scaffolding.

      Requirement 3

      Fixed inference cost

      Size and inference cost are fixed once trained, and don't grow as the plant collects more data.

      Logistic regression already satisfies Requirement 3 — once trained, it's a handful of numbers, and grading a cell costs the same on a thousand rows or a billion. That makes it the right thing to build on, not throw away. The question is what to add to it.

      4 ALREADY EVERYWHERE

      Neural networks are already running most of your day

      Models meeting all three requirements already exist, and most people use several of them before reaching a desk in the morning.

      Ranking and advertising

      A search for an industrial part returns sponsored listings that are unnervingly on-topic; later, ads on a video app shift to match.

      The system estimates, for every candidate ad, the probability this user clicks it right now — from the query, page context, time, device, and a compressed history summary. Nobody hand-engineered "is an industrial buyer" as a column. The model built that notion itself.

      Data compression

      A photo app holds thirty thousand pictures without demanding a storage upgrade every month.

      A family of networks called autoencoders learns to squeeze an input down to a small internal code and reconstruct the original from it. Training forces the small code to keep what matters and discard what doesn't — a representation that's learned, not designed.

      Image editing and enhancement

      A stray object is removed from a photo with one tap, and the background fills in convincingly.

      Underneath is image segmentation: every pixel is classified into a category. One prediction per pixel, millions of pixels, and the classes aren't known in advance in any simple way. Once the object's pixels are isolated, a second network predicts what was probably behind them.

      Text prediction

      A mail client offers three one-line reply suggestions, and one gets used.

      The model predicts likely continuations of text given everything written so far — the same family of idea sitting underneath modern AI assistants. Different scale, same building block.

      What's common to all four

      None of these systems was given hand-engineered features. In every case, raw input — queries, pixels, characters — goes in, and the model constructs its own intermediate representation on the way to the answer. That's Requirement 1, solved, at industrial scale.

      So what are these systems made of? Every one is built by repeating a single small unit, millions of times — and the blueprint for that unit came from the exact thing being replaced at the Hexa Cell desk: a human making a judgement call.

      5 INSPIRATION

      From a control room to a biological neuron

      The control room

      Forget the classifier for a moment. Hexa Cell has a control room, and when something goes wrong on the line, the shift supervisor there decides whether to halt production. She doesn't sense anything herself — everything she knows arrives from other people: the rig operator reporting test readings, the maintenance technician reporting vibration, the QA sampler reporting scrap counts, a junior trainee reporting that something "seemed off."

      She doesn't weigh them equally. The technician has been right about vibration for six years, so his word counts heavily. The trainee started Monday, so his word counts for very little — not zero, but very little. She combines what she's heard, weighted by trust — and she doesn't act on every level of concern. Small concern, she notes it. Past a point, she picks up the phone. That trigger point is a property of her, not of any one report.

      That is a complete description of a decision unit. Now look at a biological neuron.

      Fig. 5 · dendrite thickness sets influence; the cell body sums; a threshold gates firing.

      A neuron connects to many others through dendrites. Those connections aren't identical — some dendrites are physically thicker, and a signal on a thick connection influences the cell far more than the same signal on a thin one. The cell body accumulates everything that arrives. If the accumulated signal crosses a threshold, the neuron fires and sends a signal down its axon. Below threshold, it stays quiet — and that threshold is a property of the neuron itself, independent of any incoming signal.

      The mapping

      Control roomBiological neuronMachine learning
      Four people reportingSignals on dendritesFeatures x1, x2, …, xd
      How much she trusts each personThickness of each dendriteWeights w1, w2, …, wd
      Combining reports by trustCell body accumulating inputWeighted sum
      How jumpy this supervisor isThe neuron's own firing thresholdBias b
      Deciding whether to halt the lineFiring, or staying quietActivation function f
      The decision she announcesThe signal sent down the axonOutput o

      Three things worth pausing on. A weight is an importance, and it is signed — large positive pushes towards firing, large negative pushes away, near zero means the input is ignored (the trainee isn't a negative signal; he's close to zero). The bias belongs to the unit, not to any input — two neurons with identical inputs and weights can behave completely differently with different biases. Weights are learned: nobody handed the supervisor a trust table on day one, she built it over six years — and the mechanism for that in a model is gradient descent.

      One caution

      The analogy is useful and genuinely limited. An artificial neuron is a loose caricature of a biological one — real neurons involve timing, chemistry, and dynamics not modelled here at all. The inspiration was historical; the mathematics stands on its own.
      Q2. A neuron takes a temperature reading x1 (typically 20–90°C) and a vibration reading x2 (typically 0.1–0.6 mm/s). After training, w1 = 0.04 and w2 = 3.1. A colleague concludes vibration is roughly 75× more important than temperature. What's wrong with that conclusion?

      B is correct. The neuron sums w·x terms, so what a feature contributes is the product, not the weight alone. Temperature spans ~70 units → contributes about 0.04 × 70 = 2.8. Vibration spans ~0.5 units → contributes about 3.1 × 0.5 = 1.55. Temperature is actually contributing more variation — the opposite of the stated conclusion. Comparing raw weight magnitudes is only valid after standardising features.

      Practical takeaway: to read weights as feature importances, standardise your features first — then a one-unit move means the same thing for every input.

      6 THE MATHEMATICS

      Writing the supervisor down as an equation

      Words don't train. Here's the same description turned into mathematics, one piece at a time.

      Notation for the dataset

      m
      the number of training examples — 900 in the Hexa Cell training split.
      d
      the number of input features per example — 2 here.
      x_i
      the i-th training example, a vector of d numbers.
      x_ij
      the j-th feature of the i-th example — x₅,₂ is the temperature-rise deviation of the fifth logged cell.
      y_i
      the true label of the i-th example — one of 0, 1, 2.
      X
      the full feature matrix, m rows by d columns; row i is x_i.

      Step 1 · the weighted sum

      The unit receives d inputs. Each input x_j has a weight w_j attached, learned during training, plus a single bias b belonging to the unit itself:

      z  =  w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + w_d x_d + b  =  ∑j=1d w_j x_j + b  =  wTx + b

      z is the net input, or pre-activation. For the Hexa Cell desk with d = 2: z = w1·x1 + w2·x2 + b.

      What w and b actually do, geometrically

      Set z = 0 and ask which points satisfy it: w1·x1 + w2·x2 + b = 0. That's a straight line in the (x1, x2) plane — in d dimensions, a flat (d−1)-dimensional hyperplane, splitting space into z > 0 and z < 0.

      • The vector w sets the orientation. w points perpendicular to the line, in the direction z increases fastest — like the direction of "downhill" on a tilted floor.
      • The number b sets the position. Changing b slides the line without rotating it — like the sensitivity dial on a smoke alarm: it doesn't change what's being listened to, only how much is needed before it goes off.
      • The magnitude of z measures distance. For any point x, the perpendicular distance to the line is |z| / ‖w‖, where ‖w‖ is the length of w.

      Sign gives the side. Magnitude gives the confidence. Try both live in the neuron playground further down — moving w1, w2 rotates the line; moving b slides it.

      Step 2 · the activation function

      Stopping at z would leave an unbounded real number — not a decision, and the supervisor didn't behave that way; she stayed quiet below a level of concern and acted above it. So the unit applies a function to z:

      o = f(z) = f( wTx + b )

      The slogan worth memorising

      Neuron = linear part, then activation. Everything in this course is built out of that pair.

      The common activation functions

      Four choices for how a unit converts a signed distance into a response. Switch between them below — the curve redraws live.

      Figure 6 · activation function explorer

      Step
      the literal "fires or doesn't." Throws away the magnitude of z entirely, and is flat everywhere — which turns out to be fatal for gradient-based training.
      Sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᵀ)
      output strictly between 0 and 1, readable as a probability. σ(0) = 0.5 exactly. Smooth everywhere, so differentiable everywhere — a dimmer switch rather than a light switch.
      Tanh(z)
      the same S-shape, recentred: output between −1 and 1, tanh(0) = 0. Being centred at zero is often convenient during training.
      ReLU(z) = max(0, z)
      a one-way valve — passes positive signal unchanged, blocks negative signal completely. Crude, not even smooth at zero, and the default choice in most modern networks.
      Q3. A neuron has w1 = 1.5, w2 = −0.5, b = −2.0, sigmoid activation. A cell arrives with x1 = 2.0, x2 = −1.0. Which is correct?

      A is correct. z = (1.5)(2.0) + (−0.5)(−1.0) + (−2.0) = 3.0 + 0.5 − 2.0 = 1.5. Note the middle term: both weight and feature are negative, so their product is positive — the most common slip in this calculation. Then σ(1.5) = 1/(1+e⁻¹․⁵) ≈ 0.82.

      B comes from treating the second term as −0.5, or dropping the bias. C has the right z but reports σ(−1.5) instead. D adds the bias with the wrong sign somewhere. Since z = 1.5 is positive, this cell sits on the "high output" side of the dividing line — sigmoid converts that into a fairly confident 0.82, not certain (that would need a much larger z).

      7 A THREE-LINE PROOF

      Why the activation function is not optional

      A reasonable question: why not just chain more linear equations instead of bothering with an activation function at all?

      Start simple: y = 2x + 3. A straight line, no matter the value of x. Now make it "more complicated":

      y = 2(3x + 4) + 5

      Looks more powerful. Simplify it, though:

      y = 6x + 13

      Back to another linear equation. Keep adding linear operations —

      y = 2(3(4x + 1) + 5) + 7

      — and after simplifying, the result is still y = ax + b for some a and b. Stacking more linear operations never produces a fundamentally new kind of function.

      So where does non-linearity come from? An operation that can't be simplified back into y = ax + b. For example:

      y = (2x + 3)2  =  4x2 + 12x + 9

      That contains x² — no longer a straight line. It can represent curves. That's the role of an activation function: it introduces non-linearity into the computation.

      The conclusion

      Non-linearity is not decoration. Without it, repeatedly applying linear operations only gives another linear function — adding more layers does not automatically make a model more expressive. Later, activation functions are what let different layers build increasingly complex representations out of simpler ones.
      8 AT SCALE

      Doing this for all the cells at once

      At the desk, cells arrive one at a time and one z gets computed per cell. In practice, a whole batch is computed in a single matrix multiplication — getting the shapes right is most of the battle.

      X
      the input matrix, shape (m, d). Rows are cells, columns are features.
      W
      the weight matrix for a layer of h units, shape (d, h). Column k holds the d weights belonging to unit k.
      b
      the bias vector, shape (1, h) — one bias per unit.
      Z
      the net inputs, shape (m, h). Entry (i, k) is the net input of unit k on cell i.
      A
      the activated outputs, shape (m, h).
      Z = XW + b    A = f(Z)

      A worked example: one layer of h = 4 ReLU-style sigmoid units sitting on the d = 2 features, with small random starting weights. The first cell in the training set reads x1 = −0.236, x2 = 0.842:

      Four units looked at the same cell and returned four different numbers, because each has its own column of weights — its own w-direction and its own dividing line. Right now those weights are random, so the opinions are meaningless; training (gradient descent, unchanged from the previous session) is what turns them into something useful. What's new here is only the shape of the function being differentiated.

      Three things this establishes, in order. Changing w1/w2 rotates the line; changing b translates it — exactly as the algebra said, and the two knobs never interfere. Changing the activation changes the shading, never the line — sigmoid gives a soft gradient, step gives a hard edge, ReLU clips one side flat, but the boundary is fixed by z = 0, computed before f is applied. No setting of the sliders classifies the rings. The healthy cells sit surrounded on all sides, and a straight line cannot enclose a region.

      One neuron is one straight cut through the space — which is a good moment to notice that this object has been built before, under a different name.

      9 A FAMILIAR OBJECT

      Logistic regression is a neuron

      Put the two definitions next to each other.

      A neuron with sigmoid activation

      z = w₁x₁+…+w_d x_d+b
      o = σ(z) = 1/(1+e⁻ᵀ)

      Logistic regression, as written earlier

      z = w₁x₁+…+w_d x_d+b
      ý = 1/(1+e⁻ᵀ)

      These aren't similar. They're the same equation with different letters. Every logistic regression model ever trained was a single artificial neuron with a sigmoid activation — nothing hidden, just no prior reason to draw it as a unit with inputs flowing in. This object has a name: a logistic regression unit. It bundles a linear combination and a non-linearity into one repeatable block.

      Fig. 9 · the two boxes are the two halves of the slogan: linear, then activation.

      Forward propagation

      Moving from inputs, through the unit, to output is a forward pass. For one cell: take the readings → compute z = w1·x1 + w2·x2 + b → compute o = f(z) → compare o with the true label y to get the loss. Four steps.

      Recognising logistic regression as a neuron doesn't improve logistic regression. What it gives is a building block with a standard interface: d numbers in, one number out, d+1 parameters inside. A block with a standard interface can be repeated, and its output — being a number — can feed into another block of the same kind. That's the whole idea of a network.

      Other models that are also one neuron

      If sigmoid gives logistic regression, what do the other activations give? The skeleton z = w·x + b stays identical; only f changes.

      ModelActivation fOutput means
      Perceptron (1957)stepa hard class verdict
      Logistic Regressionsigmoidprobability of the positive class
      Linear SVMhinge-baseda signed margin

      The step activation gives the perceptron — built by Rosenblatt in 1957, the first thing reasonably called a neural network model, outputting a hard verdict with no notion of confidence. Hinge-type behaviour gives the linear SVM — at its core, the same linear unit trained against a hinge loss with an L2 penalty on the weights. What separates it from logistic regression isn't the architecture, it's the loss function and regularisation.

      Three models that occupy separate chapters in a classical ML course, differing only in what happens after the weighted sum. One neuron already contains three of the most important models in classical machine learning — so what does a network of them contain?

      10 MULTI-CLASS, NATIVELY

      Handling three grades, not two

      Requirement 2 was multi-class without external scaffolding. The neuron framing solves it almost for free.

      Instead of one output unit, use one output unit per class — for Hexa Cell that's three, one each for grades A, B, C. Each has its own weights and bias, producing its own net input z_k = w_k·x + b_k for k = 1, 2, 3. Three numbers, one per grade — which need to become three probabilities that are positive and sum to one.

      Why not just use three sigmoids?

      Sigmoid could be applied independently to each output, p_k = σ(z_k) — but sigmoid treats each output independently. Nothing forces the three to be mutually exclusive or to sum to one. The model could produce [0.9, 0.8, 0.7] — does that mean 90% A, 80% B, 70% C? Those numbers don't form a valid probability distribution for a single prediction. The outputs need to compete: raising the score for one class should affect the relative probabilities of the others.

      p_k = ez_k / ∑j=1K ez_j

      The exponential makes every value positive, so nothing can come out as a negative probability; dividing by the sum forces the results to total exactly one. The predicted grade is whichever p_k is largest. Softmax is the multi-class generalisation of sigmoid — with K = 2 it reduces to exactly the sigmoid used earlier. No one-vs-rest wrapper, no three separate models: three units and one normalisation step, trained together as one model.

      Figure 10 · live softmax calculator

      Move any slider — the three probabilities always stay positive and always sum to 1.00, because raising one score pulls share away from the others.

      11 FROM ONE NEURON TO A NETWORK

      Many straight cuts enclose a curved region

      One neuron is one straight cut. The rings need a closed region. So what does a collection of neurons give us?

      Think physically: one straight fence divides a field into two. Two fences at an angle carve out a wedge. Three can enclose a triangle. Four, a quadrilateral. Eight, an octagon — already a decent approximation of a circle.

      1 cuttwo halves
      2 cutsa wedge
      3 cutsa triangle
      4 cutsa quadrilateral
      8 cutsan octagon

      Nobody tells each fence where to stand. Each unit has its own w and b, and training moves them. What emerges is a set of cuts that, together, enclose the healthy region — and this is precisely what "learning your own features" means in practice. Each hidden unit computes "how far is this cell past my particular line," and those numbers, collectively, form a new description of the cell. Nobody wrote down x1²+x2²; the network assembled something that does the same job.

      The structure

      Fig. 11 · every input connects to every hidden unit; every hidden unit connects to every output unit.
      Input layer

      The raw features. Holds no parameters — just where data enters.

      Hidden layer

      Any layer between input and output. "Hidden" only because its outputs are never directly observed — there are no labels for h3, and none are needed.

      Output layer

      Produces the final prediction — one unit per class here.

      Fully connected

      Also called dense: every unit in a layer receives input from every unit in the previous layer.

      Depth

      The number of layers.

      Width

      The number of units in a layer.

      Counting the parameters

      A fully connected layer with n_in inputs and n_out units holds n_in × n_out weights (every input connects to every unit) plus n_out biases (one per unit):

      parameters in a layer = n_in × n_out + n_out

      For the network drawn above (2 inputs, 5 hidden units, 3 outputs):

      LayerCalculationParameters
      Hidden2 × 5 + 515
      Output5 × 3 + 318
      Total33

      Thirty-three numbers, learned by gradient descent, and that's the entire model. Once trained, grading a cell costs the same whether the plant logged a thousand cells or a billion — Requirement 3, and what KNN and kernel SVM couldn't give.

      Q4. An engineer builds a network with eight hidden layers of 512 units each and forgets to apply any activation function anywhere, so every layer computes only xW + b. She trains it on the Hexa Cell data. What happens?

      B is correct. Composing linear maps gives a linear map: o = x(W1W2…W9) + b′, and the product of those matrices is a single d×k matrix. This roughly two-million-parameter network has exactly the expressive power of plain logistic regression — a straight boundary, insufficient for rings.

      A is wrong — parameter count isn't expressive power; every one of those millions of parameters is spent re-parameterising a single linear map. C is wrong — this is severe underfitting (can't even fit training data), the opposite of overfitting. D is wrong — gradients are perfectly well defined; the loss will decrease and converge to the best straight-line solution, which happens to be useless here. That's what makes the bug dangerous: nothing crashes, nothing warns you.

At four, the shaded region becomes a quadrilateral: four straight cuts enclose the healthy zone, and accuracy jumps sharply with only 27 parameters. At eight and sixteen, the shape approaches a circle, but accuracy has already plateaued — more cuts stop buying much once the region is well enclosed.

      Curvature emerges from combination

      It isn't inside any single unit — which is exactly why units must be non-linear; without f, the combination collapses back to one cut.

      27 parameters beat 900 stored rows

      The RBF SVM scored similarly here, but had to keep the entire training set. This network stays a fixed size no matter how much data the plant collects.

      Nobody engineered a feature

      x1²+x2² was never written down. The network found its own way to express "distance from nominal" — Requirement 1, satisfied.

      12 A GENUINELY STRANGE HISTORY

      If four neurons solve this, why eleven years by hand?

      Four neurons solve a problem that defeated the entire classical toolkit, and the idea has existed since 1957. So why hasn't the Hexa Cell plant automated this decades ago?

      Why this matters for the plant

      Hexa Cell sorted cells by hand for eleven years, and that was the correct decision for most of that time. What changed isn't the algorithm — the mathematics was available in 1986. What changed is that the plant now runs forty thousand cells a day through an instrumented line, every reading is logged automatically, and a laptop can train this model in seconds. The mathematics was ready in 1986; the data and the hardware were not.

      13 CHOOSING A FAMILY

      Neural networks versus classical ML, as data grows

      The history above has a lesson worth extracting properly, because it's how you decide which family to reach for.

      Figure 13 · performance vs. data volume (illustrative shapes, not measured values)

      Reading the plot

      • Where data is scarce, classical ML is competitive and often better. It climbs fast and early. A well-tuned gradient-boosted tree on a few thousand rows will frequently beat a neural network, and will certainly be faster to build — the correct choice in that regime, and most business problems live there.
      • Classical methods flatten out. Past a point, more data stops helping — their capacity is bounded by the feature engineering handed to them, and more rows don't buy more features.
      • Neural networks start slower and keep climbing. With little data they overfit or fail to train — not enough examples to pin down every parameter. But their ceiling is far higher, and they keep converting extra data into extra performance long after classical curves have gone flat.
      • Bigger networks need more data to get going, and reach higher. The same family at different sizes — capacity that can't be fed is capacity that hurts.

      The operational rule

      The amount of labelled data available is a primary input into model choice, not an afterthought. Deep learning is not universally better — it's better in a specific regime, and that regime is defined mostly by data volume.
      14 AI INFUSION

      Auditing an AI assistant with what you just learned

      Three ways to use an AI assistant as a study partner this week — the point is to generate something to verify, not to outsource the thinking.

      ✎ Try this · the architecture lineage
      "Logistic regression, the perceptron, and a linear SVM are all a single artificial neuron differing only in activation and loss function. Extend that lineage forward: starting from one neuron, list the sequence of architectural ideas leading to today's large language models, and for each step state the single specific limitation of the previous step that it removed. One sentence per step — the chain of problems and solutions, not a timeline of company announcements."

      What to look for: a chain where each item removes a named weakness of the one before it — one neuron can't make a curved boundary, so stack layers; stacked layers can't be trained, so backpropagation; dense layers on images ignore spatial structure, so convolution; and onward. A list of dates and product names is the wrong kind of answer — ask again for the problem-solution chain specifically.

      ✎ Try this · a worked example with a planted error
      "Generate one worked numerical example of a forward pass through a single artificial neuron with three inputs and a sigmoid activation — pick the weights, bias, and inputs yourself, with at least one weight and one input negative. Show z and the final output. Then generate a second example of the same kind, but deliberately introduce exactly one arithmetic mistake somewhere, without telling me where. Label them Example 1 and Example 2, and don't reveal which one is broken."

      Work through both by hand, decide which one is broken and where, then ask the assistant to confirm. Two things get trained here: the mechanics of a forward pass, and the habit of checking AI output against your own arithmetic rather than trusting it.

      ✎ Try this · classifier selection for your own data
      "I'm choosing a classifier for this problem: [describe your data — rows, features, classes, whether features are raw sensor readings, images, text, or engineered columns]. For each of logistic regression, KNN, decision trees, and kernel SVM, tell me the single specific reason it would fail or become impractical on my problem — not a generic pros-and-cons list. If an algorithm would work fine, say so plainly instead of inventing a weakness."

      A good answer names a mechanism — hypothesis class, memory growth, inference cost, dimensionality. A generic comparison table that would be identical for any dataset is the wrong kind of answer.

      One piece of arithmetic worth doing

      The four-unit network above had 27 parameters. Apply the same formula, n_in × n_out + n_out, to the assistant behind any of those prompts:

      4,096 × 4,096 + 4,096 = 16,781,312

      Roughly 16.8 million parameters — from a single layer with 4,096 inputs and 4,096 units. Large models stack many such layers alongside other components, which is how published parameter counts reach the hundreds of billions. Every one of those parameters sits in exactly the structure covered today: a weighted sum, a bias, an activation function, arranged by the same slogan — linear, then activation — repeated at a scale that's hard to picture but not hard to describe.

      15 WRAP-UP

      What actually happened this session

      The toolkit's failure, precisely

      • The existing toolkit fails on the Hexa Cell data, each in a specific way: logistic regression and linear SVM draw only straight boundaries; KNN keeps the whole training set and slows as data grows; trees split one feature at a time and collapse on high-dimensional input; kernel SVM needs an m×m matrix that becomes unaffordable at scale.
      • The bottleneck was never boundary-drawing — it was representation. The same logistic regression that scored 0.34 scored 0.97 once handed x1²+x2². Classical ML does Job 2 well and leaves Job 1 to a human, and Job 1 becomes impossible once the input is pixels or text.

      The neuron, precisely

      • An artificial neuron is a weighted sum followed by an activation: z = w·x + b, then o = f(z). w orients the dividing hyperplane, b slides it, and |z|/‖w‖ is the distance from a point to it. Weights are signed importances; the bias belongs to the unit, not any input.
      • The mapping from biology is direct: dendrites are features, thickness is weights, cell body is the weighted sum, firing threshold is the bias, firing is the activation. Useful as intuition, not as biology.
      • Without an activation function, depth is worthless. Composing linear layers gives o = xW′+b′ — a single linear layer. A hundred layers with no non-linearity draw one straight line.
      • Logistic regression is one neuron with a sigmoid. The perceptron is one neuron with a step. A linear SVM is the same unit with a hinge loss — three classical models, one skeleton, different activations and losses.

      The network, precisely

      • Forward propagation is input to output; backpropagation is the reverse pass computing gradients — the chain rule applied layer by layer, and the subject of the next session.
      • Multi-class needs no scaffolding: one output unit per class, then softmax p_k = e^z_k / ∑e^z_j to turn scores into probabilities that sum to one.
      • Each hidden unit is one straight cut; many cuts enclose a curved region. Four units made a quadrilateral around the healthy cells and hit ~0.97 accuracy with 27 parameters — and the curvature emerges from combination, exactly why units must be non-linear.
      • A layer with n_in inputs and n_out units has n_in×n_out+n_out parameters, fixed after training regardless of how much data was used — what KNN and kernel SVM couldn't offer.

      Context

      • The mathematics was ready in 1986; the data and hardware weren't. Backpropagation solved the training problem, two AI winters followed for reasons of compute and data volume, and ImageNet in 2012 marked the point where both constraints lifted.
      • Data volume is a primary input into model choice. Classical methods climb quickly and flatten early; neural networks start slower and keep climbing. Neither family is universally better.
      • AI is a study tool whose output must be audited. Fluency and correctness are independent — use it to examine yourself and generate variation, and check every claim you can't yet derive on your own.
