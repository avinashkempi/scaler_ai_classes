Case File

The Case of the Midnight Robberies

Introduction to Machine Learning

Detective Maya Kapoor has spent five years solving cases with a rulebook: if a suspect was near the scene, and has no alibi, and owns a matching getaway vehicle — flag them. It works, until three robberies in one week leave clues her rulebook has never seen. This is the case where she stops writing rules by hand, and starts asking her old files to teach her something new.

01

The Case Begins

What actually separates machine learning from the programming Maya already knows.

For five years, Detective Maya Kapoor has cracked cases the traditional way: she writes down a rulebook. If the suspect was seen near the scene, and has no alibi, and owns a matching getaway vehicle, she flags them. It works — until it doesn't.

One night, three robberies happen in the same week, each with clues her rulebook has never seen: an untied rope, an unfamiliar accent, a specific brand of shoe polish. Her rulebook has no "if" for any of it. But she has hundreds of solved case files sitting in her archive, gathering dust. And she starts to wonder — instead of writing more and more rules by hand, what if the pattern buried in her old cases could teach a system to spot new ones on its own?

"So the computer just… guesses?" her new partner, Officer Rehan Verma, asks. "Not guesses," Maya says. "It looks for the pattern that best explains everything that's already happened, and bets that the same pattern holds for what happens next. That bet is called a model."

That question — pattern-from-experience instead of rule-from-a-human — is the line between classical programming and machine learning.

Classical programming vs. machine learning
Classical programmingMachine learning
What Maya provides Rules (if-this-then-that) plus new evidence Old case files — evidence paired with confirmed outcome
What comes out A verdict, straight away A model — a learned pattern that can produce verdicts on new evidence
Who writes the decision logic Maya, by hand The learning process, worked out from the old cases
Breaks when… A case doesn't fit any rule she thought of The old cases don't represent the new situation well

In classical programming, Maya is the one encoding how to decide. In machine learning, she feeds in what happened before, and the system works out the how on its own. That's the shift this lecture is about.

02

Defining the Case, Formally

Before Maya builds anything, she needs to be precise about what she's actually asking a system to do.

In 1997, computer scientist Tom Mitchell gave the field a one-sentence definition that Maya is about to reinvent for herself.

"A computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E."

— Tom Mitchell, 1997

There's no formula to compute here, but three symbols are worth keeping straight, because they describe every ML system in this lecture. Tap each one to see how it maps onto Maya's case.

If feeding Maya more old files makes her flags more accurate, that's learning — by Mitchell's own definition. Rehan pushes back with a good catch: "What if she just memorises all 500 files perfectly and repeats them back?"

If a model just memorises the old cases instead of learning the pattern behind them, it will ace every case it's already seen and fall apart on anything new. That trap has a name: overfitting. Maya guards against it by always testing her system on a batch of solved cases she deliberately held back and never let it study — her test set. If it performs well there too, she knows it actually learned the pattern, not just the answer sheet.

Field Notes — new terms in this scene

Check Yourself — Quiz 1

In Tom Mitchell's definition, if Maya's system gets more accurate at predicting guilt the more solved case files she feeds it, which part of E-T-P is "the archive of solved case files" playing?

The archive is the past data the system learns from — that's Experience. The task is what it's trying to do (flag guilt), and the performance measure is how we score it (accuracy of the flags).

03

Case Type 1: Sorting Suspects

Turning a pile of evidence into a probability, and a probability into a verdict.

Maya's first real case: a suspect walks in. Based on everything she knows about them, she has to answer one of a small, fixed set of questions — guilty, or not guilty? Not "how guilty on a scale of 1 to 100," just a label from a short list. Sorting something into one of a set of known categories is called classification.

Everyday classification tasks: spam vs. not-spam, cat vs. dog vs. neither, positive vs. negative review.

From a raw evidence score to a clean probability

Maya starts the way she will for regression a little later: she combines the evidence into a single raw score using weights and a baseline.

z = w · x + b
  • xthe evidence — for example, a combined "suspicious activity" reading.
  • whow heavily that evidence should count.
  • ba baseline nudge, before any evidence is even considered.
  • zthe raw score — could come out as anything: −40, 6, 150. Not yet a "probability of guilt."

Rehan's confused by that raw score: "z = 150? What does that even mean?" It doesn't mean anything on its own — Maya needs to squish it onto a sensible 0-to-1 scale. For that she uses the sigmoid function.

p = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)
  • eEuler's number, a fixed constant (≈ 2.718) that produces a smooth, S-shaped curve when used this way — recognise it as "the constant behind the squishing curve."
  • σ(z)read "sigma of z" — a machine that takes in any number, however large or small, and always returns something between 0 and 1.
  • pthe estimated probability that the suspect is guilty, given the evidence — safely between 0 (definitely not guilty) and 1 (definitely guilty), whatever the raw score z was.

Think of the sigmoid as a pressure valve: however hard you push the raw score up or down, the output can never blow past 0 or 1. Once there's a probability, a threshold τ — commonly 0.5 — is the line Maya has to cross before she acts:

if p ≥ τ → "guilty" · otherwise → "not guilty"

Move where she sets that needle, and she changes how cautious or aggressive the whole system is. That's a knob to tune, not a fixed law of nature — a decision completely separate from how the probability itself was calculated.

Check Yourself — Quiz 2

Maya's system outputs p = 0.72 for a new suspect, and her threshold is τ = 0.5. What does her system label the suspect?

Since the estimated probability (0.72) meets or exceeds the threshold (0.5), the decision rule assigns "guilty." Raise the threshold to 0.8, and this same suspect would flip to "not guilty" — the evidence hasn't changed, only how cautious the system has been told to be.

04

Case Type 2: Estimating a Number

Fitting a line through the clues, and letting gradient descent find it automatically.

Maya's next suspect ran from the scene. She's not asking "guilty or not" this time — she already knows that. What she needs now is how many minutes ago they left, based only on how far the trail has cooled and how far the footprints are spaced apart. That's not a category — it's a number that could be 2 minutes, or 14.5, or 31.2. When the thing being predicted is a continuous number instead of a label from a short list, that's called regression.

Everyday regression tasks: predicting a house's price, tomorrow's temperature, how long a delivery will take.

Fitting a line through the clues

Maya plots her old, solved cases on a graph: distance of the footprints from the scene, in metres, on one axis; confirmed escape time, in minutes, on the other. She draws the straight line that best follows that cloud of points, then uses it to predict escape time for a brand-new, unsolved case.

ŷ = w · x + b
  • xthe input, what we already know: distance from the scene, in metres.
  • ŷthe prediction: estimated escape time, in minutes.
  • wthe weight (slope) — how many extra minutes of escape time predicted for every extra metre of distance. A bigger w means distance matters a lot; near zero, it barely matters.
  • bthe bias (intercept) — the predicted escape time when distance is 0, Maya's baseline guess before distance is even factored in.

To find the best line, Maya compares each guess to what she actually confirmed in that old case, across all of her n old cases at once.

MSE = (1/n) · Σi=1n (yi − ŷi)²
  • yithe actual, confirmed escape time for old case i.
  • ŷiwhat the line would have predicted for that same case.
  • nthe number of old, solved cases Maya is checking her line against.
  • MSEMean Squared Error — the average, squared distance between the line's guesses and reality across all cases, in one number. Squaring makes big misses count extra and stops positive and negative errors from cancelling out. Smaller MSE means a better fit.

◆ The metaphor — a foggy hillside

Think of w and b as two dials on Maya's desk: b shifts the whole line up or down, w tilts it. Fitting a line means turning both dials until the MSE is as small as it possibly can be. Trying every combination by hand could take forever, so instead the system uses gradient descent.

Picture Maya standing on a foggy hillside, where the height of the ground beneath her feet represents how large the MSE would be for that particular pair of dial settings. She can't see the valley floor through the fog, but she can feel which direction slopes downward from where she's standing. So she takes one small step in that downhill direction, checks again, takes another step, and repeats — moving w and b a little each time — until the ground stops sloping downward. That's the bottom of the valley: the dial settings with the lowest possible error.

How big a step she takes each time is the learning rate — too large, and she might stride right over the valley floor and climb the other side; too small, and she'll take forever to get there. When she finally stops finding a downhill direction, the process has converged.

Check Yourself — Quiz 3

Maya's line is ŷ = 0.55·x + 1.2. For a suspect whose footprints are x = 20 metres from the scene, what does the line predict for escape time, and what does the 1.2 represent?

ŷ = 0.55(20) + 1.2 = 12.2 minutes. The slope (0.55) says the prediction grows by 0.55 minutes for every extra metre; the intercept (1.2) is where the line starts before distance is factored in at all.

05

Two Case Types, One Family

What makes classification and regression both "supervised."

Step back for a second. In both of Maya's cases so far, she had something crucial: old case files where she already knew the true answer — confirmed guilt, and confirmed escape time. In ML language, each old case is a pair: the features — the evidence Maya feeds in — and the label — the true answer she already knows. She used that known-answer archive to build both systems. That's what makes both of these supervised learning: a human, or history, has already supplied the correct label for every example in the training archive, and the system's job is to learn the pattern connecting features to label.

Classification vs. regression
ClassificationRegression
What it predicts A label from a fixed set — e.g. guilty / not guilty A continuous number — e.g. 12.2 minutes
Example from today Guilt decision from the probability p Escape time from footprint distance
The "label" in training data A category A number
Raw score → prediction Squished through the sigmoid, then a threshold Used directly, no squishing needed
Shared trait Both are supervised — trained on cases where the true label was already known.

So "classification or regression?" is really just one question: is the label being predicted a category, or a number? In the classification example used here, logistic regression uses a sigmoid to convert a raw score into a probability. Classification itself is broader — other models make classification decisions without using a sigmoid at all.

Field Notes — new terms in this scene

Check Yourself — Quiz 4

Maya wants to predict which of three known gangs a break-in matches, based on tools left behind. Is this classification or regression, and why?

The output is a label chosen from a fixed, known set of categories — the three specific gangs — not a continuous number. The number of possible categories (2, 3, or more) doesn't change that it's still classification.

06

The Unlabeled Evidence Pile

Finding structure in evidence when nobody has told the system the correct answer.

Here's Maya's final twist. Forensics hands her a box of 40 anonymous suspect profiles pulled from old surveillance — height, age estimate, and a "nighttime activity score" for each. There's no case file attached to any of them. No one has confirmed which ones belong to the same crew. She has evidence with no known label at all — nothing to supervise the learning with.

"So what does the model even learn from, if nobody's told it the right answer?" Rehan asks. Nothing tells it the right answer, Maya says — it has to invent its own notion of "right" by noticing which profiles look alike.

Finding groups in data with no known labels is called clustering, and the broader family it belongs to is unsupervised learning — learning structure from data when nobody has told the system the "correct" answer in advance.

Similarity, groups, and their meeting points

To group profiles, Maya first needs a way to measure how close two suspect profiles are, using their features as coordinates on a graph.

d(A, B) = √((a1 − b1)² + (a2 − b2)²)
  • Aone suspect's profile as a point: a₁ = their height, a₂ = their nighttime activity score.
  • Ba second suspect's profile, the same way.
  • d(A,B)the distance between the two profiles — small means very alike on paper, large means very different.

This distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem wearing a detective's coat — the same one from coordinate geometry, except the "coordinates" are pieces of evidence instead of map positions.

Maya decides in advance how many crews she's looking for — call that number K — and picks K starting points to act as tentative "meeting spots." Then she repeats a simple two-step routine:

  1. Assign: put every profile into the group of whichever meeting spot it's closest to, using the distance formula above.
  2. Update: move each meeting spot to the average position of the profiles now assigned to it.
ck = (1/nk) · Σ profilei for i in group k
  • ckthe centroid of group k — literally its meeting point: the average height and average nighttime-activity score of everyone currently assigned to that group.
  • nkhow many profiles are currently in group k.

Maya repeats Assign, then Update, then Assign again — the centroids drift a little each round — until a round arrives where nobody switches groups anymore. That's convergence again, the same idea from regression, just for groups instead of a line. This whole assign-then-update loop is called k-means clustering — "k" for however many groups you told it to look for, "means" because each group's centre is just the average, the mean, of its members. Maya never tells the system which profile belongs to which crew; she only tells it how many crews to look for, and lets the averaging sort out the rest.

CASE CLOSED

What We Learned

  • Machine learning vs. classical programming — instead of a human writing rules by hand, ML learns the decision-making pattern directly from past examples (experience), producing a model.
  • Tom Mitchell's E-T-P framework, plus generalization and overfitting — a model is only useful if it works on new cases, not just the ones it memorised, which is why it's tested on held-back data.
  • Classification — predicts a label from a fixed set of categories, by turning evidence into a raw score, squishing it through the sigmoid function into a probability, and comparing it to a threshold.
  • Regression — predicts a continuous number by fitting a line (ŷ = wx + b) that minimises mean squared error, with gradient descent doing the automatic dial-turning, step by step, until it converges.
  • Supervised learning — the umbrella for both classification and regression, since both are trained on features paired with a known label.
  • Clustering — groups similar data points using a distance measure, iteratively assigning points to the nearest centroid and recomputing it, for a chosen number of groups K — this routine is k-means.
  • Unsupervised learning — the umbrella for clustering and similar techniques, used when no labels exist in the data at all.
  • The core question to remember — is the answer being predicted a category (classification), a number (regression), or is there no known answer at all, so the task is just finding structure (clustering / unsupervised)?

When Maya had old cases with known labels and needed a category, she used classification — squishing a raw score through the sigmoid and comparing it to a threshold. When she needed a number, she used regression — fitting a line by minimising mean squared error, with gradient descent doing the dial-turning for her. Both of those together are supervised learning, because the truth was already sitting in her archive. And when she had a pile of evidence with no labels attached at all, she measured distance, let similar profiles cluster around shared centroids, and repeated the loop until it converged — clustering, under the umbrella of unsupervised learning.

Every ML system you'll ever build starts the same way Maya's did tonight: what's the task, what experience will it learn from, how will you measure whether it's actually improving — and is what you're predicting a category, a number, or a group nobody's labelled yet?
