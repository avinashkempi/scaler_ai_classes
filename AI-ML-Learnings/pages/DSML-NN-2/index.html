Multi-Class Classification: Teaching a Machine to Choose Among Several Options

Picture a small recycling facility that has just installed a robotic sorting arm — GreenSort. Every item on the conveyor belt has to land in one of three bins: Paper, Plastic, or Metal.

GreenSort doesn't see the item directly. A sensor pod above the belt reads two numbers for every item: its weight, $x_{i1}$, and a reflectivity score between 0 and 1, $x_{i2}$. Our job in this post-read is to design and train the small neural network inside GreenSort's controller — one that turns those two numbers into a confident, single choice of bin. We'll keep returning to GreenSort as each new idea appears, so the math always has a concrete home to live in.

Section 1

From one decision to many

GreenSort's engineers already know how to build a classifier that makes a single yes/no decision — that's Logistic Regression: one neuron, one output, and a sigmoid function squashing that output into a value between 0 and 1, read as a probability.

Can a single Logistic Regression neuron decide between Paper, Plastic, and Metal directly?

No. A single neuron with a sigmoid output can only answer a yes/no style question, such as "is this item Plastic or not." It cannot, by itself, choose among three mutually exclusive categories.

So how do we modify the approach?

Instead of training one neuron to answer one yes/no question, we train one neuron per category. Each neuron becomes a specialist that produces a raw score for its own category, based on the same two sensor readings — three specialists, one per bin, working side by side.

A model built out of multiple neurons working together like this is called a Neural Network (NN). Even this small three-neuron setup, with no hidden layers, already qualifies as one.

A shared notation

We now have a name for every piece of GreenSort's three-neuron setup, and we know how to compute one $z_j$ at a time, one item at a time. But GreenSort is meant to sort hundreds of items a minute, each with its own $x_{i1}$ and $x_{i2}$. Writing three separate equations and repeating that for every item on the belt won't scale. That is precisely the problem that pushes us toward the next step: packing this entire computation into matrices, so that all items and all neurons are handled in one shot.

Section 2

Packing the network into matrices

Writing $z_j = w_{1j} \cdot x_{i1} + w_{2j} \cdot x_{i2} + b_j$ separately for every neuron and every item gets unwieldy fast once GreenSort is processing hundreds of items a minute. We vectorize the computation instead.

All the weights are arranged into a weight matrix $W$, of shape $d \times n$, where $d$ is the number of input features and $n$ is the number of output neurons (here, $n=3$, one per bin). Column $j$ of $W$ holds all the weights feeding into neuron $j$. Think of $W$ as a control panel: $d$ input dials on one side, $n$ output readouts on the other, every dial connected to every readout through its own adjustable knob — exactly one weight.

With this setup, the entire batch of raw scores for all $m$ items and all $n$ neurons is computed in a single matrix operation:

$$Z = XW + b$$

  • $X$ has shape $m \times d$: $m$ items, $d$ features each.
  • $W$ has shape $d \times n$: $d$ input features feeding into $n$ neurons.
  • $b$ has shape $1 \times n$, one bias per neuron, broadcast across every row.
  • $Z$ has shape $m \times n$: one raw score per item, per neuron.

Try it — matrix shape calculator

This equation isn't specific to GreenSort's three bins — it's the same core computation sitting inside every layer of most modern neural networks, just with far larger values of $d$ and $n$.

But a raw score is still just a plain real number: it could be 3.2, or −1.7, or 0. Nothing about it yet forces GreenSort to make one clear, consistent choice among the three bins, and nothing stops all three scores from looking equally plausible, or equally implausible. Producing a raw number is not the same as making a decision. Turning three unconstrained numbers into one confident, consistent choice is exactly what we solve next.

Section 3

From raw scores to a fair vote

The natural instinct is to squash each raw score independently with a sigmoid function, the way we did for a single yes/no decision.

What goes wrong if we apply sigmoid independently to each $z_j$?

Suppose GreenSort's three sigmoid outputs came out as 0.7, 0.6, and 0.1. Two of these are above 0.5 — does that mean the item is both Paper and Plastic at once? That can't be right, since every item goes into exactly one bin. Independent sigmoids also don't guarantee the three numbers add up to 1, so they can't be read as a single probability distribution over three exclusive outcomes.

What's needed is a function that takes in $z_1, z_2, z_3$ and returns three numbers that are all non-negative and sum to exactly 1. That function is called softmax.

$$p_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n} e^{z_k}}$$

Here $p_j$ is the probability assigned to class $j$, $z_j$ is that class's raw score, and the denominator sums $e^{z_k}$ over all $n$ classes so the results add up to 1.

Why raise $z$ to the power of $e$, rather than simply dividing each $z_j$ by the sum of all the $z$ values?

Raw scores can be negative, since they come from a plain linear combination of inputs and weights — and a probability can't be negative, so every value first needs to become positive. Exponentiating does exactly that: $e^{z}$ is always positive, however negative $z$ is.

It has a useful side effect, too: think of it as a confidence amplifier. If GreenSort's Metal neuron produces a noticeably larger raw score than the other two, exponentiation stretches that gap further, so Metal's probability grows disproportionately more — giving the network a way to express strong confidence rather than only a mild preference.

Once every score is positive, dividing each by the total simply rescales the three numbers so they add up to exactly 1 — turning raw confidence scores into a genuine probability distribution across the three bins.

Go further

Softmax has a close cousin in physics: the Boltzmann distribution, which describes the probability of a system occupying different energy states. Ask an AI assistant to walk through that connection, and work out for yourself what the raw score $z$ corresponds to in that physical analogy.

GreenSort can now turn its raw scores into three confident-looking probabilities — say 0.7 for Plastic, 0.2 for Paper, 0.1 for Metal. But right now, $W$ and $b$ are still just the small random numbers the network started with. There's no reason yet to believe these particular probabilities are any good, and no way to know whether this specific guess was right or badly wrong. Producing a probability is not the same as learning. Before GreenSort's weights can improve, one more ingredient is needed: a precise, numerical way to score exactly how wrong a given guess was.

Section 4

Measuring how wrong GreenSort was

What loss function did Logistic Regression use for a single yes/no decision?

Log loss, defined for a single item as:

$$\text{Log-loss}_i = -\big[y_i \cdot \log(\hat{y}_i) + (1 - y_i) \cdot \log(1 - \hat{y}_i)\big]$$

where $y_i$ is the true label (0 or 1) and $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted probability of the positive class.

Can this same log loss be reused directly for a three-bin problem?

No. Log loss is written specifically for two outcomes, since $\hat{y}_i$ represents the probability of one class, with the other inferred as $1-\hat{y}_i$. With three bins, a loss that handles all $n$ classes at once is needed.

Building categorical cross-entropy

First, the true bin of an item needs a numeric representation. We use one-hot encoding: if item $i$ truly belongs to class $j$, then $y_{ij}=1$ and $y_{ik}=0$ for every other class $k$. An item that's truly Plastic (class 2 of Paper, Plastic, Metal) gets $y_i = [0,1,0]$.

Categorical Cross-Entropy for item $i$, across $k$ classes, is then:

$$CE_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{k} y_{ij}\log(p_{ij})$$

where $p_{ij}$ is softmax's predicted probability that item $i$ belongs to class $j$.

Because of one-hot encoding, every term vanishes except the true class's term, so the formula quietly collapses to:

$$CE_i = -\log(p_{i,\text{true class}})$$

Only the probability assigned to the correct bin matters — and it's penalized for being small. If GreenSort was confident and correct, $p$ is close to 1 and $-\log(p)$ is close to 0: a tiny penalty. If GreenSort was confident but wrong, $p$ for the true class is close to 0 and $-\log(p)$ grows very large: a steep penalty.

With exactly $k=2$ classes, the formula becomes $CE_i = -[y_{i1}\log(p_{i1}) + y_{i2}\log(p_{i2})]$, which — once $y_{i2}=1-y_{i1}$ and $p_{i2}=1-p_{i1}$ are substituted — is algebraically identical to log loss. Log loss is, in fact, also known as Binary Cross-Entropy: the two-class special case of the more general formula above.

AI spotlight

From sorting recyclables to powering language models

A linear score $Z = XW+b$, followed by softmax, followed by Categorical Cross-Entropy, is not a toy setup invented only for a three-bin recycling problem. It is, almost unchanged, the standard final stage of nearly every modern classification neural network.

  • Image classification networks use it to choose among hundreds or thousands of object categories, instead of GreenSort's three bins.
  • Recommendation systems use it to choose among a large catalog of items to suggest next.
  • Large language models use it at every single step of generating text: instead of 3 output neurons, there is one neuron for every possible next word or sub-word token — often more than 100,000 of them. The network computes a raw score for every possible token, applies softmax to turn those scores into a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary, and is trained with the very same cross-entropy loss to make the probability of the actual next token as high as possible.

The scale is enormously larger, with many more layers involved before this final stage — but the mathematics is exactly the mathematics derived above: the shape of $Z$, why softmax needs an exponential, why cross-entropy collapses to $-\log(p)$ for the true class.

Go further

Compare the three-class softmax used for GreenSort to the vocabulary-sized softmax used at the output of a language model. Ask an AI assistant how the computation cost changes as the number of classes grows from 3 to over 100,000, and why techniques such as hierarchical softmax or sampling-based losses were developed to address that cost.

Section 5

Teaching GreenSort from its mistakes

At this point, GreenSort can compute a probability for every bin, and a single number — the loss $J$ — that says exactly how wrong that probability was for a given item. That's real progress, but on its own it only tells the engineers that GreenSort was wrong, not which weight caused the mistake, or by how much, or in which direction it should change. To actually improve GreenSort, one last piece is needed: an algorithm that takes this one loss value and works backward through the network to tell every individual weight and bias exactly how to change.

Training the network with gradient descent follows the same overall loop used for a single Logistic Regression neuron:

  1. Initialize the parameters $W$ and $b$, typically with small random values.
  2. Forward propagation — use the current $W$ and $b$ to compute $Z$, then softmax, to get predicted probabilities.
  3. Calculate the loss, using Categorical Cross-Entropy, to see how wrong those probabilities were.
  4. Backward propagation — figure out how much each individual weight and bias contributed to that loss.
  5. Update every weight and bias slightly, in the direction that reduces the loss.

Steps 2 through 5 repeat many times, with GreenSort's predictions improving a little more on every pass. It's the loop a chef uses to refine a recipe: taste the dish (forward propagation), decide how far it is from what was intended (loss), work out which ingredient caused the problem and by how much (backward propagation), adjust that ingredient's quantity slightly (update), and taste again.

Forward propagation, revisited

For a batch of items on the belt, forward propagation is simply:

  1. Compute the raw scores: $Z = XW+b$
  2. Apply softmax to $Z$, row by row, to get predicted probabilities $P$
  3. Compare $P$ against the true one-hot labels $Y$ using Categorical Cross-Entropy, to get the loss $J$

Forward propagation gets as far as one number, $J$. But $J$ alone doesn't say how to change $W$ or $b$. Since $W$ and $b$ sit at the very start of this computational graph and $J$ sits at the very end, what's needed is a way to relate a change at the start to its effect at the end — that is exactly what backward propagation does: it traces this same graph in reverse.

The computational graph — forward and backward

Forward pass: each stage feeds the next, left to right, ending in the single loss value $J$.

Backward pass: the chain rule traces the same graph in reverse, starting from $\partial J/\partial P$ and multiplying link by link back to $\partial z/\partial w$.

Backward propagation: assigning blame

How did we reduce the loss in Logistic Regression?

By computing the partial derivative of the loss $J$ with respect to each weight and bias — how much a small change in that parameter would change $J$ — then updating every parameter a small step in the direction that decreases $J$.

For GreenSort's network, $J$ doesn't depend on a weight $w$ directly. It depends on $w$ only through a chain: $w$ affects $z$, $z$ affects $p$ (through softmax), and $p$ affects $J$ (through cross-entropy) — the same computational graph above, read in reverse.

Think of a relay race with three runners, where only the final finishing time is announced. To know how much the first runner's speed affected that final time, the finish line alone won't tell you — the trace has to go backward through every handoff: how the first runner's speed affected the second runner's start, how that affected the third runner's start, and finally how that affected the finishing time. Backward propagation performs exactly this kind of backward tracing, using the chain rule from calculus.

Formally, for the computational graph $X, W, b \to Z \to P \to J$, the chain rule gives:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}$$

Reading from right to left: $\partial z/\partial w$ says how a change in a weight changes the raw score $z$; $\partial p/\partial z$ says how that change in $z$ changes the softmax probability $p$; and $\partial J/\partial p$ says how that change in $p$ changes the final loss $J$. Multiplying these three effects together, link by link, gives exactly how much a small nudge to $w$ ultimately moves the loss $J$ — precisely the information gradient descent needs to update $w$ in the right direction.

Wrap-up

Where this leaves GreenSort

Over this post-read, GreenSort's sorting arm went from being unable to make a three-way decision at all, to having a fully specified, trainable neural network.

The same five-step loop now understood for GreenSort's three bins is, at its core, the loop used to train some of the largest neural networks in existence today — just repeated many more times, over many more layers, with many more classes.
