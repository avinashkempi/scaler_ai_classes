Classification metrics

Beyond accuracy: precision, recall, thresholds, and the cost of being wrong

Once a model has spat out a predicted label, how do you actually decide if it's any good? By the end of this, "99% accurate" should make you suspicious rather than impressed.

The setup

NovaPay's fraud problem

Meera is a data scientist at NovaPay, an online payments company. She built a logistic regression model that scores every transaction with a probability of fraud, and at the standard 0.5 cutoff, that probability becomes a decision: fraud or legit.

She walks into a meeting and announces her model is 99.4% accurate. Rohan, who leads the risk team, isn't impressed.

"Out of everything you flagged as fraud last week, how many were actually fraud? And out of everything that was actually fraud, how many did you catch?"

Meera doesn't have an answer. That's the whole story, really: accuracy is one number, and a classifier's mistakes are a much richer story than one number can hold. Everything below is about unpacking what happens after a model turns a probability into a decision.

Why one number isn't enough

Accuracy's blind spot

NovaPay sees about 10,000 transactions a day. Roughly 100 are actually fraud — rare, and exactly the kind of rare that's dangerous to ignore.

AccuracyCorrect predictions ÷ Total predictions

Now picture a lazy model that predicts "legit" for every single transaction, without looking at anything. Since only 1% of transactions are fraud, it's still right 99% of the time — while catching zero fraud, ever.

A model that never flags fraud

99.0%
accuracy — but 0 of 100 fraud cases caught

Meera's actual model, at the 0.5 cutoff

99.68%
accuracy — and 68 of 100 fraud cases caught

Both numbers look almost identical. One of these models is useless. That's the danger: a single aggregate number hides what kind of mistakes a model makes. On any imbalanced problem — fraud, medical diagnosis, rare-defect detection — the majority class can quietly inflate accuracy while the model fails at the one job it exists to do.

Every mistake, sorted

The confusion matrix

Accuracy collapses everything into "right or wrong." Meera needs more granularity — a missed fraud and a false alarm are not equally costly to NovaPay, so she needs to know exactly which mistake her model is making.

Two directions on the same matrix

Precision & recall

Rohan's two questions map directly onto two metrics — and they look at the exact same confusion matrix from opposite directions.

Precision — "of everything you flagged, how many were real?"

PrecisionTP ÷ (TP + FP)

The denominator is everything the model called fraud, right or wrong. Precision asks: of all the flags raised, what fraction were justified?

Analogy A factory QC inspector pulls aside anything that looks defective. Precision is the fraction of pulled items that are genuinely defective. A low-precision inspector creates a pile of needless rework — exactly the cost NovaPay pays every time a real customer gets wrongly blocked.

At the 0.5 cutoff, NovaPay's model has TP = 68 and FP = 0, so Precision = 1.000 — every flag raised was correct.

Quick check

NovaPay's risk team is worried too many genuine customers are being wrongly blocked. Which metric should Meera prioritize?

Recall — "of everything that was real, how much did you catch?"

RecallTP ÷ (TP + FN)
Analogy Casting a net into a pond — Recall is the fraction of all the fish actually in the pond that the net caught. A low-recall net lets most of them swim straight past — exactly the cost NovaPay pays every time real fraud goes undetected.

At the 0.5 cutoff, TP = 68 and FN = 32, so Recall = 0.680 — 68% of real fraud gets caught, meaning 32% slips through.

Quick check

Leadership decides that losing money to undetected fraud hurts the business far more than occasionally inconveniencing a genuine customer. Which metric should Meera prioritize?

Why not just report accuracy every time? Accuracy is fine when classes are roughly balanced and every mistake costs about the same. It breaks down under class imbalance, because a model can score well by just favoring the majority class — and it breaks down again whenever False Positives and False Negatives carry different real-world costs, since accuracy treats them as identical.
The same trade-off, everywhere

Where this shows up beyond fraud

This tension between catching everything and not crying wolf isn't unique to NovaPay. Whenever you hear an AI system was "made stricter" or "made more lenient," there's a precision–recall trade-off — and a confusion matrix — sitting quietly behind that decision.

Content moderation needs high Recall — catch as much harmful content as possible. But push Recall too hard and Precision suffers: too little of it means wrongly taking down harmless posts.

Spam filters need high Precision. One legitimate email wrongly binned is often worse than one spam email slipping through — so the trade-off leans the opposite way from content moderation.

AI guardrail classifiers, which screen an assistant's own outputs, face the same tension: too strict and the assistant refuses reasonable requests; too lenient and unsafe content gets through.

A retriever pulling context for an LLM is scored with Precision@k and Recall@k. Precision@k asks how many of the top-k retrieved chunks are actually relevant; Recall@k asks how many of the truly relevant chunks got retrieved at all. Feed an LLM low-precision context and it confidently reasons over irrelevant material; feed it low-recall context and it answers confidently while missing the one chunk that mattered.

Turning the dial

The threshold controls the trade-off

Meera's model doesn't output "fraud" or "legit" directly — it outputs a probability, and a threshold converts that probability into a class. Lower the threshold and more transactions get flagged: Recall goes up, Precision goes down. Raise it, and only the most obvious cases get flagged: Precision improves, Recall falls. There's no threshold that maximizes both — it's a dial, not a switch, and where NovaPay sets that dial is a business call, not a purely mathematical one.

One number, honestly combined

The F1 score

Precision and Recall each tell half the story, and reporting both is often exactly what a business needs. But sometimes you want one number — to quickly rank several models, say. The instinct is to average them, and that instinct is wrong.

A plain average lets Precision = 1.0 and Recall = 0.01 average out to roughly 0.5 — making a nearly useless model look decent.

Analogy Drive to a friend's house at 30 km/h, then drive back the same distance at 60 km/h. Your average speed isn't 45 km/h — you spent more time at the slower speed, so it's closer to 40. That's the harmonic mean, and it always leans toward the smaller number. F1 uses exactly this trick.
F1 score2 × (Precision × Recall) ÷ (Precision + Recall)

Because of the harmonic mean, F1 can only be high when both Precision and Recall are decent — which is why it's such a popular single-number summary on imbalanced data.

Plain average vs. harmonic mean, side by side

Precision = 1.0, Recall = 0.01 — a model that's exact whenever it fires, but almost never fires:

Quick check

A colleague's dashboard reports F1 = 0.5 for a model with Precision = 1.0 and Recall = 0.01. What did they most likely get wrong?

A common misconception People assume "combining two metrics" always means averaging them. The harmonic mean is a deliberate choice — it punishes imbalance between the two inputs, which is exactly the point.
From words to rupees

The cost of a mistake

So far the trade-off has been words: lower the threshold, Recall↑ Precision↓. But NovaPay's leadership doesn't think in Precision and Recall — they think in rupees. Attach a real cost to each mistake and let that pick the threshold, instead of defaulting to 0.5 just because it's the midpoint.

At threshold 0.30, total cost bottoms out around ₹27,500. At the default 0.5, it's roughly ₹1,28,000 — nearly five times as expensive, simply because 0.5 was never chosen with real costs in mind.

Wait — is "best F1" the same as "cheapest"?

There are now two ways to pick a threshold: maximize F1, or minimize business cost.

F1-optimal vs. cost-optimal threshold
Same data, two different "best" answers
F1 score Total cost (₹)

F1 treats Precision and Recall as equally important, because the harmonic mean is symmetric. But NovaPay's real costs are not symmetric — a False Negative is roughly 27× costlier than a False Positive. F1 has no idea about rupees; it's a shape-of-the-trade-off metric, not a business-cost metric. Maximizing it doesn't mean minimizing what NovaPay actually loses.

Key takeaway Accuracy can lie by hiding a majority class. This is the more dangerous version: F1 doesn't hide anything — it's computed correctly — and it can still point you at the wrong threshold if you don't know your actual cost structure. A clean metric optimized for the wrong objective is still the wrong objective.

In practice: one function call

Everything above was built from first principles on purpose — that's how the formulas stick. On the job, scikit-learn computes all of it in one line, and the numbers match exactly what you'd get by hand:

from sklearn.metrics import classification_report

print(classification_report(actual, y_pred, target_names=['Legit', 'Fraud']))

One call returns Precision, Recall, and F1, broken down per class — the summary you'll reach for most often in practice.

Precision & recall at scale

Macro vs. micro averaging

Meera's model actually runs across several payment channels — Cards, UPI, and Wallets — and they don't carry equal volume. Cards is the vast majority; Wallets is small and newer. When leadership asks "what's our overall Precision?", there are two valid answers once the data is split.

Macro-average

Compute Precision separately per channel, then plain-average those numbers. Every channel counts equally, regardless of size. Answers: how well do we do on a typical channel?

Micro-average

Pool every channel's TP/FP/FN into one matrix first, then compute. A channel with more volume naturally carries more weight. Answers: how well do we do on a typical transaction?

Neither is "more correct" — they answer different questions, which is why both are usually reported together. This is the same idea behind scikit-learn's average='macro' vs average='micro' parameters for genuine multi-class problems.

Wallets is a small channel with weak Precision (0.333). Macro-average treats all three channels equally, so that one weak channel pulls the macro number (0.552) noticeably below the strong channels. Micro-average pools every transaction first, so Cards — roughly 70% of volume — dominates, and the micro number (0.655) sits close to Cards' own number.

Same imbalance lesson as the very start of this piece, one level up: just as a majority class can quietly inflate Accuracy, a majority segment can quietly dominate a Micro-average. Report only Micro, and NovaPay's leadership might never learn Wallets is struggling.

Putting it into words

Interview rapid-fire

Three questions that come up often, with the difference between an answer that sounds right and one that actually is.

Weak: "When Recall matters more." — circular, says nothing.

Strong: "When a False Negative is more expensive than a False Positive — missed cancer diagnosis, missed fraud, missed security threat. You accept more false alarms to avoid missing the costly case."

Weak: "Because that's the formula."

Strong: "A plain average lets a very low score in one metric hide behind a high score in the other. The harmonic mean is pulled toward the smaller value, so F1 can only be high when both Precision and Recall are genuinely decent."

Weak: "Yes, F1 is a strong overall metric."

Strong: "Not necessarily — F1 assumes equal cost for both error types, which is rarely true in a real business. I'd want the actual cost of a False Positive vs. a False Negative before trusting any single threshold, F1-optimal or not."
