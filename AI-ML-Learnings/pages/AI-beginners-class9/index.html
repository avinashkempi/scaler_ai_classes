Why This Isn't "Just Generate Something"

The beginner mistake this class corrects: treating "I need an image / video / voice" as one prompt into one box, then shipping whatever comes back because it looks finished. A fake-looking hero image erodes trust before the partnership even launches. A teaser that looks nothing like the real property sets an expectation the rooms won't meet. A voice that sounds robotic or inconsistent across languages undermines the "small, personal agency" feel that got Wanderline this deal — each section ahead shows a real, public example of exactly this failure.

Today's six tools cover three output types — but the real discipline is one rule for all of them: a generated frame, clip, or voice isn't evidence of anything real until you've deliberately decided whether it's allowed to claim to be.

Why this matters for you — 2026 job reality

  • "Multimodal content ops" is now its own line item on a content team — someone who moves between image, video and voice tools daily, not someone who only writes.
  • Every one of today's six tools shipped a genuinely new capability in the last few months. Employers assume the current version, not a 2024 explainer video.
  • The valuable skill is never "can you get a tool to generate a convincing image, clip or voice." It's knowing when a generated asset is decorative and when it's making a factual claim about something real — and never letting the two blur.
  • Disclosure and consent are now the professional differentiator, the same way the fact-check pass was last class — everyone can generate. Almost nobody has a habit of labelling or asking permission.
Insider fact — say this, it reframes the whole session: As of 2026, roughly 78% of marketing teams already use AI-generated video in some part of their workflow — that ship has sailed. Here's the gap that actually matters: independent testing finds only about 9-10% of viewers can reliably tell AI-generated video from real footage on sight — and yet 84-91% of consumers say they want AI use disclosed anyway when they find out. Everyone's using the tools. Almost nobody's telling anyone. That gap between "can generate" and "should disclose" is the entire second half of this class, and it's the part your resume can actually claim that a flashy demo reel can't.
Instructor — before you teach this live: All six tools shipped a real capability change recently. Ideogram 3.0 renders embedded text at 90-95% accuracy, which is why it's the pick for anything with real words on it. Pika 2.5 closed most of the temporal "flicker" gap and added Scene Extension, building a clip a few seconds at a time. Gemini's Nano Banana image family stays useful for quick edits. § 1

Framework — Frame, Motion, Voice & Prompt Craft

⏱ 13 min

One framework for the whole class, same trick as always. Six tools, three genuinely different kinds of output — and one question that sorts them.

💒
The One Analogy

Think about three specialists at a wedding. A photographer hunts for the single frame that freezes a moment. A videographer captures change over time — the walk down the aisle, the first dance. Someone recording the vows and toasts captures nothing visual at all, purely sound. All three produce "content" from the same event, but they're not interchangeable — which one you reach for depends on whether you need to freeze a moment, show something changing, or communicate through sound alone.

The One Question That Sorts All Three

"Does this need to freeze a single moment, show something changing over time, or communicate through sound with no picture at all?" Whichever answer fits tells you which family of tool to open. Ideogram, OpenArt, ChatGPT's image generator and Gemini's Nano Banana are built to solve the first. Pika is built to solve the second. Cartesia is built to solve the third. Most real jobs — like Tsering's teaser — need more than one, stitched together.

Beginner Note: You don't need art-school vocabulary to write a good image prompt. Answer the four S's in plain language and you're already ahead of "make it look nice" — which is exactly the kind of vague prompt that gets a generic, safe, forgettable result.
Intermediate Note: Real workflows almost never nail it in one giant prompt. Generate, look at what's actually wrong, add one more constraint, regenerate. Trying to cram every possible detail into prompt one is slower than three small, targeted passes.
Advanced Note: Notice Style and Setting/Light are doing the work Format did in Class 2's RCTFC — they're the constraints that stop the model from guessing. Subject is Context. § 2

Tool Demo — Ideogram & OpenArt

⏱ 20 min

Frame first. Kabir has exactly one usable photo — the hotel's exterior, taken on a flat, overcast afternoon — and a brief from Tsering that needs a hero image, an accurate text graphic, and a few "we're excited" concept shots by Monday.

IDEOGRAM 3.0

Best-in-class text rendering — embedded words render at roughly 90-95% accuracy, versus 30-40% for most competitors. Style Codes let you save a look once and reapply it for brand-consistent output later. Magic Fill (prompt-guided inpainting) and Extend (outpainting) live in its Canvas editor — exactly what image-to-image editing needs. Batch generation from a spreadsheet is gated to Pro and above.

OPENART

A full creative workspace built around a huge library of community and proprietary models rather than one single model. Image Variations takes an uploaded photo and a plain-language description of the change you want, and generates alternatives you can check directly against your own brief. Character Builder trains on a handful of reference images to keep one subject — a building, a person, a mascot — visually consistent across many later generations. Higher tiers fold in upscaling and basic video workflows alongside the image tools.

What changed — say this out loud: OpenArt has consolidated what used to be several separate tools — style browsing, image-to-image variations, character consistency, even light video workflows — into one credit-based workspace with a left-sidebar navigation. If your reference point for "AI image tool" is a single text-box generator, the multi-tool sidebar here is the adjustment.

Step-by-step demo (share screen)

Step 1 — Open ideogram.ai and, in the text box right on the home page, upload the real exterior photo and click Add as reference.

Step 2 — Click on the image, select Edit, and give it a description-guided restyle to add a golden-hour lighting pass.

Step 3 — Generate. Check every structural line — roofline, windows, signage — against the original before calling this "the real building, better lit."

Step 4 — Start another chat (remove the image from the text box this time).

Step 5 — Type Tsering's 2nd and 3rd briefs into that new chat, one at a time — the offer graphic, then the 2-3 mood/concept images. Keep the mood-image prompts clearly in "concept" territory; these never claim to be a specific room.

Step 6 — For the 2nd brief — the offer graphic — zoom in on every character before approving: this is the one graphic where a typo is the whole failure. Save the look as a Style Code once your brand palette lands correctly.

Step 7 — Open openart.ai, click Image in the left sidebar, then Image Variations, and upload the restyled hero. Describe the variation you want in plain language — a different angle, a slightly warmer sky — and generate. Check the result against your own description: does it actually match what you asked for? If not, refine the prompt and regenerate; if it's close, run one more polish/upscale pass before calling it final.

Step 8 — Run SIM 3 below before locking a final generator choice for each asset — the point isn't "pick a favourite tool," it's matching strength to job.

Step 9 — Label every deliverable before handing off to Tsering: edited-real-photo (safe to caption normally) versus fully-generated-concept (must carry a visible "concept render" note if it ever goes public) — run SIM 4 first.

🗞️ Real Case — Willy's Chocolate Experience, Glasgow, Feb 2024

This is exactly the failure Tsering's email is warning against, and it's real. An immersive "Willy's Chocolate Experience" event in Glasgow was promoted almost entirely using AI-generated images — lush chocolate rivers, a candy-cane forest, elaborate sets — sold as previews of what ticket-holders would see. Families showed up to a half-decorated warehouse with a few printed backdrops and some balloons. Police were called, the event was shut down mid-day, and the organiser had to issue refunds to everyone.

What failed wasn't image quality — the images were polished, which is exactly why people trusted them. It was using a generated image to represent a real space nobody could walk into yet, unlabelled. That's exactly why Tsering asked for a "concept" label up front.

Ideogram & OpenArt Danger — Free Tiers Vary More Than You'd Guess

Ideogram's Free plan gives 10 prompts a day, resetting daily — genuinely usable to learn the tool, thin for production. Paid plans start at $7/month; API access runs roughly $0.03 per image on the fast "Turbo" render up to $0.09 on "Quality." OpenArt's free plan gives 40 one-time trial credits — not monthly, spend them once and they're gone. Paid plans start at Essential, $14/month ($7/month billed annually, 4,000 credits/month), rising through Advanced ($29/month, 12,000 credits, adds upscaling and video workflows) and Infinite ($56/month, 24,000 credits) to the enterprise-grade Wonder plan ($240/month). Exact daily caps and credit costs shift with product updates — verify the live numbers before quoting a client anything free.

Tool Demo — Pika

⏱ 18 min

Motion. Tsering needs 30 seconds by Monday, in a channel where a static image gets scrolled past in under a second. No footage exists — so the clip has to be built, not filmed.

PIKA · PIKA 2.5

Image-to-video turns a still into motion — feed it the real, golden-hour hero and get a slow push-in grounded in an actual photo. Text-to-video handles the opposite case — a description alone, no source image needed, useful for purely atmospheric shots. The 2.5 model closed most of the old "flicker" problem and holds character and lighting steady from a reference image. Scene Extension builds a clip 3-5 seconds at a time, regenerating only the new portion — and a Pikatwist stylistic pass can restyle a whole clip's look in one instruction. Pikadditions, Pikaswaps and Pikaframes round out the object-level and keyframe editing tools.

Beginner Note: Google's Gemini also has a video model, Veo — capable, but paid-only with no free tier at all. This class sticks to Pika for hands-on video work, since it covers both image-to-video and text-to-video in one tool you can actually try for free.
What changed — say this out loud: Pika 2.5's jump in temporal consistency is genuinely new — the flicker and identity drift that plagued AI video a year ago is largely gone, and Scene Extension means a 5-second clip can grow into a 15-second one without restarting from scratch. That consistency is what makes a single tool credible for both the grounded hero shot and the purely atmospheric B-roll below — which is exactly why the disclosure habit from the last section matters even more here.

🗞️ Real Case — Coca-Cola's AI Holiday Ad, 2024

A different failure mode than Willy's Chocolate Experience, and useful precisely because nothing here was factually wrong. Coca-Cola released a fully AI-generated version of its classic holiday commercial — the trucks, the snow, the brand's own decades-old imagery, recreated with generative video. The backlash was immediate: viewers described the result as uncanny and hollow, and criticism focused on a beloved, emotionally warm ad being handed to a generator instead of the human crew who'd made the originals feel real for 30 years.

Nothing here was factually wrong — the damage was to trust in the brand's authenticity, the exact thing Wanderline is trading on as "a small agency, not an OTA." A technically accurate video can still cost you the warmth that won the partnership, which is why Tsering's brief keeps the AI clips to landscape and building, not anything meant to feel personal.

Pika Danger — A Free Tier With Real Limits

Pika's free plan actually renews monthly — 80 credits, good for roughly 3-6 short clips — but every export is capped at 480p, carries a visible watermark, and carries no commercial-use rights, so free output isn't something you'd hand a client regardless of credit balance. Standard is $8/month billed annually (~$10/month monthly) for watermark-free HD; Pro is $28/month annually (~$35/month monthly) for commercial rights and the full Pikaffects set including video-to-video; Fancy runs $76/month annually (~$95/month monthly) for the heaviest use.

Quiz 3 · Solitude Peaks' marketing lead asks Kabir to generate a video clip of "a packed pool, full of happy guests" for the launch post — the property doesn't have any guests booked under the new partnership yet, and Kabir has no such footage. What's the right call?

AGenerate it anyway — video generation is realistic enough now that nobody will be able to tell

Why B? That's a fabricated testimonial, not a mood shot, and no disclosure label fixes a claim that's simply untrue. A relies on generation quality outrunning scrutiny, which the "9-10% detection rate" stat from Section 1 might tempt you into — but undetectable is not the same as acceptable. C treats a factual fabrication as a labelling problem instead of a content problem. D confuses length with legitimacy.

Tool Demo — Cartesia

⏱ 15 min

Voice. The teaser needs narration in English and Hindi, in one consistent voice — and nobody at Wanderline has ever recorded either.

What Is Cartesia?

A text-to-speech and voice cloning platform built around low-latency generation and a stock voice library. Instant Clone, in the left sidebar, builds a usable voice clone from a short recording. Cartesia doesn't translate for you — turning a script into another language means pairing it with a separate text model first, then feeding the translated script back into Cartesia's Text-to-Speech, cloned voice and all.

🎙 Instructor Says

"Divya's the one who's briefed every guide crossing the Rajasthan circuit for two years — warm, plain-spoken, exactly the voice Class 8's Canva captions were trained to sound like. If any Wanderline voice should carry this teaser, hers is the obvious pick. And cloning it starts with her actually saying yes, on record, first — that recording is the consent."

Two scripts, ready to paste in — the narration itself, and the consent-and-sample recording Divya reads before anything gets cloned.

Step-by-step demo (share screen)

Step 2 — Paste in the narration script above and generate the English narration in that stock voice — a stopgap for speed, so Tsering has something to review same day.

🗞️ Real Case — Pikesville High School, Baltimore County, Jan 2024

The stakes version of "what happens without consent." An athletic director at a Maryland high school used AI voice-cloning tools to fabricate an audio clip that sounded exactly like the school's own principal making racist and antisemitic remarks about students and staff. The clip spread before anyone realised it was fabricated. The real principal faced death threats and had to take leave; a criminal investigation followed, and the athletic director was arrested and charged.

The contrast with Divya's clone is exact: consent, or its total absence, is the entire difference between a useful production tool and one used to destroy a real person's reputation. The technology is identical to Section 4's demo — the permission is the only variable.

Cartesia Danger — Confirm The Tier Before You Promise It

Cartesia's free tier and paid plans are credit- or character-metered, the same shape as every other tool in this class — but exactly which features sit behind the free tier versus a paid seat (Instant Clone access, character volume, commercial-use rights) is the kind of detail that shifts release to release. Open the live pricing page the morning you teach this and confirm the current numbers before quoting anyone a "free" deliverable — same discipline as every other tool today.

Quiz 4 · Wanderline's marketing intern wants to clone a popular travel vlogger's voice from their public YouTube videos, without asking, to narrate a promotional ad — reasoning that the videos are public, so the voice is fair game. What's the rule?

AIt's fine — anything posted publicly online can legally be used for anything, including cloning the speaker's voice
BNot without the vlogger's explicit consent — "publicly postable" and "consented to being cloned and used commercially" are different permissions, which is exactly why cloning someone's voice responsibly means getting that consent on record before cloning at all, the same discipline Divya's clone followed
CIt's fine as long as the final ad doesn't mention the vlogger by name
DIt's only a problem if the vlogger notices and complains publicly
Why B? Public visibility of a video says nothing about consent to clone the speaker's voice for a commercial ad — those are two separate permissions, and this is precisely the gap a recorded-consent step exists to close. A conflates "I can watch this" with "I can extract and reuse this person's identity." C removes the name but not the identity — the whole point of a cloned voice is that it's recognisably theirs. D treats consent as a PR risk to manage after the fact instead of a permission to secure before doing anything, which is exactly the Pikesville case's shape, at lower stakes.

Copyright, Briefly — What You're Actually Exposed To

Two ongoing lawsuits define the legal backdrop: Getty Images v. Stability AI (training an image generator on licensed photos without permission) and The New York Times v. OpenAI & Microsoft (the same claim, over text). Neither is resolved, and output ownership varies by jurisdiction and by each tool's own terms. The practical rule: avoid "in the style of [a named living artist]," never generate a real public figure's likeness without rights, and check the commercial-use terms for your specific plan tier — free and paid tiers on the same platform sometimes differ.

Key Takeaways

  1. Not every AI problem needs raw material. Last class's tools organised what already existed. Today's generate what doesn't exist yet — a different kind of responsibility.
  2. One question sorts all six tools: are you freezing a moment, showing something change, or communicating through sound alone?
  3. Frame, Motion, Voice. Ideogram/OpenArt for stills. Pika for motion. Cartesia for a specific, consented spoken identity.
  4. The 4 S's are RCTFC, aimed at pixels. Subject, Style, Setting/Light, Shot — the same discipline of specifying instead of hoping.
  5. Generated is not the same as real, and it must never be allowed to pretend to be. Willy's Chocolate Experience, the fake-pool video, and the unrenovated-suite tour are all the exact same mistake, at different scales.
  6. Consent is not optional once you're cloning identity. Divya's consented clone and the Pikesville principal's non-consented one use the identical technology — the permission is the entire difference.
  7. Only 9-10% of people can reliably spot AI video on sight, and 84-91% want it disclosed anyway. Undetectable is not the same as acceptable.
