Naive Bayes

A classification algorithm built entirely out of counting. Given nothing but Bayes' Theorem and one bold simplifying assumption, it turns the words inside a message into a confident guess: spam, or not spam.

Spam "I, Nigerian prince need your help. Send money"
Ham "Meeting scheduled for 5 pm. Kindly revert"
01 — The Use Case

Spotting spam by its words

Imagine you're a data scientist tasked with building a spam classifier. Two emails land in front of you. One is obviously spam, the other obviously isn't — but what actually gives it away?

Look at the two messages above again. Certain words act as strong signals: phrases like "Nigerian prince", "lottery", or "million dollars" show up disproportionately often in spam messages and rarely in genuine ones. Our brains pick up on this instantly. The challenge is teaching a machine to do the same — to turn the presence of certain words into a probability that a message is spam.

That's exactly what Naive Bayes does: it converts word evidence into a probability of spam versus not-spam, using a 200-year-old piece of probability theory as its engine.

What the data looks like

For this problem, the dataset consists of:

  • n text messages, each made of words
  • A class label for each message — spam (y = 1) or ham (y = 0, meaning "not spam")

Each message is really just a sequence of words:

texti = wi1, wi2, wi3, …, wid
d = the number of words in texti. Different messages can have entirely different lengths.

02 — The Mathematical Foundation

Where Bayes' Theorem comes from

The goal is simple to state: given a message, predict whether it's spam or not. To do that, we need two probabilities — and whichever is larger wins.

P(y=1 | text) — probability the message is spam
P(y=0 | text) — probability the message is ham

Both of these are conditional probabilities — the probability of a class, given evidence. That's exactly the shape of problem Bayes' Theorem was built for. Before applying it to spam, it helps to rebuild the theorem itself from a simpler example.

1 Set up two events

Suppose, out of 100 cricket matches:

Event A = India won
Event B = Virat scored a century
P(A) = 60/100 = 0.6
P(B) = 12/100 = 0.12
2 Ask for a conditional probability

What is P(A | B) — the probability India won, given Virat scored a century? Out of the 12 matches where he scored a century, India won 10 of them.

P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B) = 10/12
3 Use the multiplication rule both ways

Rearranging gives the intersection probability, and it can be computed from either direction:

P(A∩B) = P(A|B) × P(B) = 10/12 × 12/100 = 10/100
P(A∩B) = P(B|A) × P(A) = 10/60 × 60/100 = 10/100

Both routes land on the same number — 0.10 — because they're describing the same overlap between the two events.

4 Arrive at Bayes' Theorem

Since both expressions are equal:

P(A|B) × P(B) = P(B|A) × P(A)

Dividing both sides by P(B) gives the theorem:

P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)

03 — Applying It to Spam

From cricket to spam classification

Swap "India won" for "the email is spam," and "Virat scored a century" for "the email contains these words" — the same formula now classifies messages.

P(y=1|text) = P(text|y=1) × P(y=1) / P(text)
P(y=0|text) = P(text|y=0) × P(y=0) / P(text)

Both expressions share the same denominator, P(text). Since the only thing that matters is which probability is larger — not their exact values — the shared denominator can simply be dropped from the comparison.

P(y=1|text) ∝ P(text|y=1) × P(y=1)

Where do P(y=1) and P(y=0) come from?

These are the class priors — the plain probability that any given message belongs to spam or ham, before even looking at its words. They're estimated directly by counting the training data:

P(y=1) = (# training points where y=1) / (total training points)
P(y=0) = (# training points where y=0) / (total training points)
Key term — Class prior
The unconditional probability that a random message belongs to a given class, estimated purely from how frequently that class appears in the training data — before any word evidence is considered.

Priors are easy. The harder piece is the other factor: P(text | y=1) — the likelihood of seeing this exact combination of words, given the message is spam. Computing that directly turns out to be almost impossible, which is exactly the problem the "Naive" in Naive Bayes was invented to solve.

04 — The Naive Assumption

Why "naive"?

Computing the exact probability of an entire sequence of words is expensive and often meaningless. Naive Bayes sidesteps the problem with one bold simplification.

The problem with computing it directly

P(text | y=1) literally asks: "given the email is spam, what's the probability it contains word w1 and w2 and w3 … and wd, all at once?" To estimate this from data, you'd need to find spam messages in the training set that contain every single one of those words together — a vanishingly small, often empty group. The resulting probability would be tiny, computationally expensive to obtain, and practically useless.

The simplifying assumption

Naive Bayes assumes that, conditioned on the class, the occurrence of each word is independent of every other word:

P("Nigerian prince" | y=1) = P("Nigerian"|y=1) × P("prince"|y=1)

Instead of one impossible joint probability, this becomes a product of simple, individually-estimable pieces — one per word.

Why this is genuinely "naive"

The words in a message are not really independent — "Nigerian" and "prince" tend to show up together precisely because they're related. The assumption is a convenient fiction, not a claim about how language actually works. It's naive, but it's a reasonable trade to make in exchange for tractable computation — and in practice it works remarkably well.

Common interview mistake — what exactly is independent?
The independence being assumed is conditional on the class label, not marginal. The claim is not "these words are independent of each other in general" — it's "once you already know the message is spam (or ham), knowing one word tells you nothing extra about another." That distinction — conditional vs. marginal independence — is exactly where this gets misquoted.

Estimating a single word's likelihood

With the independence assumption in place, each word's likelihood becomes a simple ratio. This particular version — where a word either exists in a text or it doesn't — is called Bernoulli Naive Bayes:

P(w1|y=1) = n′1 / n1
n1 = total number of spam texts  •  n′1 = number of those spam texts containing the word w1

Putting the pieces together

Comparing P(y=1|text) against P(y=0|text), whichever is larger becomes the prediction. The full picture:

P(y=1|text) ∝ (class prior) × (product of word likelihoods)
TermMeaning
P(y=1), P(y=0)Class priors — probability a message belongs to spam or ham, ignoring its words
P(wi|y=1), P(wi|y=0)Likelihoods — probability of each word's occurrence, conditioned on the class

Bayes' Theorem contributes the structure; the naive independence assumption makes the likelihood term computable. Together, they give the algorithm its name: Naive Bayes.

05 — Laplace Smoothing

The problem of a word nobody has seen

The model above has a fatal flaw once it meets the real world: one unfamiliar word can destroy the entire prediction.

Consider a new query message with words x_q = {w1, w2, w6, w7, w′}, where w′ never appeared anywhere in the training vocabulary. At prediction time, Naive Bayes still tries to compute:

P(y=1|w1,w2,w6,w7,w′) = P(y=1) × P(w1|y=1) × P(w2|y=1) × … × P(w′|y=1)

Since w′ never occurred in any spam training example, its count is zero: nw′1 = 0, so:

P(w′|y=1) = nw′1 / n1 = 0 / n1 = 0

A single zero in that product collapses the entire probability to zero — and the same thing happens for P(y=0|…). With both class probabilities at zero, Naive Bayes has no way to decide. One unseen word breaks the whole model.

Two ways to fix it

Option 1 — Ignore the term

Simply set P(w′|y=1) = 1 for any unseen word, so it multiplies the product by 1 and has no effect. This works, but it throws away information rather than handling it gracefully.

Option 2 — Add a constant (Laplace / Additive Smoothing)

Add a small constant α to every word count, and a compensating constant Cα to the denominator, so the ratio can never land exactly on zero:

P(wj|y=1) = (nj1 + α) / (n1 + Cα)

Worked example

Suppose there are 100 spam messages in training (n1 = 100), and we use α = 1, C = 2. For a brand-new word w′ that never appeared in training:

P(w′|y=1) = (0 + 1) / (100 + 1×2) = 1/102 ≈ 0.0098

Small, but no longer zero — the smoothing prevents a single unseen word from breaking the whole prediction, without meaningfully distorting the math for everything else.

06 — Bias–Variance Tradeoff

How much smoothing is too much?

Alpha isn't free to set arbitrarily large. Push it too far in either direction, and the model breaks in a different way each time.

Take a word wj that occurs in 10 of the 100 spam training messages (nj1=10, n1=100, C=2). Compare a small α against an enormous one:

α = 1 → P(wj|y=1) = (10+1)/(100+2) = 11/102 ≈ 0.10
α = 10,000 → P(wj|y=1) = (10+10000)/(100+20000) ≈ 0.50

When α is huge compared to n1, it completely dominates the ratio — every word's likelihood gets dragged toward 0.5, regardless of how often it actually appeared. Since both P(y=1|…) and P(y=0|…) would end up multiplying by likelihoods near 0.5 for every word, those terms roughly cancel out in the comparison — leaving the prediction dependent on almost nothing but the class priors. That's underfitting: the model stops listening to the words at all.

Go too far the other way — α shrinking toward zero — and the smoothed likelihood collapses back toward the raw, un-smoothed ratio nj1/n1. That's overfitting: the model trusts the training counts too literally, including their noise.

07 — Beyond Presence/Absence

Multinomial Naive Bayes

Bernoulli Naive Bayes only asks: does the word appear, yes or no? It treats "lottery" mentioned once the same as "lottery" mentioned ten times — even though the second case is a much stronger spam signal. Multinomial Naive Bayes fixes that by counting.

Each word is a binary feature: wj = 1 if the word exists in the text, 0 if it doesn't. The likelihood only ever needs to be estimated for 2 possible values per word.

P(wj=0|y) or P(wj=1|y)

Each word is stored as a count of how many times it occurs in the text. If wj occurs 3 times, the likelihood needed is:

P(wj=3|y=1)
the probability that word wj occurs exactly 3 times, across spam samples

If a word can take values {1, 2, 3, …, k} across the dataset, the model must estimate a likelihood for each of those k−1 values — noticeably more computation than Bernoulli's 2-value case, but it preserves frequency information that Bernoulli throws away.

What both versions ultimately need

Whichever variant is used, computing P(y=1|w1,…,wd) and P(y=0|w1,…,wd) always comes down to the same two ingredients:

  1. Class priors: P(y=1) and P(y=0)
  2. Likelihoods of each of the d words — the probability of word wj occurring C times, given the class: P(wj=C|y=1) and P(wj=C|y=0)
08 — Case Study

Building the classifier end to end

Turning the theory into a working spam filter comes down to a few practical decisions.

Turning text into counts

A CountVectorizer converts each message into a row of word counts — one column per unique word seen across the dataset.

Question

Will this matrix be dense or sparse?

Across thousands of messages there may be thousands of unique words — thousands of columns — but any single message only ever uses a handful of them. Most entries end up at zero, producing a sparse matrix.

Question

Should CountVectorizer be trained on the entire dataset?

No — it's fit only on the training data. The resulting vocabulary and parameters are then used to transform the test data, so the test set never leaks into how the vocabulary was built.

Question

Does Naive Bayes need feature scaling?

No. Naive Bayes is not a distance-based algorithm — it's an information-based one. Scaling the features doesn't help it converge any faster, unlike distance-based methods such as k-NN.

Tuning alpha with cross-validation

Since the dataset is imbalanced, the model is evaluated with F1 score rather than plain accuracy. A grid search sweeps across candidate smoothing values:

alpha: [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]

Using a Multinomial Naive Bayes classifier with 3-fold cross-validation, the best cross-validated performance came at α = 1, with an F1 score of roughly 0.90.

09 — Wrapping Up

Key takeaways

Naive Bayes turns a hard probability problem into a product of easy ones — and despite its simplicity, holds up well in practice.

01

It's Bayes' Theorem, applied

Predicting spam vs. ham comes down to comparing two conditional probabilities, derived directly from Bayes' Theorem.

02

Independence makes it tractable

Assuming words are conditionally independent given the class turns one impossible joint probability into a simple product of per-word likelihoods.

03

Smoothing prevents collapse

Laplace Smoothing keeps a single unseen word from zeroing out the entire prediction — controlled by the hyperparameter α.

04

Alpha is a bias–variance dial

Too small and the model overfits training counts; too large and every likelihood converges to 0.5, leaving only the class priors.

05

Bernoulli vs. Multinomial

Bernoulli only tracks whether a word appears; Multinomial tracks how many times — at the cost of more computation.

06

It performs well in practice

On the spam dataset, Multinomial Naive Bayes reached a 0.92 test F1 score, even with imbalanced classes.
