Intro to Clustering & K-Means
Intro to Clustering & K-Means · Post-Read

Finding groups nobody labeled

How a retailer with millions of anonymous transactions learns who its customers actually are — and how the K-Means algorithm gets there, from first principles to a working segmentation engine.

The Business Problem

Millions of customers. Zero labels.

ClusterMart is a fast-growing e-commerce company with years of purchase history and no idea who its customers really are. Marketing wants to run personalized campaigns — treating Premium Customers differently from Discount Hunters — but no one has ever sorted customers into those groups. There is no "customer type" column anywhere in the database.

The CEO, day one

"We have lots of customer data, but we don't actually know who our customers really are. We want to target our Premium Customers differently from our Discount Hunters — but nobody has ever labeled our customers into these groups."

There are no categories to learn from — only raw attributes: how much each customer spends, how often they visit, how many items they buy. The task is to group similar customers together without being told what the groups are.

Why this is hard

People can eyeball two-dimensional data and roughly agree on clusters. But real ClusterMart data has dozens of features — spend, frequency, recency, category preference, return rate — far beyond what anyone can eyeball. The computer needs a defined rule, not a hunch.

Framing the Problem

Why supervised learning can't help here

In supervised learning, a model learns a mapping from inputs to known, correct outputs — it needs labeled examples to learn from. ClusterMart's customer data has no labels at all.

Core Concept

What clustering actually does

Clustering is the task of grouping data points so that points in the same group are more similar to each other than to points in other groups — using only the raw features, no labels required.

It shows up constantly:

Playlists

Grouping listeners with similar taste for recommendations.

Watch history

Grouping viewers with similar streaming behavior.

Photo albums

Grouping photos of the same person automatically.

Image compression

Grouping similar pixel colors into a smaller palette.

The intuition

Whatever grouping felt natural above was almost certainly based on closeness — points that sit near each other visually. That intuitive idea of closeness is exactly what clustering algorithms formalize mathematically. The next question: how do we measure "close" precisely?

Core Concept

Measuring similarity with distance

Every customer can be represented as a point in feature space — for example, (Annual Spend, Purchase Frequency). If two customers are similar, their points should sit close together in that space.

The standard way to measure closeness is Euclidean distance — the straight-line, "as the crow flies" distance between two points:

d(p, q)  =  √ (p1−q1)² + (p2−q2)² + ⋯ + (pn−qn)²

Core Concept

What makes a clustering good?

Two analysts each submit a customer segmentation from the same raw data. Which one is better — and how would you know?

Intra-cluster distance

How spread out points are within the same cluster. Smaller means tighter, more cohesive groups.

Inter-cluster distance

How far apart different clusters sit from each other. Larger means more distinct, separable groups.

A good clustering has

Small intra-cluster distance (points within a group sit close together) and large inter-cluster distance (different groups sit far apart). Analyst A's clustering is tight and well separated; Analyst B's is closer to random noise. Ideally, the resulting clusters should also make business sense — a shirt manufacturer wants small/medium/large, not a hundred micro-clusters.

The Algorithm

Inventing K-Means from first principles

Suppose you had to automate this clustering process yourself. What steps would you write?

Most people arrive at something close to this — and if they do, they've just invented K-Means, one of the most widely used clustering algorithms since the 1960s:

  1. Initialize — pick some starting "center" points.
  2. Assign — assign every customer to their nearest center.
  3. Update — move each center to the average position of the customers assigned to it.
  4. Repeat — repeat the assign/update steps until nothing changes.
Why it works

K-Means isn't magic — it's a logical, iterative process. Each round it tightens the fit between points and centers, and it's guaranteed to keep improving (or hold steady) until it settles.

Core Concept

Why the centroid moves to the mean

Why the average position, specifically — why not the median, or some other point? Drag the marker below along a line through a small cluster of points and watch the total squared distance from that point to every member of the cluster.

Explore this further in the WCSS-minimization-by-the-mean visualizer ↗

This total is called the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS):

WCSS  =  Σp in cluster ‖p − centroid‖²

The curve always bottoms out exactly at the mean — that's not a coincidence. The mean is the single point that minimizes WCSS for a set of points, which is exactly why K-Means' update step moves each centroid there.

? Why does the centroid move to the mean during the update step, instead of the median?
ABecause the mean minimizes the total squared distance (WCSS).
BBecause the median is computationally expensive.
CBecause the mean is always one of the data points.
DBecause K-Means requires an odd number of points.
K-Means' objective is to minimize WCSS. The arithmetic mean is the point that minimizes that sum of squared distances, which makes it the optimal centroid update.
Model Selection

Choosing the number of clusters

K-Means requires deciding K in advance. Two or five or ten groups are all technically valid outputs — but which is right? K can come from domain knowledge (a shirt maker fixes K=3 for small/medium/large) or be estimated from the data using two complementary methods.

The Elbow Method

WCSS always decreases as K increases — more clusters means points sit closer to their own center, all the way down to zero once every point is its own cluster. That extreme isn't useful, so instead of chasing the lowest WCSS, look for the point of diminishing returns: the "elbow" where adding clusters stops buying much improvement.

? What happens to WCSS as K increases, with the dataset held fixed?
AAlways increases.
BAlways decreases (or stays the same).
CIncreases then decreases.
DUnpredictable.
More clusters means every point is always at least as close to some center as before, so WCSS is monotonically non-increasing in K.

The Silhouette Score

For a single point, compare its average distance to other points in its own cluster (a) against its average distance to the nearest other cluster (b):

s  =  (b − a) / max(a, b)
  • Close to +1 — well inside its own cluster, far from others: great clustering.
  • Close to 0 — sitting right on the border between two clusters.
  • Negative — probably assigned to the wrong cluster.

Averaged across every point, this gives an overall Silhouette Score from −1 to +1 for a given K. Hover the chart below — these are the actual scores from ClusterMart's data:

Putting both together

The elbow in the WCSS curve appears around K = 4. K = 2 technically has the single highest silhouette score, but K = 4 still scores strongly and captures the natural four-segment structure the elbow method points to. There's rarely one metric that gives a perfect answer — considering both together, K = 4 is the most defensible choice for this dataset.

Applied

Building the segmentation engine

With the mechanics in place, it's time to run K-Means on an actual sample of ClusterMart customer data — three features per customer: annual_spend, purchase_frequency, and discount_usage_pct.

Feature scaling comes first

annual_spend ranges into the thousands, while discount_usage_pct ranges 0–100. Because K-Means relies on Euclidean distance, an unscaled annual_spend would completely dominate the distance calculation. Every feature is standardized (mean 0, standard deviation 1) before clustering:

Limitations

What K-Means assumes — and when it breaks

K-Means is powerful, but it isn't neutral. It carries built-in assumptions worth knowing before reaching for it on the next dataset:

Spherical clusters

Because it measures distance to a single center, K-Means can't represent curved or elongated cluster shapes.

Similar cluster sizes

The "average" gets pulled around by imbalanced group sizes and densities.

Euclidean distance is meaningful

This breaks down for some data types — categorical data, or data that lives on a circle or sphere.

Sensitive to outliers

Since it uses the mean, a handful of extreme points can drag a centroid away from the real cluster center.

Summary

What the segmentation engine delivers

The finished pipeline hands back four clearly interpretable customer segments, a quantitative justification for choosing four groups over any other number, and a working understanding of where this approach holds up and where it doesn't.

Key ideas, in order
  • Clustering finds structure in data with no labels — a fundamentally different problem from supervised learning.
  • Distance (typically Euclidean) is how "similar" gets defined mathematically.
  • A good clustering has low intra-cluster and high inter-cluster distance.
  • K-Means alternates assigning points to the nearest center and moving each center to the mean of its group, until nothing changes.
  • The centroid moves to the mean because the mean is what minimizes WCSS.
  • K is chosen with the Elbow Method and the Silhouette Score together, not either alone.
  • Feature scaling matters whenever features live on different numeric scales.
  • Clusters only become useful once they're translated into business-readable segments.
