GMM
Clustering with probability

Gaussian Mixture Models  post read

Why hard, round clusters are not enough, and how a weighted sum of Gaussians lets us say "55% this segment, 45% that one".

The problem

The customer who lived in two segments

ShopSphere is an online marketplace with millions of shoppers. Last year, a consulting team segmented its customers with K-Means into three groups: Bargain Hunters, Regulars and Big Spenders.

Anjali, a data scientist who joined this month, sits with Vikram, the head of customer relations.

"Look at Shreya. She buys heavily discounted kitchen basics every other week. But last month she also bought an expensive air fryer. The model says she is a Bargain Hunter, 100%. So our recommendation engine only shows her cheap items."
"And on your scatter plots the groups look like tilted, stretched clouds, but the segments look like round balls drawn over them."

K-Means asked one question: which centroid is nearest? It then forced every customer into exactly one segment, with no sense of confidence, and it only knows how to draw round clusters.

What Vikram needs is a model that says Shreya is 55% Bargain Hunter and 45% Big Spender, and that can draw clusters in the shapes the data actually has. That model is the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM).

1 · Recap

Where K-Means struggles

K-Means starts with K centroids, assigns every point to the nearest one, and recomputes centroids until nothing changes. Because its objective sums squared distances to a centroid, it implicitly assumes round, similarly sized clusters, and it gives each point one label. Three problems follow:

1Globular clusters onlyElongated or tilted clusters get cut by straight boundaries.
2Similar sizes and spreadsK-Means tends to produce clusters of comparable width.
3Hard clusteringA border point gets the same unqualified label as a point deep inside a group.

2 · The second problem

Hard vs soft clustering

K-Means cannot give probabilities. To get them, we need a model with a notion of probability. That model is built from Gaussian distributions, so let's refresh them.

3 · Building block

Gaussians, refreshed

The 1D Gaussian

Heights of people across a country pile up around the average and thin out symmetrically on both sides. This bell curve is the Gaussian (normal) distribution:

𝒩(x | μ, σ²) = 1 / (σ√(2π)) · exp( −(x − μ)² / (2σ²) )
  • The height of the curve at x is the likelihood (density) of that value.
  • μ (mean) sets the position. Changing it slides the curve without changing its shape.
  • σ (standard deviation) sets the shape. Larger σ gives a flatter, wider curve; smaller σ gives a tall, narrow one.

1D Gaussian explorer

Move μ and σ and watch what each one controls.

The 2D Gaussian

With two variables x = (x₁, x₂), the bell becomes a hill:

𝒩(x | μ, Σ) = 1 / ((2π)d/2 |Σ|1/2) · exp( −½ (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ) ), d = 2
  • μ is now a vector with two entries; it moves the hill along both axes.
  • Σ is a 2×2 covariance matrix. Its diagonal holds the two variances (width along each axis). Its off-diagonal holds the covariance (how the two variables move together).
  • Seen from above, the hill gives contour lines: rings of equal likelihood. Equal variances and no covariance give circles. Unequal variances stretch them into ellipses. A non-zero covariance tilts the ellipse.

All points on one ring have the same likelihood, and inner rings have higher likelihood than outer rings.

2D Gaussian explorer

Rings mark 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations. Try the presets, then tune each parameter.

Takeaway. A Gaussian can be placed anywhere and shaped as any (possibly tilted) ellipse using μ and Σ. That is exactly the flexibility K-Means lacks.
4 · The model

One Gaussian per cluster

Consider minutes spent per shopping session at ShopSphere. Some visits are quick check-ins (a few minutes) and others are long browsing sessions, so a histogram has two humps.

  • One Gaussian over the whole histogram sits between the humps and describes neither group.
  • Two Gaussians, one per hump, each with its own mean and spread, describe the data well.

That is the Gaussian Mixture Model:

p(x) = Σk=1..K πk 𝒩(x | μk, Σk), πk ≥ 0, Σk πk = 1
  • Each component k is a Gaussian that stands for one cluster.
  • πk is the mixing weight: the share of the data in cluster k. A bigger cluster has a larger πk.
  • A point is assigned according to how likely that cluster's Gaussian makes it, weighted by πk. This gives soft assignments.

Mixture builder

1,000 simulated sessions from two hidden groups. Compare one Gaussian with a two-component mixture, then change the weight to see how the mixture's shape depends on π.

dataquick-visit Gaussian × πbrowsing Gaussian × (1−π)dashed = mixture

As with K-Means, we must choose the number of components K before fitting. A principled way comes later.

But how do we find the right means, spreads and weights without labels? If we knew which Gaussian each point came from, we could compute each group's mean and spread. If we knew the Gaussians, we could compute which one each point most likely came from. We know neither: a chicken-and-egg problem that Expectation-Maximisation (EM) solves by alternating.

5 · Fitting

Expectation-Maximisation: alternating guesses

Start with a rough guess for the Gaussians, then alternate between (1) using the Gaussians to guess each point's membership (soft), and (2) using those soft memberships to improve the Gaussians, until nothing changes much.

Step 0: Initialise

Choose starting values for each component's mean μk, variance Σk and weight πk. A simple choice: take K random data points as the means, give every component the data's overall variance, and set all weights to 1/K. (sklearn initialises from K-Means by default.)

Step 1: E-step, "how responsible is each Gaussian for this point?"

For every point xi and component k, compute the responsibility:

rik = πk 𝒩(xi | μk, Σk) / Σj=1..K πj 𝒩(xi | μj, Σj)the denominator normalises, so each point's responsibilities add up to 1
Why normalise, and why the weights πk? The chance that a random Indian is in Delhi is small, and in New York even smaller, and the two numbers do not add up to 1. If we know the person is in one of the two cities, we divide each by their sum. Delhi gets a much larger share, partly because many more people live there. In a GMM that "how many people live there" is the weight πk: a big cluster is a priori more likely to claim a point.

Step 2: M-step, "refit each Gaussian using its share of every point"

With Nk = Σi rik (the effective number of points in cluster k):

μk = (1/Nk) Σi rik xi
Σk = (1/Nk) Σi rik (xi − μk)(xi − μk)ᵀ
πk = Nk / N

These are weighted averages. A point that is 90% responsible to cluster k pulls on μk nine times harder than a point that is 10% responsible.

Step 3: Repeat until convergence

Each EM round never decreases the log-likelihood of the data, so the procedure converges. Like K-Means, it reaches a local optimum, not necessarily the best one.

Code: EM for a 1D mixture 
def normal_pdf(x, mu, sigma):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2) / (sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

def em_1d(x, k=2, n_iter=100, seed=0, tol=1e-8):
    rs = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    mu = rs.choice(x, k, replace=False)               # step 0: random data points as means
    sigma = np.full(k, x.std())                       #         overall spread for every component
    pi = np.full(k, 1.0 / k)                          #         equal weights
    history = []
    for it in range(n_iter):
        # E-step: r_ik = pi_k N(x_i | mu_k, sigma_k) / sum_j pi_j N(x_i | mu_j, sigma_j)
        dens = np.stack([pi[j] * normal_pdf(x, mu[j], sigma[j]) for j in range(k)], axis=1)
        total = dens.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        resp = dens / total
        loglik = np.log(total).sum()
        history.append((mu.copy(), sigma.copy(), pi.copy(), loglik))
        # M-step: weighted updates
        Nk = resp.sum(axis=0)
        mu = (resp * x[:, None]).sum(axis=0) / Nk
        sigma = np.maximum(np.sqrt((resp * (x[:, None] - mu) ** 2).sum(axis=0) / Nk), 1e-3)
        pi = Nk / len(x)
        if it > 0 and abs(loglik - history[-2][3]) < tol:
            break
    return mu, sigma, pi, resp, history

Local optima. Like K-Means, EM can end in a poor local optimum, for example two components sitting on one hump. That is why sklearn offers several restarts (n_init) and initialises from K-Means by default.

Hard counts vs soft counts

Hard counts are how many customers have each segment as their top choice. Soft counts are the sum of all customers' probabilities for a segment. They differ a little, because a soft count also credits customers who only partly belong.

7 · How many components?

Choosing K with BIC

K-Means needed the elbow and silhouette. A GMM is a probabilistic model, so we can score how well it explains the data with a criterion that also penalises complexity:

BIC = p · ln(n) − 2 ln(L̂)p = number of parameters · n = number of customers · L̂ = maximised likelihood · lower is better

More components fit better (higher likelihood), but each extra component adds many parameters (a mean, a covariance matrix and a weight), and BIC punishes that. AIC is similar with a lighter penalty.

BIC by number of components (ShopSphere data)

Hover or focus a point for its value.

Hover or tab to a point.
  • Look for the lowest point. A curve that keeps falling steeply means the data has more structure than K allows. A curve that turns upward means extra components are not worth their parameters.
  • Here BIC falls steeply from K=1 to K=3, then barely changes. Its lowest value is at K=6, but the neighbours between 3 and 8 are within a few hundred of each other.
  • If neighbouring values are nearly tied, BIC cannot separate them and business sense should decide.

What else the GMM learns

Besides labels, a GMM learns each cluster's weight (its share of the data) and covariance (its shape). K-Means gives us none of this.

fitted mixing weights (true: one third each)
[[1.06, 0.11], [0.11, 0.92]]
fitted covariance of the first component (true recipe: [[1, 0.9], [0.9, 1]] or its relatives)

The GMM ellipses follow the tilt and spread of each cloud, and it recovers the covariances, so we learn how features such as clicks and visits move together within a segment. Points in the overlap of two clouds still carry uncertain probabilities. That is not a flaw: the data really is ambiguous there.

Code: fit the GMM and draw an ellipse 
def draw_ellipse(ax, mean, cov, n_std=2.0, **kw):
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)
    order = vals.argsort()[::-1]
    vals, vecs = vals[order], vecs[:, order]
    angle = np.degrees(np.arctan2(vecs[1, 0], vecs[0, 0]))
    width, height = 2 * n_std * np.sqrt(vals)
    ax.add_patch(Ellipse(mean, width, height, angle=angle, fill=False, lw=2, **kw))

How the two are related

Force all components to have equal weights and the same round (spherical) covariance, and let that variance shrink toward zero: GMM's soft assignments become hard and the EM updates turn into K-Means. K-Means is a special, restricted case of GMM. GMM relaxes those restrictions: each cluster gets its own weight, spread and tilt, and every point gets probabilities.

9 · Honest limits

What GMM cannot do

GMM is more flexible than K-Means, but it has its own limits. Open each to see why it matters.

The last two are worth seeing. GMM can score how typical a customer is under the fitted model, but it still has to put that customer into one of its segments.

Code: likelihood of each customer under the mixture 
loglik = gmm.score_samples(Xs)          # low = unusual under every segment
cut = np.percentile(loglik, 1)
odd = loglik <= cut
Customers in the lowest 1% of likelihood: 25
Their typical segment confidence is still: 0.99 (they get assigned anyway)

The unusual 1% vs everyone else

Feature means for all customers and for the 25 lowest-likelihood customers.

Remember. The unusual customers have extreme values on some features. The mixture gives them a very low likelihood and yet still hands them to a segment, with a probability that may even look confident, because probabilities only compare components with each other. Low likelihood is a hint that a customer is unusual, but a GMM has no built-in concept of "belongs to no group" or "noise".

Key takeaways

  • K-Means gives hard labels and favours round, similar-sized clusters.
  • A GMM models the data as a weighted sum of Gaussians, p(x) = Σ πk 𝒩(x | μk, Σk), one Gaussian per cluster.
  • The mean sets a Gaussian's position, the variances set its size, and the covariance sets its tilt.
  • EM alternates: the E-step computes soft responsibilities (using the weights and normalising); the M-step recomputes weights, means and covariances as weighted averages. The log-likelihood never decreases, but EM can reach a local optimum, so use several restarts.
  • predict_proba gives soft assignments; uncertain customers sit between segments.
  • Choose the number of components with BIC/AIC (lower is better), then check with business sense.
  • K-Means is a special, restricted case of GMM.
  • GMM still assumes Gaussian shapes, needs K, and has no notion of noise.

Where this leads

Segments as a treeLeadership wants broad segments for brand campaigns; category teams want fine-grained ones. Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of nested groups, so the number of clusters is chosen after fitting by cutting the tree at the level each team needs.
Accounts that belong nowhereResellers and bots behave like no real customer, yet a GMM still assigns them to a segment. Finding them needs a different kind of clustering.
